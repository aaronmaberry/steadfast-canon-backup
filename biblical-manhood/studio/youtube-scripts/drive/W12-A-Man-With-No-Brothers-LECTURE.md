## 4. MOVEMENT ONE: BUDDIES ARE NOT BROTHERS [10:00]

**MAX:**
Movement one. Buddies are not brothers, and why most men have none.

Ask a man who could call him at two in the morning, and who he could call. Ask him who is allowed to tell him he is wrong to his face. Ask him who knows what he is actually afraid of. Then watch how many good men, with full phones, go quiet.

A brother is a man who knows what you reach for when the day goes bad, the anger or the screen or the silence, and asks about it, and you tell him the truth. He can ask you anything: money, marriage, the bedroom, what you looked at this week, whether you have prayed. He will come at two in the morning to the hospital because he is your brother. And he tells you the truth, not the nice version, said with love and said once. He is not a crowd. You need one man, maybe two. He is not a counselor, though he might tell you to see one. And he is not necessarily the man you have the most fun with. Some of your best hunting buddies would make terrible brothers.

Why we do not have them. Fine, which is pride in a work shirt. No time, and nobody has asked for the right amount yet. Do not want to be a burden, which is usually a boy who learned early that needing things was dangerous. Men do not do that, except men did it for most of history, and we have been dying quietly since we stopped and calling it independence. Been burned, and one bad meal is no reason to stop eating. And I have my wife. Good. Keep her. She is not your brother. If she is the only one who knows you, you have set a weight on her that will eventually bend her. Ask her.

I lived Dale's clipboard longer than I want to admit. Not in an ER. On a job trailer after a bad inspection, phone full of supers and owners, and nobody I would call to say I am scared this is going to cost me the contract and maybe my name. Site Max had contacts. Home Max had a wife carrying the whole weight of knowing him. That is not strength. That is a second line left blank on purpose.

Movement one, summarized. Write this down: full phone, empty second line. Buddies do things beside you. A brother knows you and comes.

## 5. MOVEMENT TWO: TWENTY MINUTES [14:30]

**MAX:**
Movement two. Twenty minutes, and how to find him.

Most men do not lack the want. They lack a picture small enough to survive their life. Weekly breakfast at six. Small group with homework. Weekend away. A man with a newborn or a business going under looks at those, knows he cannot, and does nothing.

Here is the smaller one. Twenty minutes, with one man, once a week, at the same time. A time that floats is a time that dies. Three questions and a prayer. What happened this week. Not a report. Two or three things that mattered. What's heavy. This is the one that counts: what are you carrying, and what is the fear under the week. You each tell the truth, and the other man does not fix it. He listens, and he does not fix it. What's next. You each name one move before next week out loud so a man heard you, because next week the first question will be, Did you do it. Then two minutes of prayer, out loud, for each other.

Some weeks one of you is a wreck and you spend all twenty on his what's heavy, and that is what it is for. The floor is twenty minutes, once a week, at the same time, and the floor does not move. Twenty minutes survives tax season, a twelve-hour shift, chemotherapy, grief. I have never met a man who could not find twenty minutes a week, and plenty who could not find two hours and so found nothing. Three questions also steer. Without them most men's gatherings drift to sports and trucks inside eleven minutes. The twenty is not the ceiling. It is the seed.

How to find him. Look for a man living the four marks, mostly. Already in your church or your crew. Age or older. Older is often better. Not your boss, your employee, or your brother-in-law. You need the whole truth without a job or a Thanksgiving hanging on it. Then ask in plain words: I am trying to be a better man, and I have realized I do not have a brother. Would you be willing to talk with me for twenty minutes a week for the next three months and see how it goes. Three months make it easy to say yes and easy to let go. Most of the time it works and nobody remembers to stop. Expect awkward. It passes. Expect he has been waiting too. Men are lonely, and they are not going to go first. You are.

Dale has a brother now. Took most of a year to ask a man from church he had known for two decades. Thursday drive to lunch. Three questions and a prayer. First month he thought it was the dumbest thing he had ever agreed to. Then last spring, scheduled surgery, second name on the clipboard, and the pen did not stop.

Seasons, short. Single man: house full of roommates is not the same as a brother. New father: twenty on the phone with the dishwater running. Divorced man: friends often vanished with the marriage; get one man on the phone before the big group. Retired or widower: the built-in company went out with the work or the wife. You need one man.

Movement two, summarized. Write this down: same twenty minutes. What happened, what's heavy, what's next, and a prayer. Say the sentence. Go first.

## 6. MOVEMENT THREE: THE MAN AHEAD AND THE BREAK IN THE CHAIN [19:00]

**MAX:**
Movement three. Ask the man. Chapter fourteen opens by a truck after church.

When I was young enough to think a long marriage was a trick somebody could explain, a man named Harlan got asked how he had done it. He looked a second, deciding something. Then he said, Ride with me Saturday. Feed truck route. Hardly mentioned marriage. At noon he pulled into a gas station and called his wife from the pay phone to tell her where he was and when he would be home. Every working day since their wedding. By the end of the summer more about marriage had landed from that cab than from every sermon on it.

Two men, both waiting. The younger does not ask. Figures the older is busy, or he ought to work it out alone, or his own father should have told him, and since he did not, asking anyone else is shameful. The older does not offer. Figures the young man does not want an old guy's opinion, or he made too many mistakes to have anything worth handing on. So both wait, and a generation grows up with nobody twenty years ahead telling them what is coming. That is not the design. Moses to Joshua. Elijah to Elisha. Paul to Timothy. Keep the chain going.

What to ask for. Do not ask him to mentor you. Mentor is a large, formal, frightening word. An older man hears a commitment he is not sure he can keep and a role he is not sure he is qualified for, and he will say no, or say yes and dodge you. Ask something specific. How did you stay married this long. How did you decide when to leave that job. What do you wish you had done differently with your kids. What did you do the first year after the divorce. Can I come watch you do that. One question is the whole ask. If it goes well, ask another next week. If he says ride with me, go, and bring your hands. Or borrow the twenty minutes: Could I call you for twenty minutes a week for a couple of months. I have some things I am trying to figure out, and I think you already have.

How to pick him. About twenty years ahead. Living the four marks. Not the most impressive man at church. The one still standing at the end of the road you are on. Watch his wife's face when he talks. Be careful of the man who only has wins. What you need is his scars and his noon phone calls. Reachable. Deacon nobody asks anything of. Man in the back pew married longer than you have been alive. Retired man at the hardware store. Nearly all of them are waiting.

Movement three, summarized. Write this down: both waiting is the break. One real question. Ride with me. Scars over wins.

## 7. MOVEMENT FOUR: ONCE YOU HAVE ASKED, AND TO THE MAN AHEAD [23:30]

**MAX:**
Movement four. Once you have asked, and to the man who is ahead.

Come with a question every time. The question is the respect. Do what he says, at least once, and come back and tell him what happened. That report earns the next hour. Older men have wasted advice on young men who nodded and did nothing. Be the young man who does the thing and comes back. Do not make him your father. That is a weight he cannot carry. He will give you a lot, but he cannot give you what your father did not. Say thank you, out loud and specifically. What you told me about handling my dad, I did it, and it changed something.

Now the older man, because half of you are him. You do not feel finished. Nobody finishes. You have walked a stretch a younger man is just starting, and you know where the washouts are. He does not need you to have gotten it right. He needs the truth about where you got it wrong, and what you did on ordinary days that held. Titus two: encourage the young men. Set them an example by doing what is good. Example and word, in the ordinary.

Go first. He thinks you are busy, and he is a little afraid of you. Give him something he can say yes to: I am hanging a gate Saturday and could use a hand, if you want to learn how. Then do not lecture. Ask questions. What's going on with work. How's it going with her. What's heavy. Tell the truth about your failures. Your scars are the curriculum. Let him work beside you. Men learn beside each other more than across from each other. Keep it small and steady. A young man who has never had a reliable man learns more from you showing up eleven weeks in a row than from anything you say on any one of them. And expect to get more than you give. Every older man I know who has done this says the same thing: I thought I was helping him, and mostly he was saving my life. If you have turned in your badge or your hard hat, this is what a man does with his mornings. God sets the lonely in families, in both directions at once.

Seasons for the man ahead. Single man: ask a married man about marriage before you are in one. Father with a baby: find a man whose kids are grown and ask what he wishes he had done when they were small. Divorced man: most tempted to skip out of shame; find a man who has been through it, or one married a long time who will not judge you. Man in the middle: look both ways. Older man whose own father never did this: not disqualified. Equipped by what you lived through.

Harlan has been gone a while. When the young man got married, Harlan sat in the back pew, shook his hand, and said, Well. You paid attention. Because somebody asked, and somebody said ride with me, and neither waited for the other to go first.

Before application, hold the double together.

A brother knows the week. The man ahead knows the road. Pity the man with no second line. Entrust the chain. Twenty minutes: what happened, what's heavy, what's next, prayer. Ask one real question. When he says ride with me, go. Older men: go first with a gate and a question and your scars.

The four marks land on both. Stop waiting for someone to volunteer. Pick up the weight of the sentence and the report back. Lead into the awkward ask and the Saturday cab. Work for God's pay instead of independence as your payout.

Dale's pen stopped stopping. Harlan's noon call taught more than sermons. Jonathan helped David find strength in God. Paul told Timothy to keep the chain going. Jesus asked three men to stay. You need both titles in one week. Brother beside. Man ahead. Do not split them into two courses you will take later.

Seats, blunt. Young man: your independence is loneliness with better branding. Single man: roommates are not brothers; ask. Married man: your wife is bending under a weight she was not built to carry alone. Divorced man: one man on the phone this week. Older man: ride with me is the whole job. High performer: your phone is full and your second line is blank. Wounded man: one bad meal is not a reason to stop eating.

Write both titles on the card. Brothers. Ask the man. Under brothers: the sentence and the day and time. Under ask: the name and the one question. One card. Two titles. No half week.
