## 1. Open [00:00]

[Max standing, lectern or high stool camera right. Book closed. No music.]

**MAX:**
Sit down. You are on time.

Last week I handed you the four marks and asked you which one was faintest. Some of you wrote the card. Some of you asked a brother. Today we take the first mark into an ordinary weekday. Rejects passivity is easy to admire. It is hard to practice when the thing that needs doing has been waiting for years.

This is Steadfast Men. The Way of Godly Men. My name is Max. Session four of twenty four. Same shape. Claim. Text. Movements. Application. Close. One session. Do the work. Come back. Notes by hand.

There is a stair in a house near you that nobody steps on. Third from the bottom. The one that gives a little and speaks when it takes weight. The kids learned to skip it before they learned to spell their own names. The man who owns the house has stepped over it so many times his legs do it without him. He knows exactly what it wants. Two long screws. A bead of glue. Twenty minutes with the drill hanging in the garage.

Ask him and he will tell you he is going to get to it. He means it. That is the trouble. Meaning it is the whole of what he has done.

The stair is the least of it. What worries me is what it is teaching everyone who lives with him. A man can know a thing is broken, know how to fix it, and step around it for years. It is not the only thing being stepped around in that house. There is a conversation with his wife he is going to get to. A brother he has not called since the funeral. A number on a statement he does not open. A son who went quiet about the time his voice changed. A church he is going to start showing up at again. He is going to get to all of it. He has been going to for years. The years are the story.

The first mark of a man is that he rejects passivity. I want to show you what that looks like on an ordinary weekday. The word makes most men picture somebody on a couch with the blinds down. That is not the man I am worried about. The man I am worried about is up before the sun. He is tired and useful. He is working hard at nearly everything except the four or five things that matter most. On those he is waiting. He has waited so long it does not feel like waiting anymore. It feels like the floor.

I need that picture stuck behind your eyes before we state the claim. The man on the couch with the blinds down is easy to diagnose. The man stepping over the stair while coaching on Saturdays is harder. He looks like the opposite of passive. Last week we named marks. This week we watch the first mark fail in respectable clothes. If you are a high performer, this session is aimed at your chest. If you are wounded, hear this too. Survival waiting and comfort waiting can look the same from the outside. Only one of them is still protecting you.

[Slide. The four marks]

A steadfast man stops waiting, picks up the weight, goes first, and works for God's pay, not the room's.

Write it down. Today's weight is on the first mark. Rejects passivity. Stop waiting is what that mark sounds like when the drill is still hanging in the garage.

## 2. The claim [04:30]

**MAX:**
Chapter four is titled Stop Waiting. Here is the claim.

[Slide. Passivity often wears virtue and steps over what is yours.]

Passivity often wears virtue and steps over what is yours.

Write that down. Define waiting the way the book means it. Waiting here is not the holy wait for God. Waiting here is the practiced skip of the third stair. Knowing. Meaning it. Not moving.

Passivity rarely looks like laziness. It wears patience. It wears peace. It wears humility. It wears preparation. It wears faith. It wears busyness. It trains a household to step over what a man will not fix.

What the claim does not say. It does not say every quiet season is sin. It does not say you should create conflict for sport. It does not say preparation is always a costume. It says this. When you know the good you ought to do and you keep stepping over it, you are not neutral. You are training everyone who lives with you.

The kids skipping the stair is not a cute detail. It is discipleship. Households learn manhood from what a man steps over as much as from what he says at the table. That is why the first mark matters on a weekday and not only in a crisis. A crisis will get you off the couch. A stair will tell the truth about whether you reject passivity when nobody is filming.

Hold that. Scripture under it.

## 3. The text [07:00]

**MAX:**
Genesis, chapter three, verse nine. NIV.

[Slide. Genesis 3:9 NIV]

But the LORD God called to the man, Where are you?

Say the question with me. The book will not let you leave it in Genesis. Where are you? Not where is your body. Where is the man who was set to work the ground and keep it. Some of you are in the trees with a full calendar. Some of you are in the trees with a remote. Location is not the point. Presence in the assignment is the point.

Three observations.

First. God knew where Adam was. The question was not about location. After the fruit, when God came walking in the cool of the day, Adam was in the trees. The question meant this. Where is the man I set here to work this ground and keep it, who stood right there while it all went wrong and said nothing?

Second. The first man tried hiding. Passivity after failure still hides. It steps around the stair. It skips the conversation. It leaves the statement unopened. Hiding is not always running away. Sometimes it is staying in the house and becoming expert at the skip.

Third. That question is still walking through gardens. It will walk through your house tonight, past the stair, with your name on it. Where are you?

We will add Proverbs and a psalm and James as we move. The primary text is the question. Write it down. Where are you?

## 4. Movement one. What it wears [10:00]

**MAX:**
Movement one. What passivity wears.

You will never catch passivity dressed as itself. If it showed up as plain laziness you would know it and be ashamed. Shame at least gets a man off the step. So it dresses from a better closet. It borrows the virtues.

It wears patience and says, I am giving it time. Sometimes that is right. More often time is doing the job the man refuses to do, and doing it badly. Time has no judgment. Time loves nobody in the house.

It wears peace and says, I do not want to start something. So nothing starts. The peace he is keeping is the peace of a kitchen where nobody says anything that costs anything.

It wears humility and says, Who am I to bring it up? You are her husband. You are the boy's father. You are the one standing there. That is who.

It wears preparation. Another book on marriage. Another podcast on fathering. Another long talk about the thing instead of the thing. A man can become an expert on the conversation he has never had.

It wears faith and says, I am waiting on the Lord. There is a real kind of waiting on God. There is a counterfeit that signs His name to its excuses. We will run the test in movement three.

It wears busyness, the best disguise of all. A man is on the road before dawn and home after dark. He coaches on Saturdays. He sets out chairs on Sunday. He does not look passive to anyone. Least of all himself. His wife could tell you in one breath what he is avoiding. Being busy is not the opposite of being passive. Often it is the hiding place.

Let me walk the costumes again with Tuesday examples so you cannot keep them abstract. Patience. We will talk about money when things calm down. Peace. I am not bringing up her tone because I do not want a fight. Humility. Who am I to tell my grown son I smell alcohol. Preparation. I listened to three fatherhood podcasts about the talk and never had the talk. Faith. I am praying about the call instead of dialing while I pray. Busyness. I cannot fix the stair because I am building someone else's house all week. Every one of those can contain a half truth. Every one of them can be a costume.

I know that disguise from the inside. Fourteen hour days. Promoted for disappearing into work. Nobody confronted me for the stairs I stepped over at home. The world paid me to be useful everywhere except where the drill was hanging.

For the married man. Your wife can usually name your costume in one breath. Ask her tonight after you name the stair. For the single man. Your costume often has no witness, which makes it easier to wear. Put the costume on paper anyway. For the older man. Busyness may have retired with the badge. The new costume may be waiting by the phone for someone to need you. Nobody is dialing. Walk in and ask.

Movement one, summarized. Passivity borrows the virtues. Write this down. Name the costume before you name the fix.

## 5. Movement two. Why we wait [15:00]

**MAX:**
Movement two. Why we wait.

No man has ever explained his stillness to me with the word passive. He has better words. Every one of them sounds reasonable late at night in a driveway with the engine off.

He says he did not want to make it worse. That is fear of conflict, the most common reason there is. He figures the quiet he has beats the noise he would make. He does not figure that quiet has a price too. That price compounds while nobody is making payments.

He says he was not sure he was right. That is fear of failure wearing a responsible face. Not acting is a way of acting. It is a decision with results. He will answer for them as surely as for a wrong move.

He says nobody asked him. This one runs deep. It usually starts in a boyhood where initiative got you corrected. The safest way to be in a room was to wait for instructions. Some men carry that into the office and the church and their own marriage. They are still waiting to be picked. If you are young and this is you, hear it now. Nobody hands a grown man permission to lead his own life. The ground you are standing on is yours to work.

He says it will sort itself out. It will not. A field never once weeded itself.

Proverbs, chapter twenty four, verses thirty and thirty one. NIV.

[Slide. Proverbs 24:30-31 NIV]

I went past the field of a sluggard, past the vineyard of someone who has no sense; thorns had come up everywhere, the ground was covered with weeds, and the stone wall was in ruins.

Look at what the sluggard did not do. He did not torch the field. He did not push the wall over. He did nothing, long enough, and the world handled the rest. Thorns do not ask permission. Neither does distance in a marriage, or interest on a debt, or the silence of a boy who has decided his father is not coming.

Under every reason a man gives there is one he does not say. He is comfortable. Comfort never argues. It keeps the room at the right temperature and waits for you to agree.

I sat in a driveway once with the engine off and told myself I would call my brother tomorrow. Tomorrow became three months. My reasons sounded responsible every night. Fear of making it worse. Not sure I was right. He had not asked. It would sort itself out. Under all of it I was comfortable in the quiet. The quiet was charging interest. When I finally called, nothing dramatic happened. That is the point. Most first screws are undramatic. The drama was the years.

For the wounded man. Some of your waiting started as survival. Hear me. Naming comfort is not mocking your wound. It is refusing to let an old survival strategy keep stepping over a stair that is yours now.

Movement two, summarized. Not acting is still acting. Write this down. Thorns do not ask permission.

## 6. Movement three. The real kind of waiting [20:00]

**MAX:**
Movement three. The real kind of waiting.

Scripture does tell a man to wait on the Lord. I will not take that from you. Read how it says it.

Psalm twenty seven, verse fourteen. NIV.

[Slide. Psalm 27:14 NIV]

Wait for the LORD; be strong and take heart and wait for the LORD.

Be strong and take heart. Those are not lawn chair words. That is a man standing. He is doing everything that is his while he waits on the one thing that is not. Abraham waited most of twenty five years for the son God promised him. In those years he moved his whole household to a country he had never seen. He built altars. He armed his own servants to go get his nephew back. He stood eye to eye with kings. Waiting on God never once meant sitting down on the parts that were his.

So run this test. When you say you are waiting on God, look at your hands. If they are doing the next thing you already know is right, you are waiting on God. If they are folded until He makes it obvious, you are waiting on comfort and giving it His name.

Luke, chapter nine, verse fifty one. NIV.

[Slide. Luke 9:51 NIV]

As the time approached for him to be taken up to heaven, Jesus resolutely set out for Jerusalem.

He knew what Jerusalem held. He did not drift there. He set out.

The same stair in every house, different names. In a single man's apartment it is called when I am married, as if a wedding flipped a switch. The man you will be at the altar is the man you are building this year. In a house with a newborn it is called when things settle down, and they will not. Ten minutes at the counter after the baby is asleep, phones face down, is the whole season. For the man whose marriage ended, it is the call he will make once the anger cools. Anger cools after a man acts like a man in spite of it, not before. For the man who has handed off the business or turned in his keys, it is sitting by the phone for somebody to need him. Nobody is dialing. The work is wherever he walks in and asks. A young man waits behind the words once I, and the sentence never finishes. A father in the middle of life waits for a quiet son to come around on his own. The step is different in every house. The stepping over is the same.

I want the seasons to land as seats, not illustrations you admire. Young man. Finish the once I sentence with a date and a move. New father. Ten minutes at the counter is the whole season, so stop waiting for settle down. Divorced man. Anger cools after a man acts like a man in spite of it. Older man. The work is wherever you walk in and ask. Midlife father of a quiet son. He is not coming around on his own while you step over the truck conversation. Same skip. Different stair.

Movement three, summarized. Real waiting has working hands. Write this down. Look at your hands.

## 7. Movement four. The first screw [24:30]

**MAX:**
Movement four. The first screw.

Name the thing, exactly. Not my marriage. The conversation about money I have owed her since spring. Not my son. Half an hour alone with him in the truck, radio off, and the question I am scared to ask. A vague thing cannot be done. A named one can.

Ask what you are actually waiting for, and be honest. It is almost never information. It is a feeling. You are waiting to feel ready, or brave, or sure. None of those come first. They come after.

Find the smallest move that is still a real move. Pulling up his number is not one. The call is. Sitting with the drill across your knees is not one. The first screw is.

Do it before noon. Afternoons are where a man's resolve goes to die. By evening the couch has an argument.

Expect it to feel wrong. Your nerves have been tuned to waiting for years. The first right move will register as a mistake for about a day. That feeling is not a verdict. It is a scar stretching.

Here is why before noon matters. I have watched men plan the first screw for after dinner for a decade. After dinner the couch has an argument. The phone has a feed. The feeling of readiness never arrives. Morning is when a man's resolve still has a chance. If your working day starts at night shift, translate the rule. Do it in the first watch of your day, before the fatigue costume puts its boots on.

James, chapter four, verse seventeen. NIV.

[Slide. James 4:17 NIV]

If anyone, then, knows the good they ought to do and does not do it, it is sin for them.

That verse shuts the back door. Doing nothing is not neutral ground. God writes it in the same book as the things we do. Turn it over. The Father who sees what is done in secret also sees a man come down the hall with a drill in his hand before the house is awake. He sees him because that man is finished stepping over it. He sees the number dialed and the question asked. There is a reward for that. His reward. Session seven will open that. For now, fix the step. Then make the call.

I did the first screw version of this the week before we recorded. Not a stair. A call to a brother I had been going to get to since a funeral in March. I dialed before noon on a Tuesday. It felt like interrupting. It was not. It was the first mark with a phone in its hand.

James shuts the back door and opens the front one at the same time. Knowing and not doing is sin. Doing the small real move is seen by the Father who rewards what is done in secret. That is the fourth mark peeking into the first. You do not fix the stair for applause. You fix it because He sees the hall before the house is awake.

Movement four, summarized. Named. Smallest real move. Before noon. Write this down. The first screw is.

I want the older man and the young man to hear the same line. May the God who came looking for Adam in the trees find you on your feet. Not tomorrow. Before noon. Not when it feels right. When it is named.

If your costume this week is faith, run the hands test again after you leave. If your hands are folded until God makes the stair obvious, you are not waiting on the Lord. If your hands are on the drill while you ask Him for courage, you are. Abraham moved households while he waited. Jesus set His face toward Jerusalem while He waited on the hour. Waiting never meant sitting down on the parts that were yours.

## 8. Application. Name the step [27:30]

**MAX:**
Application from chapter four.

[Slide. The thing you have been going to get to the longest.]

A thing you will not say out loud keeps all its power. Write down the one thing you have been going to get to the longest. Next to it, honestly, how long it has been.

Not three things. One. Exact. How long in months or years.

Then, because the gap between knowing and doing is where the years go, make the smallest real move on that one thing before noon on a working day. Do not wait for it to feel right.

For the older man. Your stair may be a younger man who needs a call, not a drill. Same rule. Named. Before noon.

For the single man. When I am married is not a plan. Name the move that belongs to this year.

Walk it like a worksheet. Left column. The thing, in exact words. Middle. How long. Right. Smallest real move, scheduled before noon on a named day. If the move is still pull up the number, you have not named a move. The call is a real move. The apology is a real move. The first screw is a real move. The truck with the radio off is a real move. If that is what you wrote, you have named a move. Take the paper to a brother after you do it, not instead of doing it.
