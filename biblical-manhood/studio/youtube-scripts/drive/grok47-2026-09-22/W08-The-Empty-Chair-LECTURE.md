## 1. Open [00:00]

[Max standing, lectern or high stool camera right. Book closed. No music.]

**MAX:**
Sit down. You are on time.

Last week I asked you to name a small reward and do one good thing nobody would ever know about. Some of you traded up. Some of you are still holding the yard sale box. Stay in the chair. Today we start sorting what you inherited from what is yours.

This is Steadfast Men. The Way of Godly Men. My name is Max. Session eight of twenty four. Same shape. Claim. Text. Movements. Application. Close. One session a week. Notes by hand. Do not binge.

I want to open with a parking lot. A boy comes home with his learner's permit on a Saturday. By afternoon he and his father are circling an empty school lot. Boy behind the wheel. Father in the passenger seat, trying to figure out what to do with his hands. The boy does fine. Two slow laps. On the third lap the boy puts the car in park and says, Dad. You're doing the thing.

The father does not know what the thing is until late that night at the kitchen sink. He has never sat in that seat. When he was learning, the passenger seat held his mother. Both hands braced on the dash. The two of them were scared together. His father had been gone for years. There was nobody else to do it. He had never once watched a man sit in that seat and be calm. He had no picture of it. He had a handle to hold and one word to say, and the word came out wrong.

Every man in this room learned what a man is from a chair. Not from a book. Not from a sermon. From whoever sat at the end of the table, or did not. Before you could read a word you were reading him. How he came through the door at night. What his quiet meant. Whether his hands were safe. Whether he looked at your mother when she talked or over her head. Whether he showed up to the thing you had told him about twice. You finished the most important course on manhood you will ever take before you were old enough to stay home alone.

This stretch of the course sorts what you inherited from what is yours. The four marks still stand. They do not erase the chair. They tell you what to do with it.

What sat in the chair was handed to you. What you do with the chair is yours. A wound can explain a man forever and never once change him. Understanding is not progress. Owning the fuse is.

Write that down under the four marks. What sat in the chair was handed to you. What you do with the chair is yours.

## 3. The text [07:00]

**MAX:**
Psalm twenty seven, verse ten. NIV. The man his father left in the field wrote the line I come back to most.

[Slide. Psalm 27:10 NIV]

Though my father and mother forsake me, the LORD will receive me.

Three observations.

First. David does not pretend the forsaking did not happen. He names it. Honesty before comfort. The chair is real.

Second. He says where he took it. The LORD will receive me. Not the LORD will explain me. Receive. That is a Father verb. We will open that fully in session ten. For now, plant the stake.

Third. Psalm sixty eight, five and six. A father to the fatherless, a defender of widows, is God in his holy dwelling. God sets the lonely in families. That is not a figure of speech. It is who He is. The empty chair is not the last word about fatherhood. It is the first word, and it is a lie, and the rest of this stretch of the book is about letting the truth sit down where the lie has been sitting.

Scripture is honest about men with empty chairs. Joseph's father loved him and could not keep him out of the pit. David's father left him with the sheep when the prophet came to look over his sons. Even Jesus was raised by a man who was not His father and who drops out of the story before He is grown. The chair shaped every one of them and excused none of them. That is not harsh. It is dignity.

Write it down. Named forsaking, received by the LORD.

Four movements. Let's go.

## 4. Movement one. Reading the chair [10:00]

**MAX:**
Movement one. Reading the chair.

Some of you had an empty chair, through divorce or death or prison or plain absence, and the shape at the table did the teaching. Men leave. Your mother's tiredness is yours to fix. Figure it out because there is nobody to ask.

Some of you had a full chair and the man in it was gone anyway. He worked. Or he drank. Or he gave the television an attention he never gave you. You could not name one thing he thought about you. That man gets a pass he did not earn. His hole runs as deep as the one left by a man who packed a bag. It is harder to point at. Dad was there. So what is your problem?

Some of you had a full chair and it was dangerous. You read his mood from the way he set his keys down. You got small. Or you got loud so the storm would land on you instead of your mother.

And some of you had a good man in that chair. I am glad. Even the best father had edges, and you inherited the edges too. Every man in the room inherited something. You will just have to look harder for yours.

I want you to find your seat without performing pain. The young man who is still hoping the chair will fill. The married man whose fuse is shaped like absence. The wounded man who has a clean story and an unchanged life. The older man who became the chair he hated and does not want to say so. The high performer who used competence to father himself and calls it grit. All of you are in this chapter.

Movement one, summarized. Write this down. Before you could read a word you were reading him. Name which chair taught you.

## 5. Movement two. The chair never stays empty [15:00]

**MAX:**
Movement two. The chair never stays empty.

An empty chair does not stay empty. A boy cannot live with a hole that size in the middle of his house, so something always sits down in it, and whatever sits down in it raises him.

Often it is the boy himself. He climbs into the chair and starts fathering himself, making his own rules, talking himself down when he is scared. Some of that becomes real strength. Some of it becomes a man who cannot be fathered by anyone, including God, because he has done the job alone so long that letting somebody else do it feels like drowning.

For a lot of boys a lesson takes the seat. Needing gets you hurt. So he quit needing and grew into a man who does not ask for anything. His wife can never tell what he wants. He says he does not want anything and believes it.

For others the room takes the seat, and the boy becomes an expert on everybody's mood but his own.

Or the audience takes it. He earns scarce approval with grades or jokes or a fastball. He is still auditioning with gray in his beard and cannot say for whom.

And sometimes what fills the chair is a picture of what he will never be. The man in the parking lot swore before he could drive that he would never leave his kids, and he has not, and he is right to be proud. It is also the only picture of fatherhood he owns. A man he refuses to be. That is why his hand was on the handle. He knew every turn he was not going to make. Nobody had shown him how to sit still in the seat.

I know the self-fathering version from the inside. After my own father went quiet for a stretch of years, I built a rule book in my head and called it manhood. Show up early. Never ask. Never need. It looked like strength on a job site. It looked like a locked door at home. My wife did not live with my father's silence. She lived with mine.

Movement two, summarized. Write this down. Something always sits down in the empty chair. Name what sat down in yours.

## 6. Movement three. Inherited, and still yours [20:00]

**MAX:**
Movement three. Inherited, and still yours.

What sat in that chair is what you inherited. Nobody asked a boy in grade school whether he wanted his father to leave. It happened to you. It shaped you. God knows every line of it and is not shrugging.

What you do with it now is yours. The short fuse with your kids can be shaped like your father's absence and still be yours. Your wife does not live with your father's absence. She lives with your fuse. A wound can explain a man forever and never once change him. Plenty of men have worked out precisely why they are the way they are and stayed that way, because understanding felt like progress and it was not.

This is where mark two lands hard. Accepts responsibility. Not accepts blame for what was done to you. Accepts the day that is still in front of you. The chair explains. It does not excuse. God treats you like a man who can do something with what happened to him, because you are one.

That dignity is a gift, not a verdict. The wounded man needs to hear that naming the chair is not the same as camping in it. The older man needs to hear that it is not too late to sit differently in the seats that are his now. The young father needs to hear that fear when you hold your son is not proof you will repeat the chair. It is proof you are looking, which the men who repeat the chair never did.

Movement three, summarized. Write this down. Inherited explains. Ownership changes. Understanding alone does neither.

## 7. Movement four. Where the chair goes, and the next Saturday [24:00]

**MAX:**
Movement four. Where the chair goes, and the next Saturday.

What does a man do with an empty chair?

He admits it is there. I have watched big men go quiet and say I do not really think about it when I ask about their fathers, and that sentence means the opposite. Say it out loud once. My father left. My father was there and I never knew him. My father scared me. My father was good and there is still a place he did not reach.

Then he grieves it, which is the step men skip. Grief is feeling the actual size of what you did not get. The seat nobody sat in. The hand that never landed on your shoulder. The words you needed as a teenager and are still waiting on. It is a loss as real as a death, and it deserves mourning, not managing.

The third thing gets its own session in two weeks. There is a Father who does not leave. Plant that. Do not rush past grief into theology so you can avoid feeling the size of the hole.

The chair follows you into seasons. If your father is alive and still absent, quit waiting on him to become a man before you become one. Build on the Father who is here. If you have just become a father, the chair comes back hardest now. If you are the divorced seat, hear the hardest sentence without dressing it up. A divorced father is somebody's empty chair now, and only a man honest enough to say so can do anything about it. Explaining will not fill it. Showing up will start to. Every exchange. Every game. Every call. Through the months when the answer to every question is nothing. And if your father died before the conversation ever happened, I am sorry. You can still say what you needed to say. Say it to God. He knew him better than you did. Say it to a brother. Just not to him.

Back to the parking lot. The next weekend the father went back before his son put the car in gear and told him the truth. Nobody ever sat in this seat for me. I am learning it while you learn that. Then he took his hand off the handle, set it on his own knee, and left it there. When the boy swung the first turn wide, he said, You've got it. The first fatherly thing he had ever said on purpose.

His son will not remember the driving. He will remember the man who stayed in the seat.

That is what is yours. You did not choose what sat at the end of your table. You get to sit down in the chairs that are yours now and stay in them. You get to let your kids read something different from what you read. You get to stop fathering yourself long enough to be fathered. And you get to find out that the seat beside you was never as empty as it looked.
