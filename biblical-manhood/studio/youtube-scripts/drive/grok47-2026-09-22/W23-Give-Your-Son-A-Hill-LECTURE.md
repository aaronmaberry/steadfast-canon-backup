## 2. The claim [04:00]

**MAX:**
Chapter twenty eight is titled Give Your Son a Hill. Here is the claim.

[Slide. A name without a hill is only a compliment. Pick the right hill. Stay on the step.]

A name without a hill is only a compliment. Pick the right hill. Stay on the step.

Write that down. Give your son a hill. Boys do not become men by being protected from difficulty. They become men by being handed difficulty that is slightly beyond them, with a man watching who does not rescue too soon. Say the right thing when he comes down.

Define hill.

A hill is not a chore chart. A hill is not a travel ball schedule. A hill is real. It matters to somebody besides him. The house has no water until it is done. Boys can smell a made-up job from across the yard. Sweeping a clean garage to learn responsibility is a lie. He knows it. A hill is slightly beyond him. Not what he can already do. Not what a man can do. The next thing. A hill has real risk, bounded. He can fail. He can get hurt a little. He can come down with the tap still dry. A hill has an outcome he owns. I tried is what a boy says. It's done, or it isn't yet and here is what I'm doing about it, is what a man says. A hill is followed by a name. When he comes down, the father speaks.

What the claim does not say. It does not say crush him with a mountain. Slightly beyond. Not impossible. It does not say leave him alone in danger. Lyle's father knew that box. He knew the snakes were down for the year. He was on the step. It does not thin the no-son seat. Boys in your church have never been up a hill because nobody was at the bottom.

Hold that. Scripture under it.

## 4. Movement one. Lyle and the spring box [10:00]

**MAX:**
Movement one. Lyle and the spring box.

He stood at the yard gate. He looked up that hill. He knew he could not. That lid weighed more than he did. He looked back. His father was on the back step with the coffee. He was not going anywhere.

It took most of the morning. He pried the lid off. He dropped it once on his fingers. He found a drowned possum against the screen. He lifted it out with his hands. He dug the silt out in water so cold his arms ached to the shoulder. He seated the pipe wrong. Then he pulled it and seated it right. Three times he looked down the hill. Three times his father was on the step. The coffee had to be cold by then.

The trough pipe was running before Lyle was halfway down. When the boy walked into the yard he was wet to the collar. Mud to the knees. Blood on two knuckles. His father looked at him a long moment. He said, Well. I figured you could.

He never got up off that step. If he had come up, Lyle would have learned he could not do it without him. He stayed put. Lyle learned he could.

I have stolen hills. On a Saturday I once finished my son's half-done fence repair while he was at a friend's house. I wanted it done before dark. I was faster. When he came home the fence was straight. He had learned nothing except that dad finishes what boys start. I thought I was being responsible. I was being the father who climbs it himself. Sitting with empty hands feels like passivity. It is not. The passive father is the one who does the job for him. It is easier. It forms nothing.

Movement one, summarized. Write this down. Stay on the step. I figured you could.

## 5. Movement two. What a hill is and is not [14:30]

**MAX:**
Movement two. What a hill is, and what is not on the list.

Real. Slightly beyond. Bounded risk. Outcome he owns. Followed by a name. That is the definition. Write it.

By age. In the small years, a hill is a real job that matters. Every grocery bag carried in, even if it takes four trips. He is not helping. He is working. Tell him so. Once he is in school, the hill is ownership. A garden bed. A pet's whole life. The trash out without a word from you. In the last years before he is a teenager, a job with real money for a man who is not you. A challenge that scares him a little. At least one hill he climbs alone with you in sight and not coming. In the high school years, a boss who will fire him if he is lazy. Money he loses some of. A project he owns to the finish. A decision with consequences you do not override. Then the launch. The hill is his own life. You are a man he can call.

Not on the list. Trophies, when the sport is his father's ego and every loss is the referee's fault. And screens. A screen is a machine for simulating hills without the weight. Ten thousand levels beaten, and the tap still dry.

The boy without a hill is loved and comfortable and starving. He goes and finds a hill of his own. The ones he finds are nearly always worse. The fight. The dare. The back road at night with the headlights off. Or he does the quieter thing. He never leaves the basement. Nobody ever showed him he could climb anything. The whole world looks like a hill with nobody at the bottom of it.

Movement two, summarized. Write this down. Real. Beyond. Bounded. Owned. Named. Not trophies. Not screens.

## 6. Movement three. The father's part [19:00]

**MAX:**
Movement three. The father's part.

Give it, and do not do it. You are faster. It is easier. Every time you do the job yourself you have stolen a hill.

Watch, and do not rescue. Let him struggle. Let him drop the lid on his fingers. His mother will find this harder than you do. Lead here, gently, and explain why. Rescuing a boy who is not in danger teaches him he needs rescue. Watching a boy who is not in danger teaches him he does not.

Let him fail small so he does not fail big. Forty dollars lost now because he did not plan will save him forty thousand later. A boy never allowed to fail fails for the first time when the stakes are grown and nobody is on the step. Small failure now. Big failure later if you steal every hill. Keep this week on the hill, not on other rooms of the house.

When he comes down, ask what happened. Ask what he learned. Ask what he would do differently. Not a lecture. Questions, then waiting. Then name what he did. I figured you could. You didn't quit. Every hill ends with a name, or it is only a hard day.

Raise the hill. Next time, a little more. A boy who climbs a slightly bigger hill every year of his boyhood leaves your house a man. He has the calluses to prove it.

How fathers get it wrong. Too big too fast, and you crush him. Now the hill is where he fails. He stops climbing. Your own hill instead of his. The sport you wanted. The business you want handed down. Rescue. The project finished at midnight. The call to the coach. The debt paid off. Every rescue says the same thing. You can't. Shame the fall, so every hill becomes a place of fear he will walk around for life. Lyle dropped the lid. He seated the pipe wrong. His father never said a word about either.

2 Timothy 2:3. Join with me in suffering, like a good soldier of Christ Jesus. Not a protected boy. A man trained on hills, who can take the weight and clear the spring when nobody comes.

Movement three, summarized. Write this down. Give. Watch. Let fail small. Ask, then name. Raise the hill. Do not crush. Do not steal. Do not rescue. Do not shame.
