## 1. OPEN [00:00]

[Max standing, lectern or high stool to his right. Book on the lectern, closed. Wide shot of the room, then to camera. No music.]

**MAX:**
Sit down. You are on time.

I want to open with that sentence because I know what most of the men in this room are thinking. You are thinking you should have taken this class ten years ago. You should have. So should I. That is not the question in front of you. The question in front of you is the next twenty four sessions and what you do with them.

This is Steadfast Men. The Way of Godly Men. My name is Max, and I will be teaching every session in this course. We are working through one book, one chapter a week, for twenty four weeks. Each session has the same shape. I will state a claim. I will put Scripture under it. I will teach the claim in three or four movements. I will give you an application, and I will close the same way every week so that by the end of the course you can run the close without me.

Let me tell you how to take this class. Watch one session. Do the work. Come back in a week. Do not watch four of these on a Saturday. This is not content. It is training, and training only works at the speed of practice. If you take notes, take them by hand. You will be asked to write four or five sentences down each week. That is the whole syllabus. Four or five sentences a week that you are expected to be able to say back without looking.

Now, who is in the room. The introduction to the book does something unusual. It refuses to aim at one kind of man. So I am going to name the seasons the book names, and I want you to find your seat. Not the seat you would describe at a dinner party. The actual one.

The young man. Nineteen to twenty five. Nobody is asking much of you yet. That is the hazard, not the advantage.

The single man. No wife to blame or to hide behind. More unstructured hours than anyone in the room and no witness to how you spend them. I will come back to you in the third movement.

The married man. You have a witness. She has seen every one of your Tuesdays.

The wounded man. Something happened, to you or by you, and you have been carrying a limp and calling it a personality. I am going to say this once and plainly. A course on responsibility is not a course on blame. The four marks I am about to give you are not a verdict on what happened to you. They are a description of what is still available to you.

The older man. More Tuesdays behind you than ahead. You are tempted to grade on a curve. Do not. You are the only man in this room with evidence. You have watched men settle. That makes you a resource, not a spectator.

The high performer. Good at being watched. Very good. I was a construction superintendent for fourteen years, and I could walk a hospital job at six in the morning and tell you what was going to fail by ten. I know your seat because it was mine.

One more thing about seats. Some of you did not choose this course. A man handed you the book, or sent you the first session and said, start here. Good. He may see something you do not. But you are not sitting in his seat. This class is not something you do for him, or to get him off your back. Twenty four weeks is too long to do for anyone else. You will quit by week six. The first seat is yours, even if another man pulled it out for you. Settle that now, before I state the claim.

Here is the course in one sentence. You will hear it every session, word for word, because I intend for you to be able to say it in your sleep by session twenty four.

[Slide: the four marks]

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Write it down. Four marks. Rejects passivity. Accepts responsibility. Leads courageously. Expects the greater reward, God's reward. Everything I teach for twenty four weeks hangs on one of those four hooks. Today we are going to touch all four, but the weight is on the first two.

## 2. THE CLAIM [04:30]

**MAX:**
Chapter one is titled A Quiet Tuesday. Here is the claim of the chapter, and it is the claim of this session.

[Slide: Manhood is proven on ordinary Tuesdays, not highlight reels.]

Manhood is proven on ordinary Tuesdays, not highlight reels.

Write that down under the four marks. I am going to define both terms, because I do not want you to hear this loosely.

A highlight reel is any moment where you know you are being graded. The toast at the wedding. The job interview. The night your child is in the emergency room and the whole family is watching to see what dad does. The crisis at work when the thing breaks and every head turns toward you. Men are good at those. I would go further. Most men are excellent at those, because there is a camera, real or imagined, and a camera produces a version of you that is better than you.

An ordinary Tuesday is the opposite. It is any stretch of hours in which nobody is grading you, nobody will remember it, and nobody will ever ask you about it. By the arithmetic of a life, that is most of your hours. Most of your marriage. Most of your fatherhood. Most of your work. Most of your walk with God.

Let me do the arithmetic. A married man with children might have four or five genuine highlight reel moments in a year. There are roughly two hundred and sixty ordinary days. So the highlight reel is about one and a half percent of your life, and we have built an entire idea of manhood on that one and a half percent. The book is asking you to look at the other ninety eight.

The claim is that the man is built in the second category, and the first category only displays what the second category built. The crisis does not make you a different man. It makes you more of the man you were on Tuesday.

I want to say clearly what the claim does not say, because some of you will hear it wrong. It does not say the highlight reel is fake. When you rise in the crisis, that is really you rising. It does not say the crisis does not count. It says the crisis is not most of you, and it says the crisis is not where you were made.

Hold that. Now let me put Scripture under it.

---

## 3. THE TEXT [06:30]

**MAX:**
First Corinthians, chapter thirteen, verse eleven. NIV.

[Slide: 1 Corinthians 13:11 NIV]

When I was a child, I talked like a child, I thought like a child, I reasoned like a child. When I became a man, I put the ways of childhood behind me.

Three observations from the text. Take them down.

First observation. Paul names three childish ways, and all three are internal. Talking. Thinking. Reasoning. He does not say, when I was a child I played with toys. He says the way I processed the world was a child's way. Which means a man can have a mortgage, a title, and a truck, and still be talking, thinking, and reasoning like a child. Age is not the variable in this verse.

Second observation. There is a verb. I put the ways of childhood behind me. The ways did not fall off. They were not outgrown. They were put behind, by a man, on purpose. This matters for the whole course. Nothing in the four marks happens to you. Every one of them is something a man does.

Third observation, and this is the one I want to press on. Paul does not say he stopped feeling like a child. He says he stopped talking, thinking, and reasoning like one. Feelings are not on the list. That is going to matter in about twelve minutes.

Now, the book gives a definition of childish ways that I have not been able to improve on, so I am going to give it to you as the book gives it.

[Slide: Childish ways: a life that never takes a yes that survives the feeling.]

Childish ways are a life that never takes a yes that survives the feeling.

Say it with me. A life that never takes a yes that survives the feeling.

Meaning this. I said yes to something. I meant it when I said it. I felt it. And then the feeling left, and the yes left with it. A child's yes is real. It is just that it lasts exactly as long as the emotion that produced it. That is the ways of childhood. And Paul's claim, and the book's claim, is that a man is someone who has put that behind him. His yes stands after the feeling has gone home.

So the text gives us the diagnosis. The claim gives us the location. Childish ways are the disease, and Tuesday is where you find out whether you have it.

Four movements. Let's go.

---

## 4. MOVEMENT ONE: THE CAMERA AND THE CEREAL BOWL [10:00]

**MAX:**
Movement one. The camera and the cereal bowl.

There is a line in the chapter that I underlined twice. A lot of men can rise for a camera. Fewer rise for a cereal bowl.

The cereal bowl is the book's image for the smallest possible Tuesday. Breakfast. Nobody is watching. Nothing is at stake. The child across from you is not going to remember it, and neither are you. And the claim is that the man who can rise for that, who is present for that, is a man, and the man who can only rise when the stakes go up is a performer.

Let me give you an illustration from my own Tuesday so that you understand I am not teaching from the balcony.

About four years ago I came home from the best day I had in that job. We had closed the final inspection on a hospital two weeks early. Nobody does that. I pulled into my driveway at six fifteen and I did not get out of the truck. I read the texts coming in from the project team. I answered a few. I checked the news. I checked a score. And my daughter, who was seven, came out onto the front step, sat down, and waited.

Twenty two minutes. I know the number because my wife watched from the kitchen window and told me later. She did not come out. She wanted to see how long it would take. And in twenty two minutes my daughter did not once knock on the window of the truck. Because she had learned not to.

Here is the teaching point, and I want you to get it exactly. The twenty two minutes was not the problem. The twenty two minutes was the evidence. You do not learn not to knock on a window from one Tuesday. You learn it from a hundred. The highlight reel version of that day is a man who closed a hospital early. The Tuesday version is a man who could not get out of a truck for a seven year old. Both are true. Only one of them is what my daughter knows about me.

The book puts the same idea in a second image, and this is the one I could not get past. One more throw while dad watches the clock.

The boy in the yard is not asking for the camera moment. Not the trip, not the speech, not the big Saturday. He is asking for one more throw. It costs ninety seconds. And the man cannot give it, because the man is not in the yard. His body is in the yard. He is in the meeting he left two hours ago. He is in tomorrow. He is checking the clock as if the clock is going to do something for him.

Two men. Same yard. One rose for the camera earlier that day and cannot rise for one more throw. That is the whole chapter in a single frame.

Movement one, summarized. The highlight reel is real, but it is not most of you. Most of you is the cereal bowl. And the cereal bowl is where the four marks are either present or absent. Write down the summary line: the crisis reveals what Tuesday built.

---

## 5. MOVEMENT TWO: THE ANATOMY OF A DISAPPEARANCE [15:30]

**MAX:**
Movement two. The anatomy of a disappearance.

The chapter uses a word for what a man does on a Tuesday when he fails the Tuesday, and the word is disappearance. I want to teach that word carefully, because it corrects a definition most of us are carrying.

A man disappears on Tuesday. Not dramatically. He does not leave. He is right there. His body is on the couch. His name is on the mortgage. His truck is in the driveway. And he is gone.

Let me give you the taxonomy. Where do men go.

[Slide: Where men go on Tuesday]

Into the phone. That is the obvious one and I will not spend time on it.

Into work. And note, this is the high performer's exit. Nobody ever confronted me for staying late. My disappearance came with a bonus attached. I was promoted for it. The world will pay you to disappear from your own house, and it will call it commitment.

Into the garage. Into a hobby that grew legs. Into the news. Into fatigue, real or performed. Into being the funny one, so that nothing at the table ever has to get serious. Into a screen with a game on it. Into a screen with something worse on it.

And one more that is sneaky. Into being agreeable. Sure. Whatever you want. You decide. It looks like humility. It is a man who has left the building and taped a pleasant note to the door. His wife is not married to a tyrant. She is married to a vacancy.

Now the teaching point. Here is the definition I want to correct.

[Slide: Passivity is not doing nothing.]

The first mark says a real man rejects passivity, and most men hear that as, a real man is busy. That is wrong. I was busy. Fourteen hour days. Passivity is not doing nothing. Passivity is being absent from the thing that was actually yours while you were fully occupied with something that was not. Which means the Tuesday question is not, what did you do. The Tuesday question is, where were you.

Here is what makes disappearance so dangerous. The household adjusts. That is what my daughter did on the step. She adjusted. The tragedy of a disappearing man is not that he is gone. It is that everyone around him learns to route around the hole. They stop asking. They stop knocking on the window. From the outside it looks like peace. It is not peace. It is a family that has quietly agreed to stop expecting him.

The third mark touches down here as well. Leads courageously. We picture courage as the highlight reel, running toward the burning thing. On a Tuesday, courage is mostly having an opinion about your own household and staying in the room when it costs you something. Saying no to the second screen. Saying out loud, I was not here last night, and I know it. That is not a small courage. It is an unphotographed one.

Let me add a second text, because the Lord speaks directly to the size of the Tuesday. Luke sixteen, verse ten. NIV.

[Slide: Luke 16:10 NIV]

Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with very little will also be dishonest with much.

Very little is a cereal bowl. Very little is one more throw. God's arithmetic runs from small to large, never the other direction. You are not trusted with the crisis because you crushed the last crisis. You are trusted with the crisis because you sat at the table on a Tuesday without your phone.

Movement two, summarized. Disappearance is the Tuesday form of passivity. It is not idleness. It is absence. And the household will adjust to it, which is exactly why you will not be confronted about it. Write this down: the Tuesday question is where were you.

---

## 6. MOVEMENT THREE: A YES THAT SURVIVES THE FEELING [21:00]

**MAX:**
Movement three. A yes that survives the feeling.

I told you the third observation from the text would matter later. Here it is. Paul says he put away childish talking, thinking, and reasoning. He never says he put away childish feeling. The feeling may never grow up. The man does.

That is the mechanics of a grown yes. It is not a yes that is felt more strongly. It is a yes that has been detached from the feeling and attached to the man.

Illustration. When my father died, I told my mother I would call her every Sunday. She lives alone, about six hours from us. I meant it. I felt it as strongly as I have felt anything. I called four Sundays in a row. The fifth Sunday I was tired and I told myself she would understand, and she did, which is the worst part. I called maybe fifteen Sundays out of the next fifty two. On every one of those thirty seven Sundays I was a man who had said yes and a boy who did not have to mean it. The yes was real. It just did not survive the feeling.

Now the counter illustration. My wife's father has carried her mother a cup of coffee in bed every morning for forty one years. I asked him once whether he still enjoyed doing it. He looked at me as though I had asked a strange question. He said, that is not really what it is for. Fourteen thousand cups. Nobody filmed one. That is a yes that survived the feeling, and that is what a man looks like on a Tuesday. Boring from the outside. Unmoved on the inside.

Here is why this movement is in session one and not session twelve. Everything I am going to ask you to do in this course is a yes that has to survive a feeling. You feel it tonight. You will not feel it in week nine. Week nine is a Tuesday. Week nine is the fifth Sunday. The course only works if your yes is still standing when the feeling has gone home. So I am telling you now, in advance, that the feeling will leave, so that when it leaves you do not mistake that for a reason.

Now, the single man. I said I would come back to you. You may have been sitting there thinking that the driveway and the cereal bowl belong to the fathers in the room. Hear me. Your Tuesday has no witness. That is harder, not easier. Nobody is on the step waiting for you. Your disappearance is total. You can go a whole week without anyone knowing you were gone. There is no daughter learning not to knock. There is just a man, alone, becoming someone in the dark. The chapter would say your Tuesdays are being watched most closely of all, because there is only One who sees them, and He does. And your yes has to survive the feeling without anyone to notice whether it did.

Movement three, summarized. A man is someone whose yes outlives the feeling that produced it. Write down: the feeling may never grow up; the man does.

## 7. MOVEMENT FOUR: MADE OR SETTLED [25:00]

**MAX:**
Movement four, and it is short. Made or settled.

The chapter closes with a sentence that is also your carry for the week, so I am going to teach it before I give it to you.

[Slide: Tuesday is where a man is made, or he settles.]

Tuesday is where a man is made, or he settles.

Define settling. Settling is what happens when a man stops asking the Tuesday question. It is not a decision. Nobody decides to settle. You stop answering for the day, and eventually the day stops asking. The couch gets comfortable. The phone gets warm. Everyone learns to route around you.

And here is what should bother you. From the outside, the man who settled and the man who was made look almost identical on a Saturday. Same church. Same job. Same truck. Same jokes. The difference is only visible on Tuesday. That is the entire thesis of the chapter in one line.

Notice the word the book chose. Made. Not improved. Made is a construction word. A building is not made on the ribbon cutting. It is made on the ordinary mornings when men poured what was scheduled and went home. The ribbon only reveals whether they did. A man is the same. The Tuesdays are the pour. The highlight reel is the ribbon.

Now the fourth mark, because I promised we would touch all four. A real man expects the greater reward, God's reward. This course is not designed to make you feel bad for twenty four weeks. There is a payout for the man who gets out of the truck. It is not applause. Nobody films it. But a seven year old who stops waiting on the step because she does not have to anymore, because dad comes straight in now, is a reward. Forty one years of coffee is a reward. A mother who picks up on the first ring because she knows it is Sunday is a reward. It is paid on a schedule you do not control, in a currency you do not choose. It is a better currency. It is the only one that holds.

Movement four, summarized. Made and settled look the same on Saturday. Only Tuesday tells them apart.

## 8. APPLICATION: LAST TUESDAY [27:00]

**MAX:**
Application. Every session gives you a job, and here is the job from chapter one.

[Slide: Last Tuesday is the day you answer for.]

Last Tuesday is the day you answer for.

Not your best day. Not your worst. Not the day you plan to have once things calm down. Last Tuesday. The actual one.

Take a sheet of paper. Draw a line down the middle. Left column is the hour. Right column is where you actually were. Not what you were doing. Where you were. A man can be washing dishes and be in another state.

Walk it with me. What time did you get up. What did you look at first, before your feet hit the floor. Who did you speak to in your house before you spoke to a screen. Where did you eat lunch, and with whom. What time did you get home. What did you do in the first ten minutes at home. Where were your hands at eight thirty. Where were your eyes at ten. And the hard one, at the bottom of the page. What did the people in your house get from you last Tuesday that they could not have gotten from a roommate.

I did this exercise the Tuesday before we recorded, and I will report it. I was present until about eight forty five. Then I remembered a hard conversation I had coming the next morning and I disappeared into rehearsing it. My wife said something to me and I said yeah, and I do not know to this day what she said. I did nothing about it that night. The next morning I told her, I was not here after eight forty five last night, and I know it. She said, I know. She already knew. They always already know. But saying it out loud is the difference between a boy and a man. A boy hopes nobody noticed. A man names it.

For the older man. Your kids are grown. Nobody is asking for one more throw. Here is your Tuesday. There is a man in your life, twenty seven, thirty one, who would take one call a week from you and does not know how to ask for it. He is in the truck. You know what the truck is because you sat in it. That is your Tuesday now. Do not tell me you are out of Tuesdays.

That is what the second mark means. Accepts responsibility. Not accepts blame. Accepts the day. This one is mine. All of it. Eight forty five included.

## 9. CLOSE [29:30]

[Max closes the book. To camera.]

**MAX:**
Every session closes the same four ways. Learn the shape. By session twenty four you will run it yourself. Name it. Do this week. Tell a brother. Carry this.

Name it. Name your disappearance. Not your sin in general. Not your struggles. The specific place you go on a Tuesday night when you are technically in the room. Truck. Work. Garage. Phone. News. Agreeable. One sentence, out loud, tonight: I disappear into blank. If you cannot fill the blank, ask the person who lives with you. They can. If you are married, say the sentence to her. That is the smallest courageous act you will do this week. Do it anyway.

Do this week. One meal with your phone in another room. Not face down on the table. Not in your pocket on silent. Another room. One meal. If you live alone, you sit down for it at a table, fork in one hand, nothing in the other. It is deliberately small, because we want a yes that survives the feeling, and the feeling will be gone by Thursday.

Tell a brother. Text one man this week about your Tuesday. Not a verse. Not a link. One sentence about your actual Tuesday. If you need a template: Started a twenty four week course on manhood. Session one is about ordinary Tuesdays. Mine was rough at eight forty five. What was yours. He will answer. Men answer that text.

Carry this. Tuesday is where a man is made, or he settles.

Say it with me. Tuesday is where a man is made, or he settles.

The four marks, once more, and then you are dismissed.

[Slide: the four marks]

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Session two, chapter two, next week.

One ask, and it is the only one you will hear in this course. This training does more at session twenty four than at session one, and the only way to be here at session twenty four is to make sure session two finds you. Subscribe and turn on the bell. It keeps the curriculum in front of you and it keeps this work going.

I'm Max.

Get out of the truck.
