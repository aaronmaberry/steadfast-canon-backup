WES: My daughter. She was nine. We were at supper, and she was telling a story, and she said, "Daddy, you're not listening." And I wasn't. I was thinking about the text. [CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: And the other half. "A companion of fools suffers harm." That's the half nobody puts on a coffee mug.
WES: Same thing in the other direction. You're going to catch somebody. Every man does. You're going to sit across from somebody every week, at lunch or a bar or a group text, and you're going to start sounding like them. The verse isn't asking whether you'll become like somebody. It's asking who.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And I'll go first on this one. At twenty-five I worked with a man I ate lunch with every single day. Funny. Quick. And he talked about his wife like she was a bad customer. Every lunch, a new story about something dumb she did. And I laughed. For two years I laughed.
JONATHON: And you caught it. I can see where this is going.
WES: I caught it. I didn't know until my wife said it to me one night, in the kitchen, not even mad. "You talk about me like he talks about his." That was it. I changed lunch tables the next Monday. JONATHON: Did you ever tell the guy why you moved? Or did you just disappear on him?
WES: I just moved. I wasn't brave enough to explain it at twenty-five. And here's the harm part of the verse. I ran into that man at a gas station a few years ago. Gray now. [CAM: JON MS]

## Reading narrative

# Ask the Man
### Walk Steadfast, Episode 39

At a men's breakfast in 2011, an old lineman looked across the table at Wes and asked, "What are you waiting on?" Then he ate his eggs and left. He didn't invite Wes to anything. Wes went to the church office, copied the man's number off the sign-in sheet onto a gas receipt, and carried it for nineteen days. He typed the text at red lights and deleted it. One night at supper his nine-year-old said, "Daddy, you're not listening." He went out on the porch and sent it. "You asked me what I'm waiting on, and I don't know, and I'd like you to ask me again."

The phone rang inside a minute. "Thursday. Six o'clock. The diner by the highway. I'll ask you again."

## My breakfast, not yours

Years later Wes asked Mr. Abernathy why he hadn't just called. "A man has to want it enough to ask. Otherwise it's my breakfast, not yours." After he died, his daughter found a notebook in his Bible. The first line with Wes's name on it was dated the morning of that breakfast, nineteen days before the text.

Jonathon calls the rule a test that filters out men raised never to ask. Wes thinks he may be right, and that the old man would have seen it.

> "Walk with the wise and become wise, for a companion of fools suffers harm."
> Proverbs 13:20 (NIV)

You catch a man the way you catch an accent. Wes still taps the table twice. At twenty-five he caught something worse from a lunch partner who mocked his own wife, until his wife named it and Wes changed tables.

## The quiet ones

Jonathon's man ahead turns out to be his father-in-law. He's seventy-four, married fifty-one years, in the same house, golf years and all. In all the years since the wedding, Jonathon has never asked him a real question. On Sunday, after the dishes, he will. "How did you stay?" Wes will take black coffee with one sugar to an eighty-one-year-old mechanic and ask how a man gets old without getting small. "Nobody asks the man under the van," Jonathon says.
