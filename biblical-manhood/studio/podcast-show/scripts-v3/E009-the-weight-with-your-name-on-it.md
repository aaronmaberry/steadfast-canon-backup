## Reading narrative

# The Weight With Your Name on It
### Walk Steadfast, Episode 9 · February 2, 2027

Most men have two piles mixed up. They're carrying things that aren't theirs, and they've set down things that are. In this episode Wes Hart and Jonathon take up the second of the four marks: pick up the weight.

## The check-engine light

Jonathon and his wife have been saying "we should do a will" for three years, since their son was born. At Christmas, over pie, his mother-in-law asked who would get the kids. The table went quiet. "We're working on it," Jonathon said. They weren't.

The forms aren't the hard part, he admits. The hard part is one line: who raises the children if both parents are gone. Answering it means sitting in his own kitchen, imagining he's dead, and choosing. So the will sat on the family's dashboard like a check-engine light, glowing so long he stopped seeing it. And he is the son of a man who left without a single arrangement, whose mother got rides to work for most of a year because the only car was in his father's name.

Wes has had a will since 2002. His wife did all of it, and he signed where she put the yellow stickers. He read it for the first time at fifty, nine pages, sitting on the edge of the bed. "If I'd died at thirty-eight, my kids would have been raised by a plan I never read."

## The dent

Wes had the opposite problem. In December his sixteen-year-old backed the truck into a post at the feed store. Wes said "Don't worry about it," paid the body shop himself, and called it grace. Three weeks later, after a rolling stop, his son shrugged: "It's just a truck." The dent had cost the boy nothing, so the truck weighed nothing.

## One verse, two kinds of weight

> "for each one should carry their own load."
> Galatians 6:5 (NIV)

Three verses earlier Paul tells the same churches to carry each other's burdens. The two words are different. The first is the crushing kind of heavy, too much for one man. The second is closer to a soldier's pack, sized for the man who carries it. Wes puts it in a picture from thirty years of helping people move: "Every man carries his own duffel. Nobody carries a couch alone."

## Is this just bootstraps?

Jonathon, raised by a mother who did everything alone, pushes back. Wes agrees there's a version of this that's pride with a Bible verse stapled to it. The verse doesn't mean nobody helps you. It means nobody else is responsible for your part. It's on the side of every woman who had to carry a man's load for him.

Then Jonathon names a weight he's carried since he was nine, when his mother told him he was the man of the house: her happiness on every holiday. Honoring your mother is yours. Whether she's happy on Christmas afternoon belongs to her and God. The test: "When you carry something that's yours, it gets lighter for somebody. When you carry something that isn't, nobody gets lighter."

For the man who's barely standing, asking for help is the heaviest thing he'll lift. If you're having thoughts of ending your life, call or text 988.

## Two chairs

Jonathon wasn't done with the truck. Isn't paying for what your son broke exactly what grace is? Wes answers that Christ paid a debt no man could pay, and a sixteen-year-old can pay a body shop. Jonathon holds his ground: a boy needs to see his dad eat a cost sometimes. His suggestion: "This part's on me, because I love you. This part's on you, because it's yours." Wes isn't conceding. Ask him in April.

## What they actually did

Jonathon called a lawyer from his church and booked an appointment for Thursday. The guardian decision is set for Wednesday night. He also stopped making his daughter's bed. Wes sat his son down with the actual bill and arranged for the boy to work it off over twelve Saturdays at his uncle's cattle place. And he's taken over his mother's physical-therapy drives. His knee appointment is the next morning.
