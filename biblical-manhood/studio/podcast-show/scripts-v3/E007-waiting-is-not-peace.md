## Reading narrative

# Waiting Is Not Peace
### Walk Steadfast, Episode 7

Most men aren't robbing banks. They're sitting on a long list of good things they know about and haven't done. This week Wes Hart and Jonathon take the first of the four marks on its own: stop waiting.

## The easy one and the other one

Wes has two brothers. He called the easy one, the funny younger brother in Fort Worth, and they talked for forty minutes. The older one is harder. At Thanksgiving, during an argument about their mother's care, his brother said in front of everyone, "You've always let me handle Mom." Wes said nothing and left early "to beat the traffic." There's no traffic in the Hill Country. Two months later he still hadn't called. "It hasn't felt bad," he admits. "It's felt quiet." Jonathon names it. "That's not peace. That's a cease-fire."

## Why men wait

Fear of making it worse. Comfort, because doing the thing costs today and waiting costs later. And waiting to feel ready, which never comes first. Underneath all of it is one hard observation. "When you wait, somebody else is usually paying for it." Jonathon has a sales version: the deal doesn't die in the meeting, it dies in the follow-up. He's great at follow-up at work and has none at home, where an October argument with his wife was never finished.

## The longest wait

Wes's father never said "I'm proud of you" until he was in a hospital bed. Wes was 43. "He waited forty-three years to say four words." Now Wes says it to his own kids often enough that his daughter says he sounds like a greeting card. "I'd rather be a greeting card than a hospital bed." Jonathon has never heard it from his own father. He says it to his daughter every morning at the bus stop.

## Mr. Abernathy's question

Every other Thursday for six years, Wes's mentor asked what he'd done since last time. When the answer was "nothing," he'd set down his fork, tap the table twice, and ask, *What are you waiting on?* He never lectured. By the third year, Wes was doing the thing before Thursday came.

## What James calls it

> "If anyone, then, knows the good they ought to do and doesn't do it, it is sin for them."
> James 4:17 (NIV)

We have a whole vocabulary that makes waiting sound fine: processing, giving it space, letting the dust settle. James names it plainly, so it can be confessed, forgiven and done. "You don't fix waiting by feeling bad about waiting." And as Jonathon puts it, "You can't confess a personality."

## Waiting on God or on yourself?

Scripture often calls us to wait on the Lord. Waiting on God means trusting him to do what only he can do. Waiting on yourself means God has already shown you the good, and you're waiting to feel like doing it. Patience picks a time. Waiting doesn't. When Jonathon asks whether his hesitation about his absent father is wisdom, Wes doesn't force it. "Put a date on deciding."

## This week's challenge

Pick one thing you've been waiting on, put a day and a time on it, tell one man, and do it before Friday. Married men can ask, "Is there something you've been waiting on me for?" Single men can ask someone close what they've noticed you putting off.

## Carry this

> Waiting feels like peace. Most of the time it's a cease-fire, and somebody else is paying for it.
> "We'll see" isn't an answer. Give one.
