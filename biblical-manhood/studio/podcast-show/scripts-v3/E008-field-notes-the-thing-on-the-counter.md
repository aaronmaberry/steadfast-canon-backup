On Tuesday, Wes Hart put a phone call on his calendar, Wednesday at 7:00, and told everyone watching. Jonathon played his older brother so Wes could practice. On Friday Wes reports back.

## Check the box

Wes called his older brother at 6:59 from his truck, with two notes on a feed-store receipt: "Mostly right" and "What do you need?" His brother picked up with "Well." It runs in the family. Wes led with his part. "What you said at Mom's was mostly right." The line went quiet long enough that he checked for a dropped call. Then he asked what his brother needed, and stopped talking. "I need you at Mom's doctor appointment on the ninth." Wes is taking the day off. "It's not fixed," he says. "It's started."

When Wes asked his wife what she'd been waiting on him for, she said, "Your knees." He'd been limping since October. He made the appointment. "I'm not asking because of your knees," she told him. "I'm asking because I want you around when we're old."

Jonathon finally held the review he'd postponed three times. The rep thanked him. "I knew. I've been waiting for somebody to say it." Jonathon and Dani picked a night to finish an October argument. He learned where the kids' pajamas are, with zero texts to Dani on her shift. In the bath, he told his four-year-old, "I'm proud of you." Theo asked for goldfish crackers. The next night, Theo asked whether it was still true. And Jonathon named his miss: he hasn't set a date to decide about his father's friend request.

## From the table: how do I stop waiting when I'm genuinely tired?

Tired is real, and it isn't a character flaw. But there are two kinds of tired, and they feel the same at seven in the evening. Wes's test: if someone offered free brisket at the end of the street, would you get up? Jonathon objects that brisket is fun, so Wes offers the grown-up version: would you get up for a sick child? If so, there's something in the tank, maybe ten minutes. If not, rest, and get help. For the truly worn out:

> "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest."
> Matthew 11:28 (NIV)

Come to God tired. Then give him ten minutes.

## Tool on the table: the ten-minute start

Set a timer for ten minutes (Wes uses his wife's ceramic chicken). Do the one thing you've been waiting on and nothing else. When it dings, you're allowed to stop without guilt. Most of the time you won't. If it's a phone call, the ten minutes is preparation, and when the timer dings, you dial. As Mr. Abernathy put it, the gate is the heaviest part of the field.

## Carry this

> Come to God tired. Then give him ten minutes.
> You don't have to fix it. You have to start it.
