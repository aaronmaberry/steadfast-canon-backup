[CHAPTER: Shoes]
[CAM: JON MS]

[CAM: WES MS]
WES: (long beat) Shoes.
JONATHON: Shoes? I was not expecting shoes. Okay. Go.
WES: Every Saturday night, my dad polished his church shoes. At the kitchen table. On newspaper. The farm section, always the farm section. A tin of black polish, a rag, and a brush with a wood handle. And from the time I was about six, I'd sit across from him and hand him the brush when he needed it. That's all. I handed him the brush.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: Forty-five minutes, every Saturday night, for about ten years. And he never said anything. Not "how was school." Not "you're a good helper." Nothing. He'd hold his hand out, and I'd put the brush in it. (beat) That was the most time I spent with my father in any week of my childhood. Me and him and a shoe.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Did you like it? Be honest. Because I can hear in your voice that you did.
WES: I loved it. That's the hard part. I loved it, and there wasn't anything in it. I'd give a lot for one more Saturday night of it, and I'd also give a lot to have heard him say one sentence across that table. Both. Same table.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: What do you remember about it? Not the polish. Around it. What else was in the room?
WES: The radio was on low, the station out of town that did the cattle report and then old gospel. Mom was doing dishes behind us. The kitchen smelled like polish and coffee. And his hands. He had big hands, cracked at the knuckles from winter. I watched his hands more than his face. I think because his face didn't tell you anything.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: And when he finished, he'd set both shoes side by side on the paper, toes lined up exactly, and look at them for a second. That was the only time all week I saw him look pleased about anything he'd done. A pair of shoes.
JONATHON: That's a chair.
WES: That's my chair. He was in it. And I was right there, holding the brush, and he never once looked up. I still polish my own shoes on Saturday nights. On newspaper. I didn't know why until about a week ago, getting ready for this.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Have your kids ever seen you do it?
WES: Probably. They've walked through the kitchen. I don't think I ever said a word to them about it. Which, now that you say it out loud, is the exact same chair.
JONATHON: I'm just asking questions over here. I'm not saying anything. I'm a humble question guy.
WES: You're saying plenty.
[TEXT: "I loved it, and there wasn't anything in it."]

[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Can I ask you something that might be rude? When you were getting ready for this one, did you feel like you were allowed to be in it? Because your dad stayed. Forty-some years, same ground, same table. I'd have traded you in a second.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: That's not rude. That's the exact question. And no, for most of my life I didn't think I was allowed. A man at my table says his dad left, and I think, well, mine didn't, so I don't get a chair story. Mine paid the bills and came to supper. Who am I to complain about shoes?
JONATHON: Right. That's how I'd see it, honestly. From the outside, you won the dad lottery.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: From the outside I did. And I'm grateful. I want to say that plainly. A man who stayed and worked and never hit anybody is a gift, and plenty of men listening would take him. But a man can be in the chair and the chair can still be empty. It's a different kind of empty. Yours has a hole in it. Mine had a man in it who never turned around.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: I think that's actually harder to explain to somebody. With mine, I say "he left" and everybody nods. With yours, you say "he polished shoes and didn't talk," and people say, "So what's the problem?"

[CAM: WES MS]

WES: That's why it counts. I was polishing shoes in front of three kids and not saying a word. I don't think that's a coincidence.
[TEXT: A man can be in the chair and the chair can still be empty.]

[CHAPTER: What belonged in the chair]
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: Here's the verse. And it's a verse to fathers, so it's about what's supposed to be in that chair. (puts on the reading glasses) Paul's writing to the church in Ephesus. He's just told kids to honor their parents. And then he turns around to the dads.
[CARD: Ephesians 6:4]
WES: "Fathers, do not exasperate your children; instead, bring them up in the training and instruction of the Lord."
[CAM: 2S]
WES: Two things a father's supposed to do there. Don't exasperate. Don't wear your kids out, don't drive them to despair. And train them. Teach them. Bring them up. That's the job description for the chair.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Can I ask about the order? Because he tells the kids to honor first, and then he gets to the dads. Is he letting the dads off easy?
WES: Other way around. In that world, a father owned the house. Legally, he could do almost anything he wanted with his kids. Nobody told a Roman father what not to do. And Paul turns to those men and says, there's a limit on you. Don't exasperate them. That was a strange thing to say to a father back then. He's putting a fence around the man with all the power.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: So it's a verse that protects the kid. From the guy in the chair.
WES: It is. And there's a sister verse in Colossians that says it a different way. Don't embitter your kids, or they'll lose heart. That's what exasperate means. A kid who gives up. Who stops trying, because nothing he does lands.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And look at the end. "Of the Lord." It's not the father's training. It's the Lord's. The dad isn't the top of the ladder. He's under somebody too. He's passing along what he got from his own Father. If he's not getting anything from that Father, he's got nothing to pass.
JONATHON: Which tells me something about my dad. It doesn't let him off the hook. I'm not saying that. But you can't hand down a brush you never held.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Can I say what I think was missing from my chair? The thing I can actually point to?
WES: Please.
JONATHON: Nobody taught me how to lose. That's it. That's the one. Nobody taught me what to do when it hurts. My mom was doing everything else. She was working two jobs and keeping the lights on. Because there was nothing else in the chair to argue with it.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: The pitching coach filled the chair.
JONATHON: A guy I saw twice a week for two years filled the chair. And I hid a shoulder injury for two months in college with that man's sentence. I snapped at Harper's dad with it. I yelled at Ruby over a juice box with it. Because my dad's sentence was "Be good for your mom." And that's not a sentence you can build a man on. It's a sentence you can build a kid who's very, very good.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: And I was very good. I was the best nine-year-old you ever met. I never got in trouble again. Not once. Straight A's. Made my own lunch. Kept my mom laughing at the kitchen table.

JONATHON: Nobody ever worried about me. That's what I'm finding out. Nobody ever worried about me, because I was so good. My sister got in trouble, and everybody worried about her. I got a certificate.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: Have you ever asked your sister about that Saturday? Her version?
JONATHON: No. Never. She was twelve. She'd remember stuff I don't. And I've never asked. I think I've been scared her version is worse than mine.
WES: You don't have to ask her today. I'm just noticing you've never asked.
JONATHON: You're noticing a lot today. I'm going to start charging for that.
[TEXT: Nobody taught me how to lose.]

[CHAPTER: The chair is not the last word]
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: I don't want to leave a man sitting in front of an empty chair with nothing else. So here's what I believe, and I want to say it carefully. The chair at your table isn't the last word on you. Paul says, a few chapters before our verse, that every family in heaven and on earth gets its name from the Father. Fatherhood is named after God. God isn't named after fathers.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Say that again, slower, for the guys in the back. Me. I'm the guys in the back.
WES: Your dad isn't the picture God's measured against. God is the picture your dad's measured against. Your dad's an outline. Sometimes a faint one. Sometimes a torn one. The real thing was always the Father. So an empty chair at your table is a real loss. It's not a hole in heaven.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: That helps more than I want it to. Because I grew up thinking "father" meant a man who leaves, and so when somebody at church said God's a father, I heard "God's a guy with a truck." I never said that to anybody. I just kept my distance.
WES: A lot of men do. They keep God at arm's length because the word's got a dent in it.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And the other thing God does with an empty chair is put men in it. Not as a replacement for your dad. Nobody replaces a dad. But I was thirty-six and a lineman sat down across from me at a diner and asked me what I was waiting on. He sat in a chair my dad left empty. Every other Thursday for six years.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: (beat) And a guy at a men's breakfast sat in mine when I was twenty-nine. I'm not going to make a speech. I'm just saying I know who you are in that sentence.
WES: Well. (turns the mug) I'm just a man who answered a text from the HVAC guy.
JONATHON: "No pressure." I wrote "no pressure." I was so scared.
(both laugh)
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: So for the man with no dad and no Mr. Abernathy, I'd say this. The chair's still open. God puts men in empty chairs. Sometimes he's waiting on you to sit across from one. A men's table, a breakfast, an older man at church you've nodded at for five years. Most of them would say yes if you asked.
[TEXT: Fatherhood is named after God. Not the other way around.]

[CHAPTER: In the chair and still]
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: Now here's the part that got me, getting ready for this. Because I've read that verse for twenty years as a verse about bad dads. Absent dads. Your dad. And I read it this time and it said "do not exasperate." And I've been in the chair every day of my youngest's life. And I exasperated him Saturday morning.
JONATHON: The truck. I knew something happened. You were weird at car-care.
WES: The truck. So, the Saturdays. Twelve of them, at my brother's place, working off the feed-store post. Saturday was number nine. Three left. And at breakfast I'm sitting there with my coffee, thinking about the insurance. Because when a sixteen-year-old backs into something, the insurance goes up. So I said, real reasonable, "And after the Saturdays, we need to talk about you covering the insurance increase."
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Oh, no. Wes. Three Saturdays from done, and you put a new hill at the end.
WES: And he put his fork down. And he said, "Nothing's ever done with you. You keep moving it." (beat) And he got up and left for his uncle's. Didn't eat. Didn't slam anything. That's almost worse. He just left.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And I sat there. And my first thought was, he's being dramatic. My second thought was, the insurance is a real cost. And then about an hour later, at car-care, pouring coffee for a woman with a Buick, it hit me. He's right.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: I'd done it before. When he got his license, it was "you can drive to school once your grades are up," and then "once you've done thirty hours," and then "once you can parallel park." Every time he got close to done, I moved it. Because I'm scared. Because he's sixteen and I don't trust the world with him.
JONATHON: That's exasperate. That's the exact word in the verse you just read to me.
WES: That's exactly exasperate. You keep a kid running at a line you keep moving, and eventually he quits running. (beat) I've been reading that verse about your dad for twenty years, and it was about me.
[TEXT: "Nothing's ever done with you."]
[CAM: JON MS]

## Reading narrative

# The Empty Chair at Your Table
### Walk Steadfast, Episode 27 · April 6, 2027

For twenty-eight years Jonathon has said, "My dad left. I remember the dent." For the first time, he told the whole Saturday in order. He was nine, dressed for his first start as a pitcher by eight in the morning. His father loaded the truck with a duffel, his cassettes, his fishing rods, and the living-room lamp his mother read under. "You don't take a lamp to the dump." In the hall his father said, "Be good for your mom." Jonathon watched the dented tailgate turn left at the stop sign. His mother drove him to the game late, wearing sunglasses on a cloudy day, and stayed in the car. He pitched four innings and doesn't remember a single pitch. She never bought another lamp.

## Why the detail

The summary is armor. The detail is where it lives, and the detail you won't say comes out sideways. Jonathon found one. He got angry last spring when a box of toys went out his front door.

## Shoes

Wes's father was home every night. On Saturday nights he polished his church shoes on the farm section of the paper, and from the age of six Wes handed him the brush, forty-five minutes a week for ten years, with nothing said. "I loved it, and there wasn't anything in it."

## What belonged in the chair

> "Fathers, do not exasperate your children; instead, bring them up in the training and instruction of the Lord."
> Ephesians 6:4 (NIV)

In a world where fathers held nearly all the power, Paul put a fence around them. "Nobody taught me how to lose," Jonathon said. A pitching coach's "Never let them see you hurt" filled the empty chair. Then the verse turned on Wes. Three Saturdays before his son finished working off the truck, Wes added the insurance. "Nothing's ever done with you. You keep moving it." That night he wrote "TRUCK. APRIL 24. PAID IN FULL. NO MORE CONDITIONS." and signed it. By morning it was on the fridge with both signatures. Jonathon thinks the boy should have paid anyway. Wes is paying it.

## A story without an ending

A man at Wes's Thursday table, divorced nine years, texts his seventeen-year-old every Sunday night. He hasn't had a reply in eight months. "I don't know if he reads them. I know he's getting them."

## The challenge

Find the detail and tell one man. Then find the finish line you keep moving, and keep it.
