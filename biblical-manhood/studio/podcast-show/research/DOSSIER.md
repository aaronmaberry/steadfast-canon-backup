## 1. What this means for Steadfast (read this first)

1. **Video podcasts are where men are listening now.** YouTube says more than 1 billion people a month watch podcast content there and that it is the most-used podcast service in the US. Viewers watched over 400 million hours of podcasts a month on TVs in 2024 (YouTube Blog, Feb 26 2025). TV is now the main device for YouTube in the US (Neal Mohan, Feb 2025). So the show has to look right on a living-room screen and on a phone, and sound right in a truck with the screen off.
2. **Two hosts work when they play different roles.** BibleProject is the cleanest model for us. Jon Collins plays the listener: he asks the question the audience is thinking and sums up what he heard. Tim Mackie carries the depth. Jocko/Echo, Kara/Scott, and Jeff Bethke/Jon Tyson all run some version of "one carries the weight, one keeps it human." Wes carries; Jonathon asks, pushes back, and tells on himself.
3. **The fastest-growing shows do not open with chit-chat.** DOAC opens on its strongest line. Art of Manliness says on the record that it cuts the ten-minute weekend catch-up. BibleProject puts a 2-3 minute hook with soundbites up front. Aaron wants the hosts to say the episode number, date, and title every time. Jocko proves that a spoken slate at the top can be a ritual people love ("This is Jocko podcast number 142..."). So we do both. The slate takes about six seconds, then a cold open line, then a short title sting.
4. **Length.** Our lane runs 25-45 minutes, not three hours. Forming Men runs 15-25, Wild at Heart 28-40, BibleProject 45-62, and Art of Manliness averages 46. On Buzzsprout, 31% of all episodes run 20-40 minutes. Aaron's earlier runtime call was "just under 30, a little over is fine," and that still fits. Main episodes run 25-35 minutes, aiming just under 30 per Aaron's call. Friday episodes run 12-16.
5. **Cadence.** Two a week is defensible: one main conversation plus one short follow-up. Pivot publishes twice a week. Huberman pairs a long episode with a short "Essentials." Forming Men alternated content episodes with Q&A follow-ups. In a 1,200-person study (Oct 2025), 70% of podcast listeners said they want new episodes at least a couple of times a week. The catch is production cost per minute of AI video, which Aaron has to weigh (section 9).
6. **Self-plugs work when they are spaced and specific.** Huberman puts sponsors in fixed slots and keeps a "Zero-Cost Support" chapter near the end. Jocko runs a long "Support" block at the end. Forming Men put one book-launch line in every description, not every minute of audio. Ten Minute Bible Talks keeps its ask short and the same every time (rate, share, ask a question, follow, newsletter). Our rule: a standing outro of about 30 seconds every episode, one natural mid-episode subscribe line, and book, training, or site plugs on a published rotation. E001 is the exception: a full walkthrough.
7. **Cameras.** DOAC runs 9 cameras for two people, so they can hide micro-cuts, cut tight on a vulnerable line, hold a wide for tension, and frame some angles only for clips. JRE live-switches 4 cameras by speaker and pulls up visuals on screen. We generate video rather than shoot it, so the lesson is the shot list: a wide two-shot, a single on each host, a close-up for the line that matters, an over-the-shoulder, plus inserts (hands, mug, Bible on the table).
8. **Disclosure.** YouTube requires creators to flag photorealistic AI content, including "a realistic scene that didn't actually occur." Not disclosing can mean content removal or loss of YouTube Partner Program standing. If Wes and Jonathon are AI-generated on screen, every upload gets "AI use: Yes." Aaron should decide how the show talks about that (section 10).

## 2. Show profiles

Each profile covers format, open, segments, length, chemistry, camera and edit, packaging, and plugs. "Not verified" means I could not confirm it from a source and it is left out on purpose.

### Diary of a CEO (Steven Bartlett)
- **Format.** One host, one guest, long interview, built around a story arc in the edit.
- **Cold open.** The guest's sharpest soundbite first, then a one-breath intro, an origin tease, a few rapid questions, and a cut mid-answer into the theme. About 90 seconds (secondary analysis, Medium). Their trailer editor, Ant Smith, builds every 90-second trailer from four parts: hook, lesson, emotional rollercoaster, cliffhanger. They tested opening visuals, and the guest in the chair beat B-roll (Callum McDonnell interview).
- **Camera and edit.** They started with 7 cameras in Steven's flat on a Blackmagic ATEM Mini Extreme ISO, with URSA Broadcast and Pocket 6K cameras (FEED). They now run 9 cameras for two people. Director Jack Sylvester gives the reasons: hide micro-cuts and tighten pacing without visible jump cuts, frame some cameras only for trailers and social, hold a wide for tension, and cut tight on vulnerable moments. With all angles preset, he can step out of the room so it feels like the two are alone (Jack Sylvester, LinkedIn).
- **Trailer workflow.** Research the most-searched topics for the guest, read the whole transcript, reduce it to the key moments, script the trailer from quotes, then run a 15-point checklist and a "1% session" to improve it (DOAC trailer workflow video).
- **Lesson for us.** Write every episode with three or four "trailer lines," the lines that would open a cold open or a Short. Shoot the close-up on the vulnerable line.

### The Joe Rogan Experience
- **Format.** Long, loose, mostly unedited conversation. The episode number is part of the brand.
- **Camera.** Live multicam switching. The documented setup was four Canon VIXIA HF G40 cameras into a Blackmagic ATEM Television Studio Pro HD. Producer Jamie switches by speaker, cuts to a wide, and puts websites, photos, and videos on screen full or picture-in-picture (JRE Library). Jamie also does live research on air (Next Magazine).
- **Lesson for us.** "Pull that up" works. When a host cites scripture, the verse goes on screen as he says it. Our version of Jamie is the edit: verse cards, a photo of the thing they're talking about, a text line.
- **What we don't take.** Multi-hour runtime, profanity, and the "alpha" register the old show bible bans.

### Huberman Lab
- **Format.** Solo science lectures, guest interviews, and AMAs. Full episodes run about 2-2.5 hours (ADHD episode, 2h15m). "Essentials" cutdowns run 30-35 minutes and end on a "Recap & Key Takeaways" chapter (Apple listing).
- **Intro ritual.** The same short framing line every episode ("this podcast is separate from my teaching and research roles at Stanford... zero cost to consumer information"), then sponsors, then "Okay, let's talk about..." (episode pages).
- **Segments and plugs.** Sponsor blocks sit at fixed points: one cluster around 4 minutes, then single reads roughly every 25-30 minutes, all marked as chapters ("Sponsor: AG1"). Near the end there's a "Zero-Cost Support" chapter (episode chapter lists). AdReads observed 147 sponsor reads from 21 brands over 30 days. That's an observed dataset, not a full roster.
- **Lesson for us.** Put every plug in its own chapter-like slot, so it is honest and skippable and never hidden inside teaching. Use a recap chapter.

### Jocko Podcast (Jocko Willink with Echo Charles)
- **Format.** Jocko reads a book, often military history, and draws out leadership and discipline lessons. Echo is the co-host who keeps it light. Episodes run about 3 hours (ep 121, 3:00; ep 142, 3:20).
- **Intro ritual.** A spoken slate every time: "This is Jocko podcast number 142, with Echo Charles and me, Jocko Willink. Good evening, Echo." "Good evening." It's the closest proven model for Aaron's "episode number, date, and title" opener.
- **Segments.** Chapters are Opening, the book, Final thoughts and take-aways, Support, and Closing gratitude (YouTube chapters). The Support block can run about 25 minutes of store, supplements, and books.
- **Lesson for us.** A spoken slate builds ritual. A long end-of-show sales block does not fit our lane.

### SmartLess (Jason Bateman, Sean Hayes, Will Arnett)
- **Format.** Three hosts. One brings a mystery guest the other two don't know about, and there's long improvised banter before the guest (Wikipedia; Variety 2023).
- **Lesson for us.** The chemistry comes from friends who rib each other and who are not all equally informed. Jonathon shouldn't always know where Wes is going, and sometimes Jonathon brings the surprise: a story, a question, an object.

### Armchair Expert (Dax Shepard with Monica Padman)
- **Format.** Interview, then "Fact Check," where Monica checks claims from the interview. Fact Check grew into a personal catch-up and debate between the two hosts, and listeners treat it as its own show (UGA Georgia Magazine).
- **Lesson for us.** A recurring segment where the second host holds the first host accountable becomes the heart of the show. Our version is "Check the Box" on Fridays. Jonathon checks whether Wes did the challenge, and the other way round.

### All-In and Pivot
- **All-In.** Four hosts, a banter cold open full of inside jokes, then a docket of topics. The hosts take turns and openly disagree (secondary guide). The lesson: real disagreement, stated plainly and left unresolved when it should be, keeps men watching.
- **Pivot.** Two hosts (Kara Swisher, Scott Galloway), twice a week. Banter open, news topics, then a closing "wins and fails" segment and listener mail (transcript listings). The lesson is a fixed closing segment that both hosts answer every time. Ours is "Carry This," one line each.

### BibleProject podcast (Tim Mackie, Jon Collins)
- **Format.** Weekly, 45-62 minutes, two hosts with occasional guests, run as long multi-episode series (Spirit of God, Exodus Way). Chapters are in the show notes, along with full credits and an official transcript (BibleProject site; Apple listing).
- **Open.** A 2-3 minute hook with key questions and soundbites, then Jon: "Today, Tim Mackie and I begin a new series... Thanks for joining us. Here we go. Hello, Tim." (Apple transcript; Kalsow breakdown, secondary)
- **Roles.** Jon is curious, plays the listener, asks probing questions, and sums up how he understands it. Tim makes hard things approachable without talking down.
- **Rhythm.** A short music interlude after a big idea, so the listener can sit with it. "Let's take a breath and review" episodes in the middle of a series. Previews of next week at the end. Dynamic pre-roll announcements that ask for listener questions (Kalsow, secondary).
- **Lesson for us.** This is the closest structural model. Use seasons, a hook up front, a host who plays the listener, breathing room after heavy lines, and review episodes.

### Ten Minute Bible Talks
- **Format.** Daily, one passage per episode, explained and applied, 7-13 minutes, with rotating co-hosts. Crowd-funded with no ads. 1,400+ episodes (TMBT About; Apple; PodRanker, secondary).
- **Outro.** The same short ask every time, carried in the description too: leave a rating and share, use #asktmbt for questions, visit the site and follow on Instagram/Facebook/X, subscribe to the newsletter.
- **Lesson for us.** A standing outro said the same way, fast, every time, stops sounding like an ask. It becomes a sign-off.

### Allen Parr (The BEAT)
- **Format.** A YouTube teaching channel with 1M+ subscribers. As of Nov 2023 he had no podcast (Ministries That Podcast). He's a teaching-video model, not a podcast model.
- **Lessons (DTS Table interview).** Hook in the first 15-20 seconds with a concrete reason to stay. Watch click-through rate first, then watch time. Use series and playlists. Use real faces on thumbnails.

### Wild at Heart (John Eldredge)
- **Format.** Weekly video podcast with 895 items, 28-40 minutes. John plus rotating co-hosts (Allen, Morgan, Craig, Blaine, Stasi). Multi-part series ("Ruling Your Domain," Parts 1-5). Several times a year, a "Your Questions Answered" episode is set aside for viewer questions. Talks from live events get repurposed as episodes (Wild at Heart site).
- **Lesson for us.** A men's ministry can run a weekly video podcast for years on series plus Q&A. Our 24-session training is our "live event" material, but it's never re-aired. It's referenced and linked.

### Jefferson Bethke
- **Forming Men Podcast (with Jon Tyson).** Weekly, 15-25 minutes (about 19 on average). Content episodes alternate with "(Q&A episode)" follow-ups that answer what listeners wrestled with the week before. One book-launch-team line sits in every episode description (Apple; My Podcast Data).
- **The Real Life Podcast (with Alyssa).** Couple banter, the stated goal of feeling like you're "sitting in our living room drinking a cup of coffee," and whole episodes of listener Q&A (The Banner).
- **Lesson for us.** Our Friday episode is the Q&A and follow-up slot. The book plug lives in the description every week and on mic only on rotation.

### Art of Manliness (Brett McKay)
- **Format.** One host interviewing guests. Under an hour by policy (46 minutes on average). Ums, uhs, and dead stretches are cut on purpose. They say plainly that they skip the "ten minutes about our weekends" open (AoM).
- **Launch episode.** Brett's first episode set expectations in plain words: once a week, 20-30 minutes, who would come on, what it was for (AoM #1000 transcript). That's a direct model for our E001 "what to expect" segment.
- **Show notes.** Chapters, plus a "Resources related to the podcast" link block.

## 3. Patterns across the shows

| Element | What the leaders do | Steadfast choice |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Strongest line first (DOAC), spoken slate (Jocko), short hook with soundbites (BibleProject) | Spoken slate (number, date, title, about 6 seconds), then a one-line cold open, then a 3-second title sting |
| Host roles | Carrier plus asker (BibleProject, Jocko/Echo) | Wes carries the teaching and the long view. Jonathon asks, pushes back, and confesses |
| Chemistry | Real ribbing, uneven information (SmartLess), real disagreement (All-In) | At least one honest disagreement per main episode, not always settled |
| Fixed segments | Fact Check (Armchair), wins and fails (Pivot), Q&A episodes (Wild at Heart, Forming Men) | Friday "Check the Box" and "From the Table" questions; "Carry This" closer every episode |
| Length | 15-60 minutes in our lane | Main 25-35 minutes, Friday 12-16 |
| Breath | Music interlude after a big idea (BibleProject) | One or two 3-4 second breathers per main episode |
| Plugs | Fixed, labeled slots (Huberman); description lines (Forming Men) | Spaced rotation plus a standing outro; E001 full |
| Clips | Cameras framed for clips (DOAC); 5-10 clips per episode (secondary) | 4-6 Shorts per main episode, 2 per Friday, marked in script |

## 4. Camera and editing patterns

- **Angles.** Every two-person show we looked at uses at least a wide two-shot plus a single on each host. A common three-camera podcast setup puts each host centered in his single so it crops cleanly to 9:16 (secondary, video podcast setup guides). DOAC adds overhead, over-the-shoulder, and clip-only framings.
- **Cut rhythm.** Cut on speaker change, on a reaction, and on emphasis. Practitioner guides suggest a cut every 3-7 seconds in clips (secondary). In long-form, DOAC uses extra angles to hide edits rather than to cut faster. For us, long-form cuts every 6-12 seconds in conversation, faster in Shorts.
- **Punch-ins.** A 10-15% digital push on the same single for a key line. It's the cheapest emphasis, and it works on generated footage too.
- **On-screen material.** JRE pulls up the referenced thing. BibleProject adds annotations in its app. We put a verse card on screen when scripture is quoted (exact NIV, reference, "NIV"), text for the one line that matters, and lower thirds for names on first appearance in an episode.
- **B-roll.** DOAC's own test found the guest in the chair beats B-roll as an opener. B-roll goes inside stories, never as the first shot.
- **Chapters.** Huberman, BibleProject, Jocko, and AoM all publish chapters. YouTube needs the first chapter at 00:00, at least three chapters, and each at least 10 seconds long.
- **Shorts.** YouTube Shorts run up to 3 minutes. Instagram Reels reach non-followers best at 3 minutes or less. TikTok in-app uploads go to 10 minutes. All are 1080x1920 (secondary spec guides). Each Short gets a hook in the first 2 seconds, burned-in captions, and must stand alone.

## 5. Titles and thumbnails

- **YouTube's guidance.** Accurate, short, key words first. Put episode numbers and branding at the end. Custom thumbnails (90% of the best-performing videos use them). Test up to three title and thumbnail options with Test & Compare, which picks by watch-time share (YouTube Help).
- **What the big shows do (observed, not from a published source).** Podcast thumbnails are mostly the host or guest face plus 2-5 words. Faith and men's shows lean on a real face and a plain question.
- **Steadfast.** Show the topic or tension in 3 words or fewer, and keep the episode number off the art. Use both hosts in a locked two-shot, or one host's real reaction. Navy, gold, and bone. Titles are the question a man would type ("Why Do I Feel Behind at 40?"), with "Steadfast Men Podcast Ep. 3" at the end of the YouTube title only if Aaron wants numbers visible.

## 6. Retention

- Confirm the click in the first 5-10 seconds (Paddy Galloway). Our slate plus cold open has to land the title's promise by about 0:30.
- Re-engage around minute 3 and minute 6 (MrBeast production memo). In a 30-minute conversation, a story, a disagreement, or a verse card every 3-5 minutes does that job.
- Say the listener's question out loud (BibleProject). That's Jonathon's job.
- Preview what's coming ("in a minute I want to tell you about my truck"), so the open loops close later.
- End when it's done. Don't add a second goodbye after the outro.

## 7. Plugs and outros

- **When to ask.** The Podcast Host advises a brief ask early, a fuller one at the peak, and a brief one at the end with a callback. Asks before any value cost you new listeners. Practitioner guides add: one main call to action, about 20-30 seconds, payoff first, and the spoken ask should match the end-screen element.
- **End screens.** The last 5-20 seconds, up to four elements, video at least 25 seconds long. Plan the last 20 seconds in the edit (YouTube Help).
- **Models.** Ten Minute Bible Talks (short and identical every time), Huberman (a labeled support chapter), Jocko (a closing-gratitude ritual), Forming Men (one product line in descriptions).
- **Steadfast.** The standing outro runs 25-35 seconds every episode: next episode, subscribe, where to find us, links in the description. Three to five rotating wordings so it never sounds canned. Mid-episode plugs follow a rotation (show bible section 8).

## 8. Launch episodes

- Brett McKay's first AoM episode told listeners the cadence, the length, who would be on, and why. Forming Men's episode 1 opened on "the state of being a man today," so the content started in the first episode. Our E001 does both: full introductions and expectations, then a real first conversation. It's allowed to run longer than a normal episode.

## 9. Cadence decision

- **Recommendation.** Two episodes a week. A main conversation on Tuesday (25-35 minutes) and a short "Field Notes" episode on Friday (12-16 minutes): challenge check, a question from the table, what each man actually did that week.
- **Why.**
  - It matches proven pairings: Huberman's long episode plus Essentials, Forming Men's content episode plus Q&A, Pivot's twice weekly.
  - Listeners say they want new episodes at least a couple of times a week (70% in the Oct 2025 study).
  - The Friday slot is where the weekly challenge lives, and the challenge is the habit Aaron wants.
  - Buzzsprout data shows 35% of shows publish every 3-7 days and 36% every 8-14. Twice a week puts us ahead of most shows in the feed.
- **Risk.** Generated video costs money per minute. Two a week is about 45-55 minutes of finished two-host video weekly, roughly 2,400-2,800 minutes a year.
- **Fallback.** If that's too much: Tuesday full video, Friday audio plus one vertical clip (Friday works with eyes closed), or weekly only with Friday folded into Tuesday's last ten minutes. The calendar holds in all three versions.

## 10. AI hosts and disclosure

- YouTube requires disclosure of photorealistic AI content that "generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur." The "AI use" setting goes on at upload, and a label can show on the player. Disclosure "won't limit a video's audience or impact its eligibility to earn money." Creators who consistently don't disclose risk labels being applied for them, content removal, or loss of YouTube Partner Program standing.
- If Wes and Jonathon are generated, every upload sets AI use to Yes.
- **Open question for Aaron, and it matters for a faith audience.** The hosts tell "their own stories." If the men and their stories are composites, the show should say so once, plainly, in E001's description and on the About page. The adopted line (show bible, section 13) reads: "Walk Steadfast is made with AI-generated video and voices. Wes and Jonathon are characters, and their stories are composites drawn from real men's lives." The scripts are written so this disclosure doesn't break anything.
