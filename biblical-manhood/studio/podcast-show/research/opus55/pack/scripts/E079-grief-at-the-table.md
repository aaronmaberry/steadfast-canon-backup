[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And I want to say what we mean by grieving sideways, because I think it's the thing most men do and don't know they're doing it. A woman, a lot of the time, grieves at it. She looks right at it. She cries, she talks, she calls her sister. A lot of men grieve sideways. We go at it from an angle. We work more. We fix things that aren't broken. We get mad at the lawnmower. We take long drives with the radio off. And we'd tell you we're fine. We're not lying, exactly. We just haven't looked straight at it.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And sideways isn't all wrong. Some of us grieve with our hands, and that's real. But if sideways is the only way you ever do it, it doesn't go anywhere. It just goes in the truck with you for ten years.
[CAM: 2S]
WES: The mark this week is Pick Up the Weight. Because grief is a weight. And the temptation is to set it down in the barn and leave it there. You can't. It'll still be there. You've got to pick it up and carry it to somebody.
[TEXT: Some men grieve sideways.]

[CHAPTER: Saturdays at the place]
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: So here's how I did it. My dad died on a Sunday night in the hospital. I'd been there. He'd said the thing he'd never said, a few days before. I held his hand when he went. And Monday morning, I put on a tie and went to work. Sat through a meeting. Came home. Helped plan a funeral like I was planning a work event. Spoke at the funeral. Did a good job. People said so. Didn't cry.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: And then for about a year, every Saturday, I drove out to the place my brother runs, my dad's place, and I fixed things. Every Saturday. Fences that didn't need fixing. A gate that swung fine. I re-hung a barn door that had been hanging crooked since 1985, and my dad had liked it that way. My brother'd come out with his coffee, look at what I was doing, and say, "Well." And go back in.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Did your wife know what you were doing?
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: She knew before I did. She let it go for about six months. And then one Saturday in the spring, she rode out with me, which she never did. And she sat on the tailgate while I restrung a fence my dad had strung in 1990, and after about an hour she said, "You know he's not going to come check your work." [CAM: WES CU]
WES: And I'm not telling you that so you'll feel sorry for me. I'm telling you because I think there are men listening right now who've been fixing fences for a year. Or working Saturdays. JONATHON: I think I understand your brother. He's grieving sideways too, just from the other side. You fixed everything on that place. He won't touch the one thing you fixed. WES: Maybe that's the one for the tailgate.

[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And I'll tell you where I actually learned how to sit with grief, because it wasn't from a man. Dani works labor and delivery. And most nights on that floor are the best nights anybody ever has. But some nights, not many, a baby doesn't make it. And she's the nurse in the room.

Wes's father died on a Sunday night. Monday morning Wes went to work. For about a year after, he drove out to his father's place every Saturday and fixed fences that didn't need fixing, until his wife sat on the tailgate and said, "You know he's not going to come check your work."
