## Reading narrative

# The Weight With Your Name on It
### Walk Steadfast, Episode 9 · February 2, 2027

Most men have two piles mixed up. They're carrying things that aren't theirs, and they've set down things that are. This week Wes Hart and Jonathon take up the second of the four marks: pick up the weight.

## The check-engine light

Jonathon and his wife have been saying "we should do a will" for three years, since their son was born. At Christmas, over pie, his mother-in-law asked who would get the kids. The table went quiet. "We're working on it," Jonathon said. They weren't.

The forms aren't the hard part, he admits. The hard part is one line: who raises the children if both parents are gone. Answering it means sitting in his own kitchen, imagining he's dead, and choosing. So for three years the will sat on the family's dashboard like a check-engine light, glowing so long he stopped seeing it.

He had always counted it as a shared job, which in practice meant his wife's. This week he decided it was his. "I'm the guy whose dad left when he was nine without making any arrangements for anything. I, of all people, know what it's like when a man doesn't plan for what happens when he's gone."

## The dent

Wes had the opposite problem. In December his sixteen-year-old backed the truck into a post at the feed store. Wes said "Don't worry about it," paid the body shop himself, and called it grace. Three weeks later, after a rolling stop, his son shrugged: "It's just a truck." The dent had cost the boy nothing, so the truck weighed nothing. "I took his weight and called it love," Wes says. "Some of it was love. A lot of it was me not wanting to have a hard conversation."

## One verse, two kinds of weight

> "for each one should carry their own load."
> Galatians 6:5 (NIV)

Paul wrote to churches busy comparing themselves to each other, and told them to look at their own work. Three verses earlier he tells them to carry each other's burdens. Wes puts the two together with a picture from thirty years of helping people move: "Every man carries his own duffel. Nobody carries a couch alone." Your work, your words, your marriage and your walk with God are your duffel. A death, a diagnosis or a lost job is a couch, and brothers grab a corner. Men get them backwards. We carry the couch alone because asking feels like failing, and we leave the duffel by the door because it's boring.

## Is this just bootstraps?

Jonathon, raised by a mother who did everything alone, pushes back. Wes agrees there's a version of this that's "just pride with a Bible verse on it." The verse doesn't mean nobody helps you. It means nobody else is responsible for your part. It's on the side of every woman who had to carry a man's load for him.

Then Jonathon names a weight he's carried since he was nine, when his mother told him he was the man of the house: her happiness on every holiday. Wes draws the line carefully. Honoring your mother is yours. Whether she's happy on Christmas afternoon belongs to her and God. The test is simple. "When you carry something that's yours, it gets lighter for somebody. When you carry something that isn't, nobody gets lighter. You just get heavier."

And for the man who's barely standing: "For a man who's barely standing, asking for help is the heaviest thing he'll lift all week."

## What they actually did

Jonathon called a lawyer from his church and booked an appointment for Thursday. The guardian decision is set for Wednesday night. Wes sat his son down with the actual bill, said, "I was wrong to just pay this," and arranged for the boy to work it off over twelve Saturdays at his uncle's cattle place. That also gives Wes two truck rides a week with his son and coffee in his brother's kitchen. He has also taken over his mother's weekly physical-therapy drives. She counts his reps and has noticed his limp.

## This week's challenge

Find one weight with your name on it that someone else has been carrying, and take one real step on it before Friday. Then name one thing you're carrying that isn't yours, and write down whose it is. Married men can ask, "What do you carry around here that's really mine?" Men who live alone can start with the budget, the doctor, and the emergency contact. Divorced dads, your weekends are completely yours.

> If the light's been on so long you stopped seeing it, that's probably the one.
