[CHAPTER: Pull up a chair]

WES: Pull up a chair. New season. We're calling this one The Long Road. Fall, the stretch from Labor Day to Advent, when nothing's a holiday and nothing's a big moment. Just the miles. It's the part that decides everything.

[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And we're starting with that. The work nobody sees. The practices you coach that nobody films. The carpool at six in the morning. The shift that starts before anybody's up. [CAM: 2S]
WES: The mark this week is Work for God's Pay. Because if the clap's your pay, you're going to be underpaid your whole life. And if God's your pay, then nothing you do in the dark is wasted.

[CHAPTER: The tacos]
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: So, the tacos. I said I'd bring breakfast to the shop Labor Day, because half our techs were on call, because nobody's air conditioner knows it's a holiday. Six o'clock. Two big pans. And I'd promised myself, no speech. I'd stand by the door and shake every man's hand and say his name. Which I did. I'd had to look up a few names.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: And at the end, one of the younger guys, as a joke, started clapping. "Let's hear it for the boss." And they all clapped. For me. For the tacos. And I stood there and I felt it. I liked it. I'm not going to pretend I didn't. That warm thing in your chest. And I broke my rule. I made a speech. It was short. I said, "Wrong direction," and I pointed at them, and I clapped back. About ten seconds. Then I sat down and ate a taco, and they looked at me like I'd lost my mind.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And then I looked around that room. Those men had been in attics all summer. A hundred and thirty-eight degrees. Old ladies sitting in their cars. And in all my years at that company, I've never once seen anybody clap for one of them. Not a customer. Not me. Nobody claps for a capacitor. And the guy who drove to the taqueria got a round of applause. That's backwards.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: Where was your senior tech?

[CHAPTER: Three hundred mornings]
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: I want to tell you about a clap that came late, because it happens sometimes, and I think it's dangerous if you build for it.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: When my oldest boy played high school football, August practice started at six in the morning, before the heat. Three summers. And two boys from down the road played too. Their dad worked nights at the plant and their mother didn't drive. So every morning at five-thirty, I'd pull up at their house and honk once, and they'd come out with their cleats in their hands and half asleep, and I'd drive the three of them to the field. Three Augusts. And then in the fall, the same thing, for the early lifts on Tuesdays and Thursdays.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: Nobody ever said a word about it. Their dad waved at me in the church parking lot once, I think. That's fine. I wasn't doing it for a wave.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: And in July, at the feed store, a young man walks up to me. Big. Short hair. And I didn't know him. And he says, "Mr. Hart. You drove me to football." It was the younger one. He's twenty now, home on leave from the Army. And he said, "I did the math one time. You drove me something like three hundred mornings. My dad never once drove me. Not one." And he shook my hand. And then he said, "I just wanted you to know I knew."
[pause 1s]
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And I'll be honest, I didn't know what to do with it. I mumbled something. I said, "Well, you were on the way." He wasn't on the way. And I drove home and sat in the truck in the driveway for a while.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Why's that dangerous?
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: Because it felt so good. And if I let myself, I'd start doing things hoping for that. For the young man at the feed store years later. And most of the time he never comes. Most of the boys you drive never say a word, and they shouldn't have to. I'd have driven those mornings the same if I'd never seen him again. I have to keep it that way. The handshake was a gift. It wasn't the pay.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And he's going to drive somebody's kid someday.
WES: That's the only clap I want. That he drives somebody.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: And I'll say this for the dads listening. Somebody's driving your kid right now, and you don't know it. A coach, a neighbor, a youth leader, the mom who does the carpool on Wednesdays. And they're not going to get a handshake at the feed store for fifteen years, if ever. You could be the handshake now. You don't have to wait for your boy to grow up and do it.
[TEXT: The handshake was a gift. It wasn't the pay.]

[CHAPTER: The loud sigh]
[CAM: WES MS]

[CAM: WES CU]

His wife would rather he didn't do the dishes than do them like a martyr.
[CAM: WES MS]

And she'd rather have the pan done quiet than done loud. Every wife I've ever asked has said the same thing. Do less if you have to. Just don't do it at her.

## Reading narrative

# What You Build When Nobody Claps
### Walk Steadfast, Episode 71 · September 7, 2027

On Labor Day morning Jonathon brought tacos to the shop at six. One of the younger techs started clapping, and the whole room joined in, for the man who'd brought breakfast. Those men had spent the summer in attics. Nobody claps for a capacitor. The senior tech had already left on a call; he ate his tacos cold on his truck seat at ten.

In his thirties Wes coached Little League for three seasons: an hour early to drag the infield, the ice, the rain calls. At the banquet the coach-of-the-year plaque went to his assistant. At breakfast the next Thursday, Mr. Abernathy tapped the table twice. "Who were you doing it for?" And then: "If it was for the kids, you already got paid. They played ball."

> "Therefore, my dear brothers and sisters, stand firm. Let nothing move you. Always give yourselves fully to the work of the Lord, because you know that your labor in the Lord is not in vain."
> 1 Corinthians 15:58 (NIV)

"Therefore" points back to the resurrection. Because Jesus rose, there is a day when everything gets seen, and nothing given to the Lord is wasted.

## The handshake isn't the pay

For three Augusts Wes drove two neighbor boys to six a.m. football practice. This July one of them, home from the Army, found him at the feed store: "I just wanted you to know I knew." It was a gift. It wasn't the pay.
