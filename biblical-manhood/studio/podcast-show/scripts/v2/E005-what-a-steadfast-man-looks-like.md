**Arc.** *Hook:* the sentence for the back of the card, and Jonathon's suspicion that the last three words were written for him. *Tension:* four marks, each with a weekday picture: the gutter, a dad's dropped weight landing in a son's yard, the scariest text Jonathon ever sent, and a coach-pitch parent saying "great job, Coach." Jonathon worries "goes first" will be misused, and asks whether "pay" means earning. *Turn:* Philippians 2:5, where all four marks come from one mindset. Then real pushback: lists don't change people, and these aren't just for men. The hosts disagree about which mark is hardest and leave it there. *Resolution:* a week of one-out-of-four scores, a Christmas money talk, a second brother, and a customer told the truth. The challenge: find your weakest mark and tell one man.

## SEGMENT 1 · The back of the card

WES: Here's the plan. I said the sentence once. Now we'll take it apart, one piece at a time, and I want you to see each piece on a regular day. A sentence you can't picture on a Tuesday is just a slogan. If you're driving, don't write. Listen now, write it at the next stop.

JONATHON: Can I say what I like about it before we take it apart? It's all verbs. Stops, picks up, goes, works. There's not one adjective in there. It doesn't say a man is strong or tough or tall. Which is good news for a man his son calls medium.

WES: That's on purpose. You can't feel your way into this sentence. You can only do it. A man who's not tall, not tough, not loud, not anything the internet says a man is supposed to be, can do every word of it by Thursday. And a man who's all of those things can fail every word of it. I've met him plenty of times.

JONATHON: And there's four. Why not five? I'd add "prays." Or "loves his wife." Or honestly, "grills," because a man should know his way around a brisket.

WES: Prayer's in all four. You can't do any of them without it. Loving your wife is in all four too. Grilling is optional. There's no fifth mark.

---

## SEGMENT 2 · Stops waiting, picks up the weight

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on the first two. Stops waiting means when it's yours to do, start now, before you feel like it. The verse under it is James 4:17. If you know the good you ought to do and you don't do it, James calls that sin. Not a personality type. Sin.

JONATHON: And the picture is your gutter. Fourteen months. Fixed now, for the record, with an eighty-one-year-old holding the ladder.

WES: The picture is my gutter. And here's what's sneaky about waiting. It doesn't feel like doing anything wrong. It feels like nothing. I never decided to leave that gutter hanging. I just never decided to fix it. Every day, I just didn't. It feels like peace, and it isn't. There's something on your list right now that's yours to do, you know it's yours, and you're waiting to feel like it.

JONATHON: Mine was training wheels. Ruby's bike. Last summer I told her, "This weekend we'll take them off." Every weekend. Too hot, a game on, a work thing. By October she'd stopped asking, which was worse than the asking. Then one Saturday Dani's on shift and I just grabbed a wrench before I could talk myself out of it. Forty minutes of running behind her up and down the street holding the seat, and she rode to the stop sign by herself.

WES: And how long had it actually been sitting on the list?

JONATHON: About four months, for a forty-minute job. And she didn't remember the four months. She remembered the stop sign. I'm the one who remembers the four months.

JONATHON: Okay, mark two is mine. Can I take it? Because I think I've been living inside this one my whole life without a name for it.

WES: Take it. Picks up the weight means carry what's yours, even when somebody else dropped it. Galatians 6:5. Paul says, "for each one should carry their own load." Your load. Not everybody's. Not nobody's. Yours.

JONATHON: "Even when somebody else dropped it." That's my whole story. My dad dropped a lot of weight when he drove off. A wife working doubles, two kids. And for years I thought, that's his weight, he dropped it, why should I have to carry it? Here's what I figured out, slowly, mostly at breakfast with this guy. I don't carry his weight. I can't. He'll answer for that. But my kids are mine. My marriage is mine. How I treat my mom now is mine. And the stuff he left in me, the anger, the "win the room," that's in my house now.

WES: You didn't drop it. But it landed in your yard.

JONATHON: It landed in my yard. I can stand there yelling about who dropped it, or I can pick it up. And if you never had anything like that, the picture's smaller, but it's the same. It's the dishes you walked past. It's the bill you let your wife worry about alone. It's the thing at work that fell between two desks, and one of them's yours.

[BOARD: Not everybody's load. Not nobody's. Yours.]

## SEGMENT 3 · Goes first, God's pay

WES: Mark three, goes first. Step out ahead of your people, scared or not. In John 10, Jesus says a good shepherd goes on ahead of his sheep, and they follow him because they know his voice. A shepherd doesn't push from behind with a stick. He walks in front. And the picture is you, the text.

JONATHON: The scariest text I ever sent a man. "Hey, this is Jonathon from the breakfast, the HVAC guy, no pressure." I had to get your number from the contractor.

WES: Somebody had to go first, and I didn't have your number. That's this mark. At home it looks like the first apology after a fight, the first one to bring up the hard thing, the first one to pray at the table.

JONATHON: Okay, here's the one I'm worried about. Guys are going to misuse this. "Goes first" turns into "I'm in charge, I decide, I go through the door first, I get the remote." Men hear "lead," and they hear "boss." I've worked for that guy. I've been married to that guy, for about two years, until Dani set me straight.

WES: That's a real worry. Going first means going first into the cost. Not first to the good seat. The shepherd goes first because the front is where the wolf is. If your going first never costs you anything, you're not leading. You're cutting in line. The first apology costs you. The prayer out loud costs you. The remote doesn't cost you a thing.

JONATHON: I still think guys will misuse it, and I'm bringing it up again in February when we get to it.

WES: I'm counting on it. Mark four. Works for God's pay, not the room's. Colossians 3:23. "Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord, not for human masters." Then Paul says you'll receive an inheritance from the Lord as a reward.

JONATHON: I grew up Easter-and-Christmas, so I have to ask. "Pay." Does that mean I'm earning my way in? Because I've heard you say a hundred times you can't earn it.

WES: You can't, and I'm glad you asked. Paul says it plainly in Ephesians 2. It's grace. It's a gift. Nobody gets to brag. You don't work for God's pay to get into the family. You're already in. Look at his word. Inheritance. Nobody earns an inheritance. A son works his father's land because it's his father's, and someday it's his. Not to get adopted. He's already adopted.

[BOARD: Ephesians 2:8 (NIV) · "For it is by grace you have been saved, through faith"]

JONATHON: Okay, that helps. Because for me the opposite of God's pay is the room's pay. The parents at coach-pitch saying "great job, Coach" when we were oh and six. That's my pay. I've been working for it since I was nine. It's great for about an hour, and then it's gone, and I need it again tomorrow.

WES: That's the thing about the room's pay. It doesn't keep. God's pay keeps.

JONATHON: So the front of my card says "win the room," and the back says "not the room's." You planned this.

WES: I planned it a little.

## SEGMENT 4 · The Word

WES: We're about halfway. Where does a sentence like this come from? I don't want anybody thinking we picked four things that sounded good on a coffee mug. It starts with one line. Paul's writing to a church he loves, in Philippi, and they're fighting with each other. Jonathon, read it.

JONATHON: "In your relationships with one another, have the same mindset as Christ Jesus." And then there's a colon, so he's about to tell you what that mindset is.

WES: In the next few verses Paul says Jesus was God, and didn't hold on to that for his own advantage. He made himself nothing. He took the nature of a servant. He humbled himself and obeyed, all the way to a cross. Now look at him. He didn't wait for us to get better. He came. That's stop waiting. He carried what wasn't his, our sin, and didn't put it down. That's pick up the weight. He went ahead of us, into the worst of it. That's goes first.

JONATHON: And he definitely wasn't winning the room. Everybody walked away from him. The crowd, the disciples, all of them.

WES: He lost the room completely, on purpose, for us. He was working for his Father's joy, not the crowd's. So the four marks aren't four rules a man adds to his life. They're what that one mindset looks like on a man, in a house with a gutter.

JONATHON: That actually changes how I hear it. I've always heard lists like this as a bar to clear. Like, here's the standard, try harder. But if it's his mindset, then I'm not trying to become a better version of me. I'm trying to look a little more like him, and he's the one doing the changing.

WES: That's right. And it's why you don't panic on a zero night. The mark isn't a grade. It's a direction.

[BOARD: Hebrews 12:2 (NIV) · "For the joy set before him he endured the cross, scorning its shame, and sat down at the right hand of the throne of God."]

## SEGMENT 5 · Pushback

JONATHON: Okay, here's my problem with all of this. It's a list. I've been to a lot of sales trainings. Every one has a list. The five C's of closing. I've never once changed because of a list.

WES: You're right. A list never changed anybody. Here's the difference I'd draw. A sales list is a technique, for getting something. This is a description of a man Jesus is changing. The sentence doesn't change you. It tells you what to look for. Hold it up on a Tuesday night and ask, did I wait on anything today? Did I pick up what was mine? Did I go first anywhere? Who was I working for? Most nights you'll get one out of four. That's a good night.

JONATHON: Second problem. Dani does all four of these better than I do. She goes first constantly. She works nights for patients who'll never remember her name. Isn't this just "be a good Christian"?

WES: In one way, yes. Paul wrote Philippians to the whole church, women and men. We'd all do well to watch Dani. But we say it to men on purpose, because we tend to fail in a particular way. Go back to the garden. When things went wrong, the man was standing right there and didn't say a word. He waited. That's the recliner, and that's the long way home.

JONATHON: Different furniture, same story. Okay, last one. You've sat with hundreds of men. Which mark do they fail at the most?

WES: Stop waiting. Not even close. Every other mark starts with it. You can't pick up the weight while you're waiting. Most men I've sat with aren't bad men. They're waiting men. Waiting for the kids to get older, waiting for the job to settle, waiting to feel ready.

JONATHON: See, I think it's the pay one. Because the pay one is underneath the other three. Why do I wait? Because the thing I'm waiting on doesn't pay. Nobody claps when you do bath time. Nobody claps when you pick up somebody else's weight. Going first into the cost, by definition, nobody's clapping. A man still working for the room will wait forever on anything the room won't clap for.

WES: That's an "I'm going to think about it on the drive home" answer. You might be right about you. A man who grew up winning rooms, the pay is probably the root. A man like me, who grew up not making trouble, I think it's the waiting. I didn't need applause. I needed comfort.

JONATHON: So we disagree, and I'm fine leaving it there. Fair warning, though. I'm going to keep bringing it up.

## SEGMENT 6 · What we actually did

WES: I wrote the sentence on the back of my card, put it in my Bible, and for five nights I did the one-out-of-four check before bed. Monday, one. The body shop called with the estimate on the truck my youngest backed into a post at the feed store, and I didn't yell. I handled the whole thing myself instead of making him do it.

JONATHON: Hang on, though. That might be the opposite of picking up the weight. That might be you picking up his weight, Wes.

WES: That's fair. I'll take a half. Tuesday zero, Wednesday one, Thursday zero, Friday two. Friday I went first. My wife and I had a disagreement about Christmas money that had been sitting there since Christmas, and I brought it up before she did. Then I picked up my part, which was that I'd said yes to a thing for my brother without asking her.

JONATHON: The brother you haven't called since Thanksgiving? Because that's a whole other mark.

WES: Different brother. I have two brothers. I haven't called the other one.

JONATHON: We'll come back to that. Mine. The card's on my truck visor. Monday I had a call with a big customer whose order was going to be three weeks late. Our warehouse messed up. Everything in me wanted to win the room. Blame the manufacturer, promise something I couldn't deliver. I had the visor down, and I saw the card. So I told him the truth. Our warehouse messed up, it's three weeks, here's what I can do, here's what I can't. He was mad for about two minutes, then he said, "Thanks for not jerking me around." And he didn't cancel.

WES: That's God's pay.

JONATHON: Is it, though? He kept the order. Honestly, that feels a lot like room pay to me.

WES: The order's a bonus. God's pay was you being a man who told the truth when lying was easier. Nobody saw that but God and your visor.

JONATHON: My visor and my score sheet. I did the nightly check too, and mine were worse than yours. Monday one, for the customer. Tuesday zero, because I spent bath time answering emails in the hallway with the door open, which counts as being there to nobody but me. Wednesday, one. Thursday zero. Friday I left my phone in the truck and did the dishes while Dani slept, so one. But Dani asked me Saturday what I was scoring every night, and when I told her, she said, "You know you're supposed to do the marks, not just grade them." Ten years, and she still edits me.

## SEGMENT 7 · This week's challenge

WES: Before we're done, here's the challenge. Write the sentence on the back of your card. Then pick the one mark you're weakest in. Just one. And tell your one man which one.

JONATHON: Married guys, ask your wife which mark she sees you do best. Not worst. Best. This one's nicer than the last one. Then ask which one she'd love to see more, if she wants to tell you.

JONATHON: And if you're twenty-two, single, two roommates, I was you, only worse. Stop waiting is the job. Show up like it matters before anybody's watching. Pick up the weight is your own money and your own laundry. Going first might mean being the first guy in your friend group to say, "I think you're drinking too much." Nobody said that to me at twenty-two. I'd have listened.

WES: Single, ask a friend the same two questions. If you're a divorced dad, going first might be being decent at the handoff in the parking lot. And if you're sixty-five, retired, and your wife passed a couple of years ago, your waiting looks like the recliner, and nobody would blame you. Picking up the weight might be your grandkids. Going first might be calling your grown son first, after years of him always calling you. And God's pay matters more now, because most of the room that used to clap is gone. You're not done. Most men your age I know are in the middle of something. And everybody, every night this week, do the one-out-of-four check before bed. Takes thirty seconds.
