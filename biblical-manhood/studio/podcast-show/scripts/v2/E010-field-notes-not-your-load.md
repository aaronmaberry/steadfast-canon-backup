**Arc.** *Hook:* Wes's first emoji ever, a thumbs-up, sent at 4:01 on knee day. *Tension:* the report. Arthritis but no surgery ("It's not Dad"), PT at his mother's clinic with his mother counting reps, and twenty-seven years of birthday cards his wife signed in his name. His oldest son's job decision goes in the "not mine" column. Jonathon and Dani spend two hours deciding who raises the kids. He learns the mortgage date, and finds out the Sunday calls he's been missing are his. *Turn:* how do you know a load isn't yours, and where does it go? Back to its owner, or up to God (1 Peter 5:7). *Resolution:* the two-column list, a dinosaur on a phone, and a tease of Valentine's week.

WES: It was an accident.

JONATHON: It was a thumbs-up, Wes. Four-oh-one. You have to hold it down. That was on purpose.

WES: This is Walk Steadfast, Field Notes. I'm Wes Hart.

JONATHON: And I'm Jonathon. Tuesday's challenge had two parts. Put your hand on one weight with your name on it, and name one thing you're carrying that isn't yours. And married guys, ask your wife, "What do you carry around here that's really mine?"

## SEGMENT 1 · Wes checks the box

JONATHON: Knees first, though. Before the forgery. I need to know you're okay.

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on it. Arthritis, both knees, the right one worse. The doctor turned the screen around and showed me the pictures, and it looked like a gravel road. No surgery. Not now. Physical therapy twice a week, exercises at home, and he'd like there to be a little less of me around the middle.

JONATHON: So it's not your dad. That's the thing you were scared of on Tuesday.

WES: It's not my dad. Not yet, and maybe not ever, if I do the boring stuff. I sat in the truck in the parking lot, and I said out loud, "It's not Dad." To nobody. Then here's the funny part. Guess where the physical therapy is. Same clinic as my mom. Same Tuesday slot. So starting next week I drive her, and we both do our exercises side by side on those little tables, and she counts my reps out loud. "Eight. You're cheating. Eight."

JONATHON: Your mom is your physical therapist now. That's the best thing I've heard all year.

WES: My unpaid physical therapist, and tougher than the paid one. Then the wife question. I asked her, "What do you carry around here that's really mine?" She didn't even think. "Your family's birthdays." Every birthday on my side. My mom, both brothers, their wives, their kids. For twenty-seven years she's bought the cards, signed my name, mailed them, and reminded me to call.

JONATHON: She signs your name? Like, actually writes "Wes" on the card in your handwriting?

WES: Better than I do. And I never once thought about it. My family always knew I remembered. I did not remember. She remembered, and I got the credit. That's a duffel I've been handing her for twenty-seven years. So Wednesday night, after the knee thing, she sat down with me and we put every birthday in my phone, with a reminder a week before. My little brother's is March eleventh. I'm buying the card myself.

JONATHON: And signing it yourself? Because he's going to think somebody else wrote it.

WES: He's going to think it's a ransom note. And part two, what I'm carrying that isn't mine. My oldest son's job. He's twenty-one, he's deciding whether to stay with his company once he tops out, and I've been carrying that decision for him. Sending him articles. Asking every time he calls. So I wrote it down. "His job. His." That's all so far.

## SEGMENT 2 · Jonathon checks the box

WES: Now you. Wednesday after bath.

JONATHON: Dani and I sat at the kitchen table and decided who raises the kids. Two hours. There was crying, both of us. It's a terrible conversation. You say out loud, "If we both died," and then you have to keep talking. We picked Dani's parents for now, because the kids know them and they're close. My sister's the backup. And we'll look at it again in five years, because her parents are in their seventies, and that's the honest thing. Then Dani said, "Thank you for bringing it up."

WES: That's the whole thing. That's what she'd been waiting three years for.

JONATHON: Three years of her bringing it up, and all I did was bring it up once. Then the lawyer, Thursday at four-thirty. Most boring hour of my life, and I loved it. We sign on the eighteenth. Then Dani showed me the spreadsheet. And she'd bought yellow sticky notes, on purpose, because she heard you say it. She listens to this now. The mortgage comes out on the third. I know the password. I'm not saying it here.

WES: Please don't. And the wife question?

JONATHON: She said, "The garage." Then, "No, wait. The garage is mine. I'm the one who wanted the shelves." And she thought a second, and she said, "Your mom." My mom calls on Sundays. I miss it, because I'm doing something with the kids, or I see it and think, I'll call her later. And Dani calls her back. Every week, for years, because she doesn't want my mom to feel forgotten.

WES: And that one's yours. That's the duffel that's been sitting by your door.

JONATHON: That one's mine. And here's what messed with me. Tuesday I said my mom's happiness isn't mine, and that's true. But calling her back is mine. I'd been so busy carrying the part that isn't, I set down the part that is.

WES: You carried her feelings and dropped her phone calls. So what's the step? Because that's a left-column item if I ever heard one.

JONATHON: Sunday, when she calls, I pick up. And if I miss it, I call back before supper, not Dani. It's in my phone for two o'clock, with an alarm, because I know me. And I told Dani she's off Sunday duty. She said, "We'll see," which, coming from her, is fair. I've earned a "we'll see." And the bed. Ruby made it three out of four days. Thursday she forgot, and I stood in the doorway, looked at it, and did not make it. I felt like a bad dad, which is how I know it's working.

WES: And the single check?

JONATHON: My nephew did the emergency contact one. He looked it up at work. His emergency contact was his ex-girlfriend. They broke up a year ago. So he changed it to his roommate, then made his roommate check his, and the roommate's was his mom in Ohio, with a number that's been disconnected. Two single guys in an apartment, and nobody could reach anybody. It's fixed. He also texted me his first budget. It has one category, called "food and stuff."

WES: That's a start. One category is one more than he had last week.

## SEGMENT 3 · From the Table

WES: We're about halfway. Here's the question. "What if I'm carrying stuff that isn't mine?" First, how would you even know? I watch for three signs. One, resentment. If you're carrying something and you're quietly mad about it all the time, some part of you probably knows it isn't yours.

JONATHON: That's the holidays at my house. I'm mad by the second week of November every single year, and I never knew why.

WES: Two, exhaustion with no progress. You carry it every day, and it never gets lighter for anybody. Three, control. You're carrying it because if you set it down, somebody else will do it differently. My son's job. If I set it down, he might choose something I wouldn't.

JONATHON: And he might. He's twenty-one. Honestly, didn't you choose wrong at twenty-one?

WES: I did, and I'm doing fine, mostly. So once you know it's not yours, you can't just drop it on the floor. Every weight that isn't yours belongs to somebody. You hand it back to the person it belongs to, or you hand it to God. Those are the only two places it goes.

JONATHON: Can I read the verse for that one? Because I've had it on a sticky note on my monitor since my first year in sales. Peter's writing to people who are getting pushed out of their jobs and families over their faith. "Cast all your anxiety on him because he cares for you." Cast it. Like a heavy bag off your shoulder.

WES: He doesn't say stop worrying. He says throw it on him. My son's job, part of it I can hand back with a sentence. "That's your call. I trust you. If you want to talk it through, I'm here." And then I don't send him any articles. The articles are me taking it back. And part of it is just me being scared for my kid. I can't hand that part to him. That part goes up.

[BOARD: Psalm 55:22 (NIV) · "Cast your cares on the Lord and he will sustain you; he will never let the righteous be shaken."]

JONATHON: Okay, but I'll push on the "hand it up" part. It can sound like a spiritual way of not caring. Like, "I gave it to God," and then you just stop showing up for people.

WES: That's fair, and it happens. Handing it up isn't walking away. You still call your mom. You still love your son. You just quit trying to carry what only God can carry. If handing it to God makes you show up less for the people in your duffel, you didn't hand it up. You just dropped it.

## SEGMENT 4 · Tool on the Table

WES: We've got about four minutes. The tool is a two-column list. Draw a line down the middle of a legal pad. Left side, "Mine." Right side, "Not mine." Sit down for ten minutes. Use the chicken timer if you have to. Write everything you're carrying right now, everything keeping you up, and put each one in a column. Can't tell which? Ask Tuesday's question. If I carry it, does it get lighter for somebody?

JONATHON: And then what do you do with the columns? Because I can make a list and ignore it forever.

WES: On the right side, next to every item, write a name. Whose is it? Some of them, the name is God's. Then you say the sentence to that person, or you pray it. On the left side, next to every item, write one next step. Not the whole thing. One step. Birthdays in my phone was one step.

JONATHON: I did mine on the "Family Devotions" whiteboard, which belongs to Theo now. I erased a dinosaur. On the right I wrote "Mom's Christmas," and the names next to it were "Mom" and "God." On the left I wrote "call Mom back," and I drew a phone. Then Theo added a dinosaur on top of the phone. So now it's a dinosaur on a phone, and honestly, I'm leaving it.

WES: And do it with your one man if you can. Read him the right side, because that's the stuff you'll try to pick back up next week, and he'll notice. Divorced dads, half your right column is probably what happens at her house. Write her name on it and pray it. You can't carry it from across town.

JONATHON: And live-alone guys, this one's built for you. Nobody's going to notice what you're carrying unless you write it down and show somebody. At my house, Dani sees me pacing. At your place, nobody's watching but the dog.

WES: That's right. If you live alone, the list is how somebody else gets to see the load. Take a picture of it and text it to your one man. He doesn't have to fix anything. He just has to know what's on the right side, so when you start picking it back up in a couple of weeks, somebody says, "Hey, isn't that the thing you gave back?"

[BOARD: Matthew 11:30 (NIV) · "For my yoke is easy and my burden is light."]
