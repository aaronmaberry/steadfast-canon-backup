**Arc.** *Hook:* for two years Jonathon took the long way home on purpose to miss bath time, and paid a sitter while he sat in traffic. *Tension:* who these two men are, told by each other and corrected. Most men get measured by highlight days, but a man gets made on a Tuesday. Jonathon's 1604 years and Wes's recliner years. Paul and Jesus raise the bar: you put childhood down, and the very little is the test. Jonathon pushes back on rest, on the big days (he stays unconvinced), on the single man and the man who's great at work. *Turn:* the nine-year-old on the floor with her plate. *Resolution:* what each man did this week, what the show is, the first challenge and the close.

JONATHON: For two years I drove home the long way on purpose. Loop 1604 at six-fifteen, which is a parking lot, so I'd miss bath time.

WES: And he paid a sitter by the hour while he sat in it.

JONATHON: Until one night my wife looks at me and says, "Since when do you like 1604?"

## SEGMENT 1 · Who's at the table

WES: It's the first one, so let's take the next few minutes on who you're sitting with. Jonathon, you introduce me, because I'm bad at it, and I'll fix what you get wrong.

JONATHON: Okay. This is Wes Hart. He's fifty-two. He lives on a few acres out in the Hill Country, west of San Antonio, in a town he will not let me say out loud. Married since 2000. Three kids. A daughter who's twenty-five, married, teaches second grade. A son who's twenty-one and finishing an electrical apprenticeship. And a sixteen-year-old who just got his license, which explains the look on Wes's face most days.

WES: He drives like he's late. He's never late. He's got nowhere to be. I rode with him Saturday and held the handle above the door the whole way to the feed store. I always wondered what that handle was for. Now I know.

JONATHON: And for a living, Wes does what, exactly? I've known this man seven years.

WES: I work. Same as you. I get up early, I come home tired, and my truck has too many miles on it.

JONATHON: That's all you get. But here's the part that matters. For twenty-some years Wes has sat across tables from men. Kitchen tables, church fellowship halls, deer camp. And a while back he wrote down what he'd learned at all of them, and that's his book. That's my version. Fix it.

WES: You were kind. You left out that I wasn't any good at this for a long time. I grew up on the home place, next to my granddad's land. My dad was steady and quiet. He was at every game I ever played and almost never said what he felt. He said "I'm proud of you" exactly once, from a hospital bed, when I was forty-three. That's not a bad-dad story. It's a good man who never said it out loud, and I've sat with a lot of men carrying that one.

JONATHON: And then your thirties. The part your wife has a name for.

WES: The fog years. Decent job, coached Little League, third row at church every Sunday, and gone. Gone in my own living room. My wife saw it before I did, and she was patient a lot longer than I deserved. Then at thirty-six an old lineman at a men's breakfast looked at me and asked, "What are you waiting on?" I didn't have an answer. He met me every other Thursday for six years until I did.

JONATHON: That's Mr. Abernathy, and you'll hear plenty about him. Okay. Now do me, and be nice about it.

## SEGMENT 2 · Who's across from him

WES: I'm always nice. This is Jonathon. He's thirty-six. He grew up in Pasadena, Texas, east of Houston, refinery country. Flat, and it smells like work. He lives on the north side of San Antonio now, in a subdivision with an HOA that has opinions about his trash cans. He's a regional sales manager for a company that sells commercial air conditioning.

JONATHON: Which, in Texas, is the easiest pitch in the world. "Would you like to not die in August?" I close that every day.

WES: Married ten years to Dani, a labor and delivery nurse who works nights, three a week. Ruby's seven and runs the house. Theo's four and runs into things. He pitched at a small college till his shoulder went, and now he coaches Ruby's coach-pitch team.

JONATHON: Badly. We went oh-and-six last fall. At the end of the season Ruby picked out this watch for me, and it beeps every hour on the hour. It has beeped in church. I can't turn it off. Okay, here's the rest. My dad left when I was nine. A Saturday in October. I remember his truck had a dent in the tailgate, and I watched the dent go down the street. Not his face. The dent.

WES: And he's still around.

JONATHON: He's alive, in Lake Charles. He calls about twice a year. I'll say it now: I have not figured that out, and I'm going to be the guy on this show who didn't heal on schedule.

WES: That's allowed here. And your mom carried the rest.

JONATHON: Two jobs, and by eleven she was calling me the man of the house. She meant it as a compliment. Then my twenties. Good at sales, lived for the leaderboard, and drank too much on weeknights to come down from it. No wreck, no arrest. Just a little too much every night, and I called it unwinding.

WES: When did that turn?

JONATHON: Twenty-nine. Dani was pregnant with Ruby, and she sat across from me at our kitchen table, very calm, which was the scary part, and said, "I'm not raising two kids." A month later a customer invited me to a men's breakfast, and I went because I couldn't think of an excuse fast enough.

WES: And sat in the parking lot.

JONATHON: Eleven minutes. Walked in, one open seat, across from this guy. Three months later I texted him, and that's still the scariest text I've ever sent a man.

WES: "Hey, this is Jonathon from the breakfast, the HVAC guy, no pressure."

JONATHON: And "no pressure" means a whole lot of pressure. That was seven years ago. I came to faith that year, and I haven't had a weeknight drink in six years. I'm here for the guy in that parking lot, watching the clock on the dash. And if Wes says something that sounds great at a breakfast and wouldn't last five minutes in my house at six-fifteen, I'm going to say so.

WES: He will. He's been doing it to me most Thursdays for seven years, and he's usually right.

## SEGMENT 3 · A quiet Tuesday

WES: Which gets us to Tuesday. Most men get measured by the highlight days. The wedding, the promotion, the trip, the night you were the guy. Those days matter. But that's not where a man gets made. He gets made on the day nobody takes a picture of. So tell them 1604 properly, because you gave them the short version.

JONATHON: Okay. Ruby was four, Theo was one, and Dani had just gone back to nights. Six o'clock at our house is supper, two baths, somebody screaming, and the pajama fight. And I'm coming off a day of being great at the room. Then I'd get near my exit and feel it in my chest. So one night I missed the exit on purpose. Got on 1604 at six-fifteen. When I walked in, baths were done, both kids in pajamas, and I got the good part. The story, the hug, the little prayer.

WES: You got the highlight and skipped the Tuesday.

JONATHON: For two years. Paying a sitter by the hour so I could sit in traffic on purpose. Dani's a nurse. She deals in facts. She asked since when I liked 1604, I said, "It's not that bad," and she said, "Okay." That's all. That "okay" followed me around for a week. I'd have rather had the fight.

WES: I got one of those once. Here's where I have to tell on myself. In the fog years I did all the big things. Built a deck. Coached. Took the family to the coast every summer, planned it for months, and the pictures are great pictures. Then on a normal Tuesday I'd come home and eat supper in the recliner with the news on, fifteen feet from my family at the table. For a while my wife would call me over. "Come sit with us." "In a minute." And then one night I noticed she'd stopped asking. That's when I should've been scared. Not while she was asking. When she stopped.

JONATHON: Okay, so this is the thing you promised. What got you up?

WES: Nothing heroic. My daughter was about nine. One night she picked up her plate, walked over, and sat down on the floor next to my recliner. Because that's where her dad was. She wasn't making a point. She just wanted to eat supper with her dad. And I looked down at her eating off her lap, and I thought, what am I doing? What am I waiting on? About a year later an old lineman asked me that exact question. I'd already heard it. From her. I just hadn't answered it.

[BOARD: Proverbs 20:6 (NIV) · "Many claim to have unfailing love, but a faithful person who can find?"]

JONATHON: That one's going to sit with me, because I've got a seven-year-old who would absolutely do that. She'd bring a napkin and tell me I was sitting wrong.

WES: If you've got a chair you eat in alone, look at it tonight. Somebody in your house has noticed where it is.

## SEGMENT 4 · The Word

WES: Let's get the Bible open. First Corinthians thirteen, the chapter everybody reads at weddings. Right in the middle, Paul says this. "When I was a child, I talked like a child, I thought like a child, I reasoned like a child. When I became a man, I put the ways of childhood behind me."

JONATHON: We had that at our wedding. I don't think I heard a single word of it. I was trying not to lock my knees.

WES: Two sentences of context. Paul's writing to a church in Corinth that had everything, gifts and talent and big moments, and they were acting like kids with it. Everybody chasing the impressive stuff, nobody loving anybody on a regular day. And look at the verb. "I put the ways of childhood behind me." He did something. You don't turn thirty and wake up a man. You set something down.

JONATHON: So what's the something? Because I want this to be concrete for the guy driving right now.

WES: For me it was the recliner. That's where I went to be a kid on a Tuesday. Nobody needs anything, the TV does the talking, I don't have to show up. That was me reasoning like a child at forty years old. And Jesus says something in Luke sixteen that's the whole Tuesday idea in one line. Jonathon, read it.

JONATHON: "Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with very little will also be dishonest with much." Okay, that's uncomfortable, because I'd have told you I was trustworthy with much. Big accounts, big numbers. I just wasn't trustworthy with a bath.

WES: That's it. Tuesday is the very little. Six-fifteen, supper, the pajama fight. You don't get to be one man on the trip to the coast and a different man in the recliner. It's the same man. The trip just has better lighting.

[BOARD: Psalm 90:12 (NIV) · "Teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom."]

JONATHON: I have a lot of trip pictures, Wes. I'm going to go home and look at them differently now, and I'm a little mad about it.

## SEGMENT 5 · Pushback

WES: We're about halfway. Jonathon, you've been sitting on something. Go ahead.

JONATHON: Here's my problem. Some Tuesdays I'm just tired. Dani's on nights, I've had the kids since five, Theo's been sticky since five-oh-two. Is it a sin to sit in the car for ten minutes before I go in? Because some days the car is the only quiet I get, and I'm not giving it up without a fight.

WES: No. Ten minutes in the car isn't a sin. Here's the test I use. Rest brings you back to your people, and it brings you back better. Hiding makes sure you don't come back until the hard part's over. Ten minutes in the driveway with the engine off, praying, breathing, then you go in and do bath. That's rest. Twenty minutes on 1604 so somebody else does bath is the other thing.

[BOARD: Rest walks back in the door. Hiding waits for someone else to finish.]

JONATHON: Okay. I'll buy that. Second one, and you're not going to like it. I think you undersell the big days. You keep saying the trip just has better lighting, like it doesn't count. Ruby still talks about the time we went fishing at Port Aransas. We caught one fish. A hardhead catfish. It stung me. She brings it up every week like it was the moon landing.

WES: She should. I'm not saying throw out the big days. My kids remember the coast. But she remembers Port Aransas because you were there on a hundred Tuesdays around it. If you'd been gone on all the Tuesdays, that trip would be a nice picture of a guy she doesn't know very well.

JONATHON: That's not fair, because I can't argue with it, and I still think you're wrong. Kids build their whole idea of their dad out of three or four big days. I'm putting that on the record.

WES: We'll come back to it. Who else is listening that we haven't talked to?

JONATHON: The twenty-two-year-old in an apartment with two roommates. No wife, no kids, nobody's bath to skip. What's his Tuesday?

WES: His Tuesday is his whole life right now, not practice for some other one. Is he the guy who shows up when a buddy's moving, or the one who's suddenly busy? Does he work like it matters when the boss is gone? Does his money go anywhere on purpose? Is there one man who knows what's really going on with him? Whether there's ever a wedding or not, the man he is on Tuesday is the man he is.

JONATHON: Last one, and it's me. What about the guy who's great at work? My problem was never lazy. At work every day's a Saturday. I hit the number, my name goes on the board, people need me. Then I drive home, and nobody at my house cares about the board. Ruby doesn't know what a quarter is. Theo wants me to be a dinosaur, usually the one that gets eaten.

WES: That man's the hardest one to reach, and I say it with love, because I've been him in a different shirt. He's not checked out. He's spent. He gave the best of himself to the people who sign his check, and the people with his last name get what's left. It's still the recliner. It's just a nicer recliner with a company logo on it.

JONATHON: Okay. That one's going to bother me on the drive home. Which is probably the point, and I don't love it.

## SEGMENT 6 · What we actually did

WES: We've got about six minutes. So, what each of us actually did this week. Mine first. I put my phone in the console of my truck before supper. Three nights out of five. The reason it wasn't five is Tuesday, of all days, my youngest had a question about his car insurance, I took the phone to the table to look it up, and twenty minutes later I was watching a man restore a tractor.

JONATHON: So you brought the recliner to the table. It's a recliner you carry around in your pocket. I've got one too. Mine has a leaderboard on it. Was it at least a good tractor?

WES: It was a very good tractor. Your turn.

JONATHON: I drove the short way home every night Dani worked. Three nights. Monday was great. Tuesday was fine. Wednesday, Theo flooded the bathroom. I turned around to grab a towel, turned back, and there's water going under the door into the hallway, and he's sitting in the tub smiling at me like he invented something. And for one second I thought, this is exactly why I took 1604. Then Ruby comes to the door, looks at the water, and says, "Daddy, it's okay. It's just water." The seven-year-old handled it better than me.

WES: She's right, though.

JONATHON: She's right. And if I'd been on 1604, the sitter would've texted me a picture and I'd have laughed at the picture. Instead I was standing in it in my socks. That was the Tuesday. I'd rather be in it.

## SEGMENT 7 · What this is

WES: Before the challenge, let me say once what all this is, so you never have to wonder. It started with a book I wrote, Steadfast Men. Thirty-five short chapters in five parts, from the man you were built to be to the long road. Every chapter ends with something to do that week. Then there's a video training, twenty-four sessions, me teaching a room of men. You can do it alone on your phone at lunch, or a few guys can run it on a weeknight.

WES: And this show is the breakfast after the class. Tuesdays are the long one, one topic, one passage, and he pushes back. Fridays are Field Notes, shorter, where we tell you whether we did the challenge.

JONATHON: And I'm going to fail on air, and I've made my peace with it. If you're not sure about God at all, you're welcome here too. I was Easter-and-Christmas until twenty-nine.

---

## SEGMENT 8 · This week's challenge

WES: So here's your challenge. Two parts, small on purpose. One meal this week with no phone. Not face-down on the table. In another room or in the truck. And at that meal, ask one question: "What's Tuesday like for you?" Then listen. Don't fix anything.

JONATHON: If you're single, eat one meal with somebody, roommate, buddy, your folks, phone in the other room, and ask him the same thing. If you live alone, invite somebody to supper Thursday. And if nobody can come, eat at the table, not in front of a screen, and pray out loud before you eat. That counts.

WES: If you're divorced and you've got your kids this week, that's your meal, and that's your question. Part two: text one man, "How's your Tuesday?" That's the whole text. You don't need a reason.

## CLOSE (word for word)

WES: Here's what I want you to carry out of here. You don't become a man someday. You put something down on a regular Tuesday.

JONATHON: And whatever your 1604 is, take the short way home. Even if there's water in the hallway.

WES: Friday we'll tell you whether we did it, and what my oldest texted back. Until then: one meal, no phone. One text to one man.

JONATHON: Then tell us what your Tuesday looks like at walksteadfast.com, and find us everywhere at @steadfastwalk.
