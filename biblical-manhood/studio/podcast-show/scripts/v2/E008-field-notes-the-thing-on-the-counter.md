**Arc.** *Hook:* Jonathon's 7:01 reminder text got back one letter, "K," because Wes was already on the phone with his brother. *Tension:* the report. "Well." "I know." "Mostly right." A brother who didn't want an apology speech, just a man at Mom's doctor appointment. A wife who's been waiting on Wes's knees, and the Tuesday drives Wes is finally taking over. Jonathon's overdue review ("I've been waiting for somebody to say it"), a night on the calendar next to Pancake, and a bin of pajamas. *Turn:* how do you stop waiting when you're genuinely tired? The brisket test, Matthew 11:28, and two tired people in one kitchen. *Resolution:* the ten-minute start, a 1999 chicken timer, and a seven-year-old's watch that finally has a job.

## SEGMENT 1 · Wes checks the box

JONATHON: Okay, the whole call, from six fifty-nine, because I've been waiting since Wednesday.

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on it. I was in the truck out by the barn, because I didn't want an audience. I called a minute early so I wouldn't lose my nerve. He picked up, and the first thing he said was "Well."

JONATHON: That's the whole greeting? Just "Well"? I'd have hung up and tried again.

WES: It runs in the family. My dad said it. My brother says it. I say it. So I said, "I've been meaning to call since Thanksgiving." He said, "I know." And then I said the thing I'd waited two months to say. "What you said at Mom's was mostly right."

JONATHON: "Mostly." You couldn't give him the whole thing, could you?

WES: I gave him what was true. He was quiet long enough I checked the screen to see if the call dropped. Then he said, "I didn't need you to say I was right. I need you at Mom's doctor appointment on the ninth." He didn't want an apology speech. He wanted a man in the room. So I'm going. February ninth. I'm taking the day off.

JONATHON: And how was it after that? Because I'd love to hear it was all hugs, but I know your family.

WES: It's not fixed. He was still a little cold. We talked twenty minutes, mostly Mom's hip and his cattle. In my family, twenty minutes is a filibuster. It's not fixed. It's started. And started is a lot more than a cease-fire.

[BOARD: Twenty minutes of cattle and a hip still counts.]

JONATHON: And the wife question. You said you braced for it, so what did she actually say?

WES: I asked her, "Is there something you've been waiting on me for?" I figured it'd be a big marriage thing. She said, "Your knees." I've been limping since October. She's asked me to see somebody about five times, and I keep saying it's fine, it's age, I'll get to it.

JONATHON: "I'll get to it." There it is again, the old sentence on the front of the card.

WES: There it is. So I called Thursday morning. Appointment's February third. And she said, "I'm not asking because of your knees. I'm asking because I want you around when we're old." I'd been thinking about a knee. She'd been thinking about twenty years. And then I asked her for one more thing. Starting Tuesday, I'm taking Mom to physical therapy. My brother's been carrying the doctors, and my wife's been carrying Tuesdays since August. I'm done leaving early to beat the traffic. She said she'll miss the drive, so she's riding along for the first one.

JONATHON: That's a big one. And the doctor thing, I'd bet a lot of men listening are sitting on an appointment right now.

WES: Make the appointment. Today. Before the end of this.

## SEGMENT 2 · Jonathon checks the box

WES: Now you. The review.

JONATHON: Thursday. The rep whose review I'd moved three times. I told him the truth. Six months, here are the numbers, here's what has to change, here's what I'll do to help. I was braced for him to be mad. He just sat there, and then he said, "Thank you. I knew. I've been waiting for somebody to say it."

WES: He was waiting too. What did that do to you?

JONATHON: Honestly, it flattened me a little. He knew he was underwater, and nobody would say it, so he couldn't fix it. My waiting was keeping him stuck. I thought I was being nice. I was the cease-fire. Six months of being liked, and he paid for every one of them.

WES: And Dani? The conversation from October?

JONATHON: I said, "We never finished that. Can we pick a night?" She looked at me like you did when I said I'd read your book. Suspicious. Then she said yes. Sunday, February seventh, after the kids are down. It's on the kitchen calendar, next to Pancake. Big circle in August for the dog, little circle on the seventh for us.

WES: And then you asked her the question.

JONATHON: I asked her, "Is there something you've been waiting on me for?" And she said, "Since I redid the kids' rooms at Christmas, you text me at bedtime every shift asking where things are. I've been waiting for you to learn the new rooms." Which, fair. She moved every drawer over Christmas break. Theo's pajamas are in a bin now. A bin, Wes.

WES: So did you learn the rooms, or did she get a text Wednesday night?

JONATHON: Wednesday night, zero texts. I found the bin. Took me twenty minutes and one wrong closet, but I found it. And here's the thing. It wasn't really about the pajamas. Every one of those texts was her at work, between patients, being told she's still on duty at home. So the bin was the small thing. Leaving her alone on her shift was the big one.

WES: That's a good catch. And the single check?

JONATHON: My nephew asked his roommate, "What have I been putting off that you've noticed?" And the roommate didn't even look up from the TV. "The dentist. You've been chewing on the left side since Halloween." He made the appointment and sent me a screenshot of the confirmation. That's how I know it's real with him.

## SEGMENT 3 · From the Table

WES: We're about halfway. Here's the question, and I think it's the most honest one about Tuesday's challenge. "How do I stop waiting when I'm genuinely tired?"

JONATHON: That's my house most nights. Dani's coming off nights, I've had the kids and a full workday, and at seven-thirty every list in the world sounds impossible.

WES: And I want to be careful, because I won't tell a tired man to just try harder. Tired is real. It's not a character flaw. So first, figure out which tired you are. There's the tired where your body really needs to stop, and the tired where you're avoiding something and calling it tired. At seven p.m. they feel exactly the same. Here's my test. If somebody called and said there was free brisket at the end of the street, would you get off the couch?

JONATHON: Yes. Immediately. I'd be in the truck before he finished the sentence. And I've failed this test already, honestly. Tuesday night Ruby asked me to read her one more chapter, and I said, "Daddy's too tired, baby." Ten minutes later my buddy texts that the Spurs game went to overtime, and I'm up off that couch and in the living room like somebody fired a starter pistol.

WES: So what did you do with that?

JONATHON: I went back in. She was still awake. I read the chapter, and the overtime was over by the time I came out. We lost, for the record. But I wasn't tired. I was done being a dad for the day and called it tired.

WES: Then you're not that tired. You're avoiding. If you wouldn't get up for free brisket, you're actually tired, and you need rest, and that's not a sin. God made you to sleep.

[BOARD: Psalm 127:2 (NIV) · "for he grants sleep to those he loves."]

WES: And for the bone-tired kind, there's something Jesus said to tired people. He's talking to folks worn down by religious rules piled on them, and he turns to the crowd. You're the tired one. Read it.

JONATHON: "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest." I've heard that on a hundred coffee mugs, and I've always read it as a nice thought. Not as an instruction.

WES: It's an instruction. He didn't say, come to me once you've finished your list. He said, come tired. The first thing a tired man does isn't the gutter. It's go to God tired. "I'm worn out, there's a thing I've been putting off, and I don't have it in me." That's a real prayer.

JONATHON: Okay, but here's the version that's actually my house. It's not one tired guy. It's two tired people. There's a pile of dishes. Who goes first?

WES: You do.

JONATHON: That was fast. Even when I'm more tired than she is? Because I'll be honest, most nights I think I'm more tired.

WES: Can you actually measure who's more tired? Every marriage I've sat with, both people think they're the tired one. Stop measuring. Move first. When you move first, she usually gets up too. When you both sit there waiting, the dishes win.

JONATHON: In my house the dishes are undefeated. And if she's really more tired, like she's coming off a twelve-hour shift and delivered three babies?

WES: Then you send her to bed and do it, and you don't mention it in the morning.

JONATHON: I'd absolutely mention it. I'd mention it at breakfast and probably again at lunch.

WES: I know you would. That's the pay thing. Don't mention it. Let God see it.

[BOARD: Galatians 6:9 (NIV) · "Let us not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up."]

## SEGMENT 4 · Tool on the Table

WES: We've got about four minutes. The tool is a timer. This one's my wife's chicken timer. She's had it since 1999. It's the most reliable thing in our house. The tool is the ten-minute start. Pick the thing you've been waiting on. Set a timer for ten minutes, and for ten minutes you do that thing and nothing else. Phone in the basket. When it dings, you're allowed to stop. No guilt. Ten minutes is a win.

JONATHON: But you don't stop. That's the trick, right? Nobody stops at ten minutes.

WES: Most of the time you don't, because the hard part of almost anything is the first ten minutes. But you have to really mean it when you say you can stop, or your brain knows it's a trick. And if it's not a ten-minute job, like a phone call, the ten minutes is getting ready. Sit in the truck, write down the two things you want to say, and when the chicken dings, you dial.

JONATHON: My version is the watch. Ruby's watch beeps every hour on the hour, and I still haven't figured out how to turn it off. So now I use it. When it beeps at seven, I start the thing, whatever it is. Bath, dishes, the email I've been avoiding. The watch finally has a job, and Ruby's thrilled that it's helping. Thursday it beeped at seven, and I'd been avoiding the garage since before Christmas. Ten minutes. I ended up out there an hour, and I found the box of Theo's baby stuff Dani's been asking about since November.

WES: And if you're the kind of tired where even ten minutes isn't there, run the brisket test. If you fail it, go to bed. Tomorrow you'll have ten minutes. So here's your weekend. One thing you've been putting off, ten minutes, and a timer. And if it's a doctor, the ten minutes is the phone call.
