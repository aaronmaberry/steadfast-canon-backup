**Arc.** *Hook:* Theo, in the bath, asks "Daddy, what's a man?" and Jonathon has no answer but "a big boy." *Tension:* nobody hands a man a sentence for what a man is, so he lives one he picked up by accident. Wes's was "pay the bills and don't make trouble," written on a diner receipt for an old man who kept thirty-some of them. Jonathon writes his on air: "win the room." *Turn:* Jonathon pushes back that manhood talk is too loud already. Wes answers with Genesis 1:27: you were made to show something. *Resolution:* each host says a better sentence out loud to one of his sons, and gets a very small answer back. The challenge: write the sentence you've actually been living.

WES: What did he say?

JONATHON: He looked at me and said, "But you're not big. You're medium." I'm five-ten. He's right.

## SEGMENT 1 · The bathtub

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on that bathtub, because I don't think "a big boy" came out of nowhere. What happened earlier that day?

JONATHON: That's the part I didn't want to tell. Earlier that afternoon Theo fell off his bike in the driveway. Skinned his knee, not bad, and he's crying the way a four-year-old cries, like the world ended. And I heard myself say, "Come on, buddy. Be a man about it." It just came right out of my mouth.

WES: Where'd you get that one? Because nobody makes that up on the spot.

JONATHON: My mom's boyfriend said it to me when I was ten. I'd hurt my wrist, and he said it standing in the kitchen doorway with a beer. I swore I'd never say it to a kid of mine. Twenty-six years later I'm in my own driveway, saying it word for word. So that night in the bath, when Theo asks what a man is, I panicked, because I'd already told him once that day. A man doesn't cry about his knee.

WES: So the first answer he got was the bad one, and the second one was "a big boy." And that's most of us. We got one or two sentences by accident, from whoever was standing in the doorway, and we never got a better one.

JONATHON: But I'm a grown man. I've been a Christian for seven years. You'd think I could come up with one decent sentence for a four-year-old. I sell air conditioners for a living. Talking is the whole job.

WES: Talking's not the problem. Nobody handed you the sentence. Think about it. Who actually sat you down and told you what a man is? Not a list of don'ts. A sentence you could carry.

JONATHON: Honestly? Nobody. My dad left when I was nine. My mom worked doubles. I got football coaches and the boyfriend and whatever was on TV. And I got a lot of rules, honestly, but nobody ever said it straight out.

WES: That's the thing I want every man listening to hear today. The gap you feel is real. You're not crazy. You're walking around with a job description nobody ever wrote down, and then getting graded on it.

## SEGMENT 2 · The receipt

JONATHON: Okay, so what about you? Because you've had a lot longer than me to come up with something, Wes. Who gave you yours?

WES: Nobody gave it to me either. I found it on a receipt. About a year into meeting with Mr. Abernathy, he slid a diner receipt across the table, turned it over, and handed me a pen. And he said, "Write down what you think a man is. Not what you'd say at church. What you've actually been doing."

JONATHON: That's a mean question to ask a man before he's finished his eggs. What did you write?

WES: I sat there a long time, and I wrote, "Pay the bills and don't make trouble." And I slid it back. He read it, and he nodded, and he said, "Well, that's honest." That's all he said.

JONATHON: That's it? No sermon? Because I'd want to defend it. Paying the bills is good. I've had years where paying the bills was the whole victory.

WES: It is good. That's what makes it so slippery. Nothing on that receipt is wrong. I paid every bill for fifteen years, and I didn't make trouble. I also didn't make much of anything else. My wife didn't have a partner. My kids had a man in a recliner. I was keeping the peace in a house where nobody was at peace.

JONATHON: So it's not a bad sentence, it's a small one. It's a sentence for a tenant, not a husband.

WES: That's exactly it. Then he said, "Next time you can write a better one." And I didn't, for a while. But it was the first time I'd ever looked at my own sentence. I'd been living it fifteen years. Then, years later, when he passed, his daughter gave me his Bible. And when I opened it, there were receipts tucked in it. Thirty-some receipts. Thirty-some men, every one with a bad sentence on it, in their own handwriting. He kept every one.

JONATHON: Okay, that gets me. He wasn't collecting them to embarrass anybody. He was praying over them, wasn't he?

WES: I believe he was. Mine was in there, from the week we met. Every one of those men went home with a better sentence eventually. That's the whole point. You can't trade a sentence you've never looked at.

JONATHON: Can I ask where yours came from, though? Because "pay the bills and don't make trouble" sounds like somebody's dad.

WES: It was my dad's, near as I can tell, though he never said it. He fed cattle every morning, worked every day, paid every bill, and never raised his voice. I don't remember him ever telling me what a man was. He just lived that sentence in front of me for eighteen years, and I copied it like a kid copies handwriting. He told me he was proud of me one time, when I was forty-three and he was in a hospital bed. I'm not angry at him. He was living the sentence he caught from his dad. But I'd like it to stop with me.

## SEGMENT 3 · Win the room

WES: So here's a receipt from my truck. Feed store, Saturday. Two bags of deer corn and a salt block. Turn it over. You get the pen.

JONATHON: You're doing it to me right now, on purpose. Okay. Give me a second. I'm actually going to write the true one.

WES: Take your time.

JONATHON: "Win the room." That's mine. I wrote it fast, which tells me it's true. Win the room. Every customer, every meeting, every dinner party. I walk in, I read everybody, and I make sure everybody likes me before I leave.

WES: Where do you think you got that? Because it sounds like a sales trainer, but I don't think that's where it started.

JONATHON: I've never said this part out loud. I think I got it the year my dad left. Because if he could leave, anybody could leave. And the way you make sure people don't leave is you make them like you. Every room, every time. I was nine, and I turned into the funniest kid in fourth grade.

WES: And it works, is the problem. It's made you a good salesman. Probably a good friend in a lot of ways. Where does it cost you?

JONATHON: At home. Because Dani's not a room I need to win. When she's tired and upset, I go into sales mode. I make a joke, I make a plan, I get the mood back up, and she can tell. She told me once, "I don't need you to close me." And then my pitching coach in college, he had one sentence he said about a hundred times a season. "Never let them see you hurt." So between the two of them, I got "win the room, and never let them see you hurt."

WES: Give me a Tuesday where that sentence ran the show.

JONATHON: Last spring I had a dinner with a big customer, a hospital system, the kind of account that makes your whole year. Halfway through, Dani texts me that Theo's running a fever of a hundred and three, and she's got to leave for a shift in an hour. And I texted back, "Twenty minutes." And I stayed for an hour and ten, because the room wasn't won yet. I closed it. Dani called her mom. And I drove home feeling like a hero for about four miles.

WES: And then what happened at mile five?

JONATHON: I pictured her standing in the kitchen in scrubs holding a hot four-year-old, watching the clock. I'd won the room I was in and lost the one I was supposed to be in.

WES: That's a full sentence. And look at what it does to a man on a Tuesday. He can never lose a room and he can never limp. So he can't be honest at home and he can't ask for help at work.

JONATHON: I just heard myself, and that's a lot of pressure to carry around in a polo shirt. But it's also the reason I sit here and talk easy. I don't want to throw out the whole thing.

WES: You don't have to throw out the gift. A man who can read a room is a gift to his church and his family. You just can't let the gift be the sentence. You read the room so you can serve the people in it, not so they'll keep you.

## SEGMENT 4 · Pushback

JONATHON: Okay, I've got to push back on something, because you keep talking like the problem is men are too quiet. Every time I open my phone, somebody's yelling about manhood. Alpha this, provider that, get up at four and take ice baths. Manhood talk isn't quiet. It's the loudest thing on the internet.

WES: That's fair. You're right that it's loud. But think about where all that yelling lands. Most of it is a costume. It tells you what to look like. Buy the truck, lift the weight, talk like this, never apologize. And a costume can't tell you how to sit with your wife in a hospital parking lot.

JONATHON: But some of those guys are saying true things. Get up, work hard, quit whining, take care of your family. My mom's boyfriend would have agreed with half of it. I'm not sure you can just throw it all in a costume pile.

WES: I'm not saying it's all wrong. Some of it's true. My question is whether it fits a real Tuesday. A sentence that fits works in the kitchen, in the driveway, at the bedside, in the truck with a sixteen-year-old. A costume only works when somebody's filming.

JONATHON: I'm not totally sold. I think a lot of men need somebody loud to wake them up, honestly. I was asleep for years. A quiet sentence on a receipt might not have done it.

WES: Did the loud stuff work on you? Tell the truth.

JONATHON: For about a month. Two years ago I got into a whole thing. Up at four-thirty, cold shower, a journal with a leather cover, a podcast guy yelling at me during my commute. Dani said it was like living with a motivational poster. And I was more disciplined, honestly. But I was also tired and short with the kids by seven, and I still took the long way home some nights. The discipline was real. It just never made it through the front door.

WES: That's my whole point. A costume can get you up at four-thirty. It can't get you through the front door.

WES: Duly noted.

JONATHON: That's what he says when he's not changing his mind. Just so everybody knows. "Duly noted" means "I heard you, and I'm still right."

WES: That's what it means. But here's where I'll meet you. The loud guys are answering a real hunger. Men want somebody to tell them who they are. I just don't think the answer starts with a man. I think it starts at the very beginning of the book.

## SEGMENT 5 · The Word

WES: We're about halfway. Genesis 1:27. Here's the setting in two sentences. God has just made the light and the land and the animals, and at the end of it he makes people, and he does it differently. Here's how it reads. "So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them."

JONATHON: I've heard that verse a hundred times. I always thought "image" meant we look like God somehow. Two arms, two legs, a face. What am I missing?

WES: Here's what helped me. In the world Genesis was written in, a king would put up a statue of himself in a city he ruled. That statue was his image. It said two things. This place belongs to him, and this is what he's like. So when God says he made people in his image, he's saying, you're the statue. You're the thing in the world that shows what I'm like and who this place belongs to.

JONATHON: So a man isn't made to win the room. He's made to show something. That's a totally different job description.

WES: That's it. And a man living "pay the bills and don't make trouble" isn't showing much. He's a statue facing the wall.

JONATHON: That's a mean picture, and I'm going to think about it every time I get home. But it also takes some pressure off, honestly. I don't have to invent what a man is from scratch. Somebody already decided what I'm for.

WES: That's the relief of it. The loud guys and the quiet guys are both trying to write the sentence from the ground up. It's already been written from the top down. Your job isn't to invent manhood. Your job is to show something true about God to the people in your house on a regular weekday.

JONATHON: But what's the sentence, though? You've got me all worked up, and you still haven't given me the better one. Theo's going to ask again.

WES: Next week. I'll give you four marks and one sentence a man can say out loud. Today I just want every man to see the sentence he's been living. You can't trade it in until you've read it.

[BOARD: Ephesians 2:10 (NIV) · "For we are God's handiwork, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance for us to do."]

## SEGMENT 6 · Saying it out loud

WES: Here's what I did this week. My youngest drives me to the feed store on Saturdays. He's sixteen, new license, and I hold the handle above the door the whole way. And I said, "Can I tell you something embarrassing?" And he said, "Is it about your knees?"

JONATHON: Ha. That's a sixteen-year-old who knows exactly where your soft spot is. What did you tell him?

WES: I told him about the receipt. For about fifteen years I thought a man was somebody who paid the bills and didn't make trouble. And he was quiet for a second, and then he said, "That's kind of what I thought you thought."

JONATHON: Oof. So he already had it. He'd read it off of you without you ever saying a word.

WES: He watched me for sixteen years. He already had my sentence. And I said, "I'm working on a better one." And he said, "Okay." And then he ran a yellow light.

JONATHON: A yellow light, with you holding the handle? Wes, be honest with me. How yellow are we talking about here?

WES: A dark yellow. We'll leave it there. But I said it out loud to him, and he didn't make it weird. What about you? Did you go back to the bathtub?

JONATHON: Thursday night, same tub, same shark. I said, "Hey, buddy, you asked me what a man is. I've got a better answer. A man takes care of people, even when he's tired." And Theo looks at me and asks, "Are you tired?" And I said, "Always, buddy." And he reached up and patted my head with a wet hand, like I was the kid.

WES: That's a good trade. He got a better sentence than "be a man about it," and you got patted on the head. How did it feel to say it?

JONATHON: Honestly? Scary. It's not a perfect sentence, and I'll probably change it. But it was the first time I ever said one out loud to my son on purpose. He's already got "be a man about it" in there. Now at least he's got two to choose from.

## SEGMENT 7 · This week's challenge

WES: Before we're done, here's the challenge. Write down the sentence you've actually been living. Not the church one. The real one, even if it's a bad one. Especially if it's a bad one. Mine was "pay the bills and don't make trouble." Yours might be "keep everybody happy," or "don't need anybody," or "win." Write it on something. A receipt works.

JONATHON: And then, if you're married, ask your wife what she thinks your sentence is. That's the scary part. But there are rules, because I've done this wrong. Pick a calm night, not after a fight. Say yours first, so she's not guessing alone. And whatever she says, don't swing back. Just say thank you.

WES: If you're single, ask a friend who's known you a long time, or your brother, or your dad if you can. Same rules. Say yours first and don't argue. And if you've got kids at home, try a better sentence out loud on one of them. It doesn't have to be perfect. Theo's dad got patted on the head.

JONATHON: And bring the receipt Friday, because we're going to ask what you wrote. Mine's still on the table. "Win the room." It looks worse in ink.
