**Arc.** *Hook:* Theo asks, "Why do you only do the coach voice at practice?" *Tension:* three shapes the trouble inside takes on an ordinary week: skip the house, run the room, wear the job as your name. Jonathon is the best dad on the field and skips his son from four feet away. A contractor ran his house like a job site until his wife put down a dish towel. Wes lost a title in 2019, didn't tell his wife for three weeks, and found out she never knew what it said. *Turn:* Proverbs 14:12. All three look right from the bleachers. The way that's actually right picked up a towel. *Resolution:* pushback, an unsettled argument over which shape is worst, twenty minutes of grounders that end at the neighbor's door, and "Three kids and your mother." The challenge: name your shape and do one contrary act this week.

## SEGMENT 1 · The coach voice

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on Theo, since he already told on you.

JONATHON: Coach-pitch is back. Ruby's team, the Bluebonnets. Zero and six last fall, undefeated in losing. And we are going to win one this spring. I've said it out loud, so it's on the record. I brought the whistle for moral support. I'm not going to use it.

WES: Please don't. Tell me about Saturday.

JONATHON: First practice. Twelve seven-year-olds, a bucket of balls, and me. And I'm not saying I'm good at baseball. We lost six games. I'm good at the kids. I learned every kid's name and one thing about each of them before practice. Harper has a lizard. One of the twins is scared of the ball, the other one isn't. A kid cried because his glove was the wrong color, and I sat him on a bucket and talked him down in about ninety seconds. I'm loud and fun, everybody gets a high five, and I can feel the parents in the bleachers thinking I'm the best dad on the field.

WES: That's a real gift. I mean that. So then what happened?

JONATHON: Then I go home. Noon. I lie down on the couch, pick up my phone, and I'm just done, like somebody pulled a plug. About two o'clock Theo comes over with his glove. He's four. He's not even on the team. He just wants to throw. And I say, "Not now, buddy," without looking up. He stands there a second and says, "Why do you only do the coach voice at practice?" Dani's in the kitchen, and she says, "Daddy's tired, bud. He coached all morning." Covering for me, which was kind. And she was wrong.

WES: How was she wrong? You did coach all morning.

JONATHON: I wasn't tired. I'd have gone back and coached another two hours if somebody'd asked. I was done performing. There were no bleachers in my living room. I've got plenty of energy. I just spend it where there's an audience. Sales meetings, the men's breakfast, my VP. And the people who get the leftovers are the two under four feet tall who live with me. And Dani, when she's awake.

WES: That's the first shape. Skip the house. And it almost never looks like a man who's never home. You were home. You were on the couch. You skipped it from four feet away.

JONATHON: I skipped it from four feet away. I'm going to be chewing on that one for a month.

WES: Do you know when the coach voice started?

JONATHON: Exactly. Nine. The week my dad left, my mom said I was the man of the house. I figured out that if I was loud and funny and on, she'd laugh, and the house felt less empty. So I was on, all the time, for her. And I can feel that one wanting to be a "because." I'm not using it. It's where it started. It's not why I said "not now" to Theo.

WES: That's the last sentence, right there.

JONATHON: I've been listening for the last sentence all week. It's very annoying. I can't tell a story at work anymore without checking who's in it.

## SEGMENT 2 · The crew

JONATHON: The middle one, run the room, I'm going to tell on somebody else, with permission. Our contractor friend said, quote, "Somebody might as well learn something from it."

WES: The welding man. He's earned the right to tell it.

JONATHON: Sixty. Runs a crew of twenty, and you want him running your job site. Everybody knows what they're doing, nobody stands around, every decision's made fast, and it's his. And for thirty years he ran his house the exact same way. Supper was a job-site meeting. He picked the vacations, the cars, the church, the school. Fast, and his. He told me he really thought that was leading. Then about ten years ago they're in the kitchen, he's laying out the plan for a new roof, and his wife sets down a dish towel and says, "I'm not on your crew."

WES: Oh, that's a hard sentence to hear in your own kitchen.

JONATHON: He said it was like a two-by-four. Because she was right, and because she'd been on his crew for twenty years without ever being asked if she wanted the job. He went out and sat in his truck for an hour. Didn't start it. Then he came back in and said, "What kind of roof do you want?" She said, "Metal. I've wanted metal for six years." He'd been about to put shingles on it again. They got the metal. He says it's the best roof he's ever put on, and he didn't pick it.

WES: And that's where the welding comes from, isn't it?

JONATHON: I asked him why teaching, why welding. He said, "I've been the boss my whole life. I'd like to work with somebody who's allowed to talk back." At sixty. He's still learning.

## SEGMENT 3 · The title

WES: Mine's the third one, wearing the job as your name. I didn't know it was mine until it got taken off me. 2019. I'm forty-four, I've had the same title for nine years, and on a Tuesday they reorganize. Conference room, a man from out of town, a slide with boxes on it. Same pay, different desk. My box on the slide is gone. I report to a man I used to sit next to. Nobody fired me. On paper, nothing happened. And I drove home and didn't tell my wife.

JONATHON: For how long? Because I'm guessing it wasn't one night.

WES: Three weeks. I went to the new desk and came home and said "fine" at supper. If I wasn't that title, I didn't know what to say at supper. I'd tell stories about meetings, complain about the people under me. That was my whole supper. So I got short with everybody. Not loud. Short. Sighing at things, snapping at my youngest about homework. And one night my daughter, she was seventeen, put her fork down and said, "Are you mad at us?"

JONATHON: Oh, that's the question every dad dreads. What did you tell her?

WES: I said no, which was true. I wasn't mad at them. I'd lost my name, and I was taking it out on the people who never called me by it. They called me Dad. That name was fine. I just didn't count it. That night I told my wife, in the kitchen. She said, "Three weeks? Over a title?" I said yes. And she said, "Honey, I didn't even know what your title was." She knew where I worked and what I did all day. She never knew the words on the door. Nine years. It was the most important thing about me, to me, and the woman I married couldn't have told you what it said.

JONATHON: That's brutal, Wes. That's the most brutal kind thing I've ever heard.

WES: It was a mercy. Took about a week to feel like one. I quit telling meeting stories at supper, mostly because I didn't have good ones anymore, and the kids filled the hole. My daughter talked about student teaching. My youngest talked about a video game for what felt like a full year. Last month my daughter, she's twenty-five now, said, "That was the year you started asking us stuff." I didn't know she'd noticed. I didn't know it had a year.

JONATHON: So you got demoted into being a dad. That's the story.

WES: I'd say I got reorganized into one. It took a man from out of town with a slide.

## SEGMENT 4 · The Word

WES: We're about halfway. There's a verse that fits all three of these, and it's short and not comfortable. Let me get the glasses. "There is a way that appears to be right, but in the end it leads to death." Proverbs is mostly sayings about ordinary life, and this one's about the road that looks fine. Not the road that looks evil. The one that looks right.

JONATHON: Because all three of these shapes look right. That's what's scary about them.

WES: The coach voice looks like a great dad. The man with the title looks like a hard worker. The man running the house looks like a leader. Nobody at church pulls you aside about any of them. They compliment you. And "in the end it leads to death," I don't think that usually means you drop dead. Something dies slow. A kid who quits bringing his glove. A daughter asking if you're mad at her. A wife who finally puts down the dish towel.

JONATHON: Can I tell you which part gets me? "Appears to be right." Appears to who? To the bleachers. To the VP. To the guys at church. Every shape looks right to everybody who doesn't live with you, and the people who live with you see it every day.

WES: That's exactly it. If you want to know which shape is yours, don't ask the bleachers. Ask the house.

JONATHON: So what's the way that's actually right, if all three look right and aren't?

WES: The best picture I know is the night before Jesus died. He's the one man in that room with every right to run it. And he gets up from supper, wraps a towel around his waist, and washes their feet. Twelve men. The dirtiest job in the house. And then he tells them, I'm your Lord and I did this, so you do it for each other. He's not just a better way. He called himself the Way, and you follow him out of your shape. You don't think your way out.

JONATHON: He picked up a towel. And the contractor's wife put one down.

WES: That's about the whole difference. The right way looks a lot less impressive from the bleachers. Nobody claps for feet.

[BOARD: John 13:14 (NIV) · "Now that I, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also should wash one another's feet."]

## SEGMENT 5 · Pushback

JONATHON: Pushback, and this one's for me. Somebody has to make decisions. If I don't run the room, who does? Isn't some of this just leadership?

WES: Some of it is. I'm not telling you to stop deciding things. Here's the difference I've seen. Running the room means you win every conversation. Leading means the other people in the room can actually change your mind. One question: when's the last time your wife changed your mind about something that mattered?

JONATHON: The decaf. And before you say it, the decaf matters a great deal to her. Okay, something that mattered. Easter. She changed my mind about my mom coming for Easter. It took two years.

WES: Then you're not all the way the contractor. But it took two years. What about the single guy? There's no house to skip.

JONATHON: That's my question, so I'll take it. My nephew, twenty-two, is in the Bluebonnets parent group chat. He's Ruby's cousin, he comes to the games, and he offered to bring the oranges. Nobody else offered to bring the oranges.

WES: That's a young man spending his best on somebody who can't do anything for him. And the shapes start way before a house. A single man can already wear his job as his name and give his best hours to the gym or the group chat. Don't wait for a wife to fix it. Getting married won't give you more energy for home. It'll just give you a house to skip.

JONATHON: The retired guy. His name was the job for forty years, and now he's home all day, and he doesn't know who he is at the table either.

WES: He's got the best shot of anybody. The title's already off. Mine got taken off at forty-four, and it took me a year to notice I was lighter. His is off for good, and he gets to pick. The contractor's picking welding. My mom's picking pot roast. What do you want to be known for at seventy? Start Monday.

JONATHON: And the guy whose wife runs the room. Not him. Her. He's sitting there nodding, thinking, finally, they're talking about her.

WES: Maybe so. I don't know your house. But this isn't something to hand to her. It's a mirror, not a flashlight. Find your shape first. If you go home tonight and say, "The podcast said you run the room," I promise that's going worse than the fan.

JONATHON: You haven't told anybody about the fan yet. Everybody's waiting.

WES: Friday. And divorced dads, it's easy to do the coach voice all weekend and go dark the other twelve days. The kids know which days they get. Pick one thing on your off weeks that's for them. A call, same time every night. They'll remember that longer than the weekend.

[BOARD: Philippians 2:4 (NIV) · "not looking to your own interests but each of you to the interests of the others."]

## SEGMENT 6 · Two chairs

JONATHON: Can we disagree about something? I think run the room is the worst one. The other two, you're hurting yourself or you're absent. The guy running the room is actively flattening people every day. His wife's been on his crew for twenty years. That's not leftovers. That's taking.

WES: I'd say skip the house is the worst, because nobody ever catches it. The man running the room, at least everybody can see it. His wife can say "I'm not on your crew," because there's a crew to point at. The man who skips the house just isn't there. A kid can't say "you're flattening me." He can only say "why do you only do the coach voice at practice." And most kids never say it. They just stop bringing the glove.

JONATHON: But you can fix skipping. You just show up. Running the room is who you are, and that's a lot harder to change.

WES: You think showing up is easy? I spent twelve years not showing up. It wasn't easier.

JONATHON: Okay. Then we're not settling this one today, and I'm fine with that.

WES: We're not settling it. Pick whichever one made you wince.

## SEGMENT 7 · What we actually did

WES: So what did you do?

JONATHON: Sunday afternoon, after church, after lunch, the couch is calling, and I can hear Theo in the backyard throwing a ball against the fence by himself. I got up. Went out there and did the full coach voice. "Let's go, Theo!" Twenty minutes. Grounders, pop-ups, high fives. He loved it for about fifteen minutes. Then he threw one over the fence into the neighbor's yard. Hard. On purpose, I think. The neighbor's a retired guy who does not love us, and when he opened the door Theo said, "My dad's a coach." Like that was going to help.

WES: Did it help? I want to know if the neighbor bought it.

JONATHON: He gave us the ball. He did not look impressed. And here's the honest part. Monday I came home and I was on the couch again, phone out. Theo came over, and I said, "Give me ten minutes." Which is better than "not now." But one Sunday doesn't fix it.

WES: It's not supposed to. You did one contrary thing. That's the assignment. Mine was Sunday at church. There's a new man, maybe forty, first time there with his wife. After the service he's standing by himself, I go over, and he asks the question every man asks every other man in the first minute. "So what do you do, Wes?" I had the answer loaded. The job, the company, the whole thing. And instead I said, "I've got three kids and a mother who counts my reps at physical therapy."

JONATHON: That's a great answer. I'd want to keep talking to that guy.

WES: He laughed, and he told me about his two boys. One's in a cast. We talked ten minutes, and at the end he asked about the job, and I told him. Second. It felt like taking off a coat I'd worn so long I forgot it was a coat. It's still on the hook by the door. I'll probably wear it next week. But I knew it was a coat.

JONATHON: Was your wife there for any of this? Because I feel like there's more.

WES: She was standing right next to me. In the car she said, "Three kids and your mother." I said, "What?" She said, "That's who you are. Three kids and your mother."

JONATHON: You left her out. Twenty-seven years, standing right next to you, and you left her out.

WES: Eighteen inches away. She brought it up at supper, and again Monday, so I'd call it medium mad. You take off one coat and you're standing there in another one.
