**Arc.** *Hook:* Jonathon admits he and Dani have no will. *Tension:* two piles mixed up. Jonathon has left a weight with his name on it sitting on the dashboard for three years, because one line on the form means picturing himself gone. Wes picked up a weight that was his son's, paid for a dented tailgate and called it grace, and heard "It's just a truck." *Turn:* Galatians 6. Every man carries his own duffel, and nobody carries a couch alone. Jonathon pushes back that this sounds like bootstraps, and Wes turns it around: the verse is on his mother's side. Then the harder one: a mother's holiday happiness was never a nine-year-old's to carry. *Resolution:* a lawyer's appointment, a bed made badly, a body-shop bill on the kitchen table, twelve Saturdays at a brother's place, and Wes's first Tuesday driving his mom. The challenge: one weight to pick up, and one to name as not yours.

## SEGMENT 1 · The check-engine light

WES: Let's take the next few minutes on that will. Three years?

JONATHON: Since Theo was born. Every time one of us gets on a plane, somebody says it. "We really need to do the will." Then we land, and nobody says it again. And then Christmas, at Dani's parents' place in New Braunfels, her mom's at the table, and out of nowhere, over pie, she says, "So if something happens to you two, who gets the kids?"

WES: Over pie. That's a brave woman. What did you say?

JONATHON: The whole table went quiet. Dani looked at me. I looked at my pie. And I said, "We're working on it." We were not working on it. We were nowhere near working on it. On the drive home Dani said, "That's four times I've brought it up." I said, "Has it been four?" She said, "Five, counting tonight." She wasn't mad. She just said it like the weather.

WES: So why haven't you done it? It's not hard. You call somebody and fill out some forms.

JONATHON: That's what I told myself. I hate paperwork. But that's not it. The forms aren't the hard part. One line on the forms is. Who raises Ruby and Theo if we're both gone. And that's either my sister in Pasadena, who I love, or Dani's parents, who are wonderful and in their seventies. And to answer it, I have to sit at a table and picture my kids without me. So I didn't. For three years.

WES: That's a real reason. Not a good one, but a real one.

JONATHON: It's like a check-engine light. It comes on, you're worried, then a week goes by and the car still runs, and pretty soon you don't even see it. It's just part of the dashboard. And I always figured it was both of ours, which in my house means it's Dani's, because she does the paperwork. But I sat with it this week, and it's mine.

WES: Why yours, though? Most couples would call that shared.

JONATHON: Because I'm the one who keeps saying I want to protect this family. And I'm the guy whose dad left when he was nine without arranging anything for anybody. I, of all people, know what it's like when a man doesn't think about what happens after he's gone. I'm the last guy who should be driving around with that light on.

WES: Can I tell you something embarrassing about my will? I've had one since 2002, when my daughter was a baby. And my wife did it. All of it. She found the lawyer, filled out the forms at the kitchen table, and I signed where she put the little yellow stickers. And when she asked me who should raise the kids, I said, "Whoever you think." She wrote down her sister, and that was that. I found out it was her sister when I was fifty.

JONATHON: Okay, so I'm not the only one. That honestly makes me feel a lot better.

WES: You're in a big club. Most married men I know have a will their wife did, or none at all. And either way, the one question that matters, who raises my kids, a man let somebody else answer. Because it's the one question you can't answer without picturing yourself gone. Which is exactly why it's a man's question. Somebody has to picture it, so the kids don't have to live it without a plan.

## SEGMENT 2 · The dent

WES: Mine's the opposite. Mine's a weight I picked up that wasn't mine. In December, at the feed store, my sixteen-year-old backed my truck into the post by the loading dock. The post was built in 1971. It was fine. My tailgate was not.

JONATHON: I've ridden with him. He drives like he's late for something that hasn't been scheduled yet.

WES: That's him. He came in the house white as a sheet, and I went out and looked, and I said, "Don't worry about it." When the estimate came, I paid it and didn't even tell my wife what it cost. It felt like grace. My dad would've yelled for a week, and I wasn't going to be that.

JONATHON: That sounds nice, though. Honestly, that's the dad I'd want to be. What's wrong with it?

WES: Three weeks later he rolled through a stop sign with me in the truck, and when I said something, he said, "It's fine, it's just a truck." Just a truck. Because to him it was. He never saw the bill. I carried it, so he learned the truck doesn't weigh anything.

JONATHON: And I flagged this, by the way. When you scored yourself a point for handling the body shop, I said that might be you picking up his weight.

WES: You did. You were right, and I gave myself half a point anyway. I took his weight and called it love. And some of it was love. But a lot of it was me not wanting a hard conversation with a sixteen-year-old. So I paid my way out of it.

JONATHON: So you've got the opposite problem from me. I set down one that's mine, and you picked up one that's his.

WES: Same problem, different pile. Either way, the weight ends up in the wrong place, and somebody doesn't grow.

## SEGMENT 3 · The Word

WES: We're about halfway. Galatians 6, verse 5. Hold on, let me get these on.

JONATHON: Oh, he brought the reading glasses. I'm not going to make it a thing. Okay, I'm going to make it a little bit of a thing.

WES: Paul's writing to churches that were comparing themselves, a lot of "at least I'm not him." And he says, quit looking sideways and look at your own work. Then this. "For each one should carry their own load." That's the whole verse.

JONATHON: But that's the same chapter that says carry each other's burdens. I remember that one from a men's retreat.

WES: Three verses earlier. "Carry each other's burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ." So which is it? Both. I've helped people move for thirty years, so this is my expertise. Every man carries his own duffel. Nobody carries a couch alone. Your duffel is your stuff. Your work, your words, your marriage, your kids, your health, your walk with God. Nobody carries that for you, and you don't hand it to your wife and call it teamwork. A couch is the thing that's too heavy for one man. A diagnosis, a death, a layoff. That's when you call your brothers.

JONATHON: And the problem is, men get those backwards. We try to carry the couch alone, because asking for help feels like failing. And then we leave our duffel by the door for somebody else, because it's boring.

WES: That's exactly it. The will is a duffel. It's a little heavy, a little awkward, but it's yours. And the dent was my son's duffel, and I carried it for him like it was a couch.

JONATHON: I've done the couch thing too, the carrying it alone part. My first year as a manager, I lost our biggest account. A national chain, gone in one phone call. And I didn't tell Dani for two months. I didn't tell anybody. I'd lie awake doing math at three in the morning, and then I'd get up and be funny at breakfast. Win the room, never let them see you hurt. Dani finally found me in the garage at midnight with a calculator and asked if we were okay.

WES: And what happened when you told her? Because that's the part men are scared of.

JONATHON: She said, "Okay. What do we cut?" That was it. Two months of not sleeping, and she had a plan in about four minutes. It was never a one-man couch. I just wouldn't call anybody to grab the other end.

WES: And sometimes the man who won't grab the other end is the one standing right there. When my dad was dying, my older brother carried most of that couch. The hospital, the cattle, the paperwork, Mom. I drove out on weekends and felt like a good son. I wasn't carrying the couch. I was visiting it. I've never said that to him. That's part of what was sitting under Thanksgiving, if I'm honest. So when a man asks me which is which, I tell him: look around and see who's been carrying your end.

[BOARD: Your duffel is yours. A couch needs your brothers.]

## SEGMENT 4 · Pushback

JONATHON: Okay, here's my pushback. This can sound a lot like bootstraps. Every man for himself. Carry your own load, don't complain, don't need anybody. I grew up with a mom who did everything by herself, and I watched what that did to her. I'm not sure "carry your own load" is the gospel.

WES: That's fair, and there's a version of this that's just pride with a Bible verse on it. Carrying your own load doesn't mean nobody helps you. It means nobody else is responsible for your part. Your mom carried her duffel, and your dad's duffel, and probably a couch or two, alone, for years. That's not what Paul means. That's what happens when a man doesn't pick his up.

JONATHON: Okay. That's my dad. He set down his duffel, and my mom picked it up, and she's still carrying some of it twenty-seven years later.

WES: So the verse is actually on her side. Most people hear it as "don't ask for help." It's really "don't make somebody else carry what's yours."

JONATHON: I've never heard it that way. Okay, second one, and this is the one I really care about. What about the stuff I'm carrying that isn't mine? Because I think I've got some. My mom's feelings, every holiday. That's the fight Dani and I had in October that we're finishing Sunday. Every Thanksgiving, every Christmas, the whole schedule gets built around whether my mom's going to be okay. I've carried that since I was nine. She told me I was the man of the house the week he left.

WES: She told you that? At nine years old?

JONATHON: She didn't mean anything by it. She was thirty-one with two kids, and she was drowning. But I heard it, and I took it, and I've been carrying her happiness ever since.

WES: Can I say something careful? Honoring your mother is yours. That's in the Ten Commandments. Calling her, seeing her, making sure she has what she needs, that's your duffel. But whether she's happy on Christmas afternoon is not yours. Here's how you can tell. Have you ever once made your mom happy on a holiday by moving your schedule around?

JONATHON: No. She's still sad. We're just tired, and Dani's mad. That feels mean to say out loud.

WES: It's not mean, it's true. When you carry something that's yours, it gets lighter for somebody. When you carry something that isn't, nobody gets lighter. You just get heavier.

[BOARD: Exodus 20:12 (NIV) · "Honor your father and your mother"]

JONATHON: Last one, and this isn't me. It's the man listening right now who can't. He lost his job, or he's depressed, or his body doesn't work right, and "pick up your load" sounds like one more thing he's failing at.

WES: Then I'd say this to him. Your load right now might be really small. It might be getting out of bed and calling one person. That counts. For a man who's barely standing, asking for help is the heaviest thing he'll lift all week.

## SEGMENT 5 · Whose spreadsheet

WES: Now I've got one for you. Who pays the bills at your house?

JONATHON: Dani. She's better at it. She's got a spreadsheet with colors, and I'm not allowed to touch the spreadsheet. I think that's healthy.

WES: That's fine. Couples divide things up. My wife's better at money than me too. Here's the question. If Dani couldn't do it for three months, could you pay the mortgage next month?

JONATHON: I don't know what our mortgage is. I mean, I know about what it is. I don't know the number, I don't know the day it comes out, and I don't know the password. So, okay. I came in here with one duffel and I'm leaving with two.

WES: You don't have to do the spreadsheet. But a man ought to know where the weight is in his own house, so if the person carrying it goes down, he can pick it up the same day.

JONATHON: She's been trying to show me that spreadsheet for six years. She's going to be so happy, and so unbearable about it.

## SEGMENT 6 · What we actually did

WES: So what did you actually do?

JONATHON: Sunday night I found a lawyer. There's a woman at our church who does wills. I knew that, because she announced it at a men's breakfast once and I avoided eye contact. Monday morning I called her office. Appointment's Thursday at four-thirty. The calendar entry just says "Will. 4:30." I made it short so I couldn't argue with it. I told Dani at supper, and she laughed, not mean, just surprised. Then she said, "Okay. Who's watching the kids Thursday?" Which, fair. And then, "We have to decide the kids before Thursday."

WES: So when are you two having that conversation? Because that's the hard line on the form.

JONATHON: Wednesday, after bath. And Sunday's the other talk. It's a big week in our kitchen. I don't know how it ends. My sister or her parents. But that light's not going to be on the dashboard by Friday. And one small one, because you said look for the dent. Ruby's supposed to make her bed. That's her one job. And every morning, while she eats toast, I go make it for her, because it takes me forty seconds and takes her eleven minutes and a negotiation. So this morning I didn't. We were four minutes late, she was furious, and the bed looked like a raccoon slept in it. She made it herself. I've never been prouder of a terrible bed.

WES: That's a good bed. Saturday morning I sat my son down at the kitchen table with the body-shop bill. The actual piece of paper. And I said, "I was wrong to just pay this. Not because you're in trouble. Because it's yours, and I took it from you." He looked at the number a long time, and he said, "That's a lot of money." I said, "Yes, it is." Most honest conversation we've had about that truck.

JONATHON: So he's paying you back? A sixteen-year-old doesn't have that kind of money lying around.

WES: He's working it off. I called my older brother and said, "Could you use a sixteen-year-old on Saturdays?" Long quiet, then, "Send him." So my son spends twelve Saturdays at my brother's place feeding cattle and fixing fence. And I drive him out and pick him up. That's forty minutes of truck time each way and coffee in my brother's kitchen. I didn't plan that part. I'm not that smart.

JONATHON: That's God being funny. Your son pays for the dent, and you get your brother back out of it.

WES: And this morning I drove my mom to physical therapy for the first time. My wife rode along like she said she would. Mom counts my reps, even though I'm only the driver. She said, "You're limping worse than me." And tomorrow's my knee appointment. I figured out this week why I put it off since October. My dad had both knees done in his sixties, and he was never the same. The limp was safer than the answer.

JONATHON: So you're picking up a weight your dad set down. I'm going to text you at four tomorrow to ask how it went.

## SEGMENT 7 · This week's challenge

WES: Before we're done, the challenge, in two small parts. One, find one weight with your name on it that you've been letting somebody else carry, and put your hand on it before Friday. A phone call, an appointment, a hard sentence. Two, find one thing you're carrying that isn't yours. You don't have to set it down yet. Just name it, and write down whose it actually is. Friday there's a tool for setting it down.

JONATHON: Married guys, ask your wife on a calm night, "What do you carry around here that's really mine?" And then don't argue. Write it down. Single guys, yours is usually the stuff nobody notices if you never do it. Your budget, your health, that thing with your landlord. And who's your emergency contact? If you had to think about it, that's your duffel.

WES: Divorced dad, you can't carry what happens at her house. But your weekends are completely yours, and they count. And if you're a parent, look for the dent. What have you been paying for that your kid should be carrying? Age-appropriate. Don't hand a six-year-old the mortgage.
