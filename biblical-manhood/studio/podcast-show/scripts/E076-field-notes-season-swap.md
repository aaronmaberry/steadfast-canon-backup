JONATHON: Mine. I said I'd ask the sixty-one-year-old rep for one thing I'm doing wrong as a manager. I asked him Monday, in his truck, before a call. And he didn't even think about it. He told me I only talk to the guys who are winning, and everybody else hears from me when something's wrong.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: And I wanted to argue. I had a list. And then I pulled up my texts and scrolled back a month, and he was right. I'd texted my top three reps about forty times, and the bottom three, mostly about numbers. He's watched managers for thirty-five years. He saw it in about a week of me.

[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: That's the answer to his question, though. He's already doing it. You don't connect with a man like that across a table. You connect next to him. Side by side, not face to face. Under a car, in a deer stand, on a ladder. For a lot of older men, and a lot of younger ones, sitting across a table and being asked questions feels like a job interview. Handing each other wrenches doesn't.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: I learned that with Dani's dad. He's seventy-four, married fifty-one years, and for the first several years I knew him, I tried to talk to him at the table. Questions. How's retirement, how's the yard. He'd give me one word and go back to his plate. And he's watched golf on TV every Sunday afternoon for thirty years. I'd been trying to get in the front door for five years. The side door was a golf tournament. And now when I go up there, I don't ask him how retirement is. I ask him who's leading.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And the second thing I'd tell my son. Bring the coffee. Black, one sugar. Without asking. Every month. A man who doesn't talk notices who notices him. I've been bringing that man his coffee for months, and it's the only reason he'll tolerate me.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: And third, ask about the work, not about the man. Don't ask him, "So what was your life like?" He'll shut the hood on you. Ask him, "Where'd you learn brakes?" A man who won't tell you about himself will tell you about his work for an hour, and somewhere in the middle of that, he tells you about himself.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: And here's a verse for the other direction. Because it's not just young men who need old ones. It's a warning about what happens when a young man only listens to his own age. [CAM: JON MS]

[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And I'd say the old men need a verse too, but I don't have one handy, so here's mine. The old man who only talks to old men gets bitter. [CAM: JON CU]

JONATHON: Tool on the table. The cross-generation lunch. And the name's a little fancy for what it is, which is lunch.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Step one. Pick one man about twenty years older or younger than you. Not a crowd. One. Somebody you already see. Church, work, the gym, the feed store. Don't go looking for the most impressive man you know. Go looking for the one you keep noticing. The one who's always stacking chairs, or the young guy who sits alone in the back. You already know who it is. You've been walking past him.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: Step two. Ask him plain. "Can I buy you lunch? I want to ask you something." That second sentence matters. Men need a reason. "I want to ask you something" is a reason. "Let's get together sometime" is how nothing happens for five years.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Step three, three questions, and you don't need more. What are you working on right now? What do you wish somebody had told you at my age, or what do you wish men my age understood about yours? And, what should I be asking you that I'm not?
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: Step four, listen most of the time. If you're the older man, this is the hard one. We want to give the speech. I've got about nine of them loaded at all times. Don't. Ask your questions, and answer his, and save the speech for when he asks. He'll ask, if you didn't give it to him the first time.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Step five, set the next one before you leave the table. "Same time next month?" If you leave it open, it'll stay open till one of you dies.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And if he's a man who doesn't sit down to lunch, skip the lunch. Do a job together. A ladder, a fence, a car on the first Saturday. The questions work just as well over a tailgate. They might work better.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: How it goes wrong. It turns into a program. You print questions, you call it mentoring, you start keeping track. Then it's a meeting, and he can smell it. I've done this one. I tried it with a younger rep a few years ago, with a printed list of questions, and by the second lunch he was answering like he was in a performance review. Because he was. I'd made it one. Or it turns into a lecture, the older man talking for an hour while the younger one nods. Or the younger man performs. He tries to sound wiser than he is. Just be the age you are. That's the whole point.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: Older men, I'll say one more thing to you, because I'm one now. Go first. Don't wait for the young man to ask. Most of them think you're too busy or you don't care. My son's mechanic didn't ask. My son did. That's backwards. That should've been us. When I was thirty-six, I didn't go find Mr. Abernathy. He found me. He sat down across from me at a men's breakfast and asked me a question I couldn't answer, and then he kept coming back every other Thursday for six years. If he'd waited for me to ask, I'd still be waiting. I'm the age now where it's my turn to sit down across from somebody.
