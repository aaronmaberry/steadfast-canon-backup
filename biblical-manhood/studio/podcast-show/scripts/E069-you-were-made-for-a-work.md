[CHAPTER: The attic]
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: So, the attic. Every summer I ride along with a couple of our techs. My boss likes it. It's good for the sales guys to see what we're selling. Usually I do it in June, when it's merely awful. This year I put it off till August, which was a mistake.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Thursday I rode with our senior tech. Sixty years old. Thirty-one years in attics. Hands like baseball gloves. And the last call of the day was an eighty-four-year-old widow on the east side. Her unit died Wednesday night. Eighty-four, a hundred and four outside, no air. She'd been sitting in her car in the driveway with the engine running to stay cool.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And he goes up in that attic, and it's a hundred and thirty-eight up there. I know because he had a thermometer clipped to his belt, and he showed me, like it was a fun fact. And he's up there an hour and a half. And I'm on the ladder with the flashlight, sweating through everything I own, and I'm thinking, I could not do this for one week. He's done it for thirty-one years.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: And here's what got me. He got it running. Not a new unit. He found a capacitor and a burnt wire, and he fixed what she had. And my whole job, my actual job, is selling her a new one. That's where the money is. And he climbed down and said to her, "Ma'am, that'll hold you a few years. When it goes, call me first." And she brought him a glass of tea with ice, and she held his face in her hands like he was her grandson.
[pause 1s]
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And I drove home thinking, I've managed that man for four years. And somewhere in my head he's been a cost. A line on a sheet. Labor hours. And I watched him do the most useful thing anybody did in San Antonio that day. For a woman sitting in her car.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: Did you say anything to him?
[CAM: JON CU]

[CHAPTER: Does selling count]
[CAM: JON MS]

[CAM: WES CU]
WES: What happened at that company, that he could fix her old unit and not get in trouble for it?

[CAM: WES CU]

[CAM: JON CU]

[CHAPTER: The chests]
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: I'll tell you about my granddad's chests. He wasn't a carpenter by trade. He farmed. But in the winters, in the shed, he built cedar chests. One for every grandchild. And he didn't buy plans. He'd go into your house, and he'd measure the foot of your bed. Then he'd build a chest that fit right there. Exact. You could slide it under the footboard with a finger's width to spare. He'd work out there with a woodstove going and the radio on the farm report, and my grandmother would send one of us out with coffee, and he'd never stop to drink it till it was cold. Every board planed by hand. Every corner dovetailed. No nails anywhere in it. I've looked.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: And here's the part I didn't understand until I was grown. He built mine before I had a bed it would fit. I was in a crib. He measured the bed my parents said I'd be in when I was big enough, the one my older brother was about to move out of. And he built the chest for that bed. Two years early. And when I moved into that bed, the chest was already there. It had been waiting on me.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: It's at the foot of our bed right now. Twenty-seven years. My wife keeps the winter quilts in it. And if you lift the lid, inside, burned into the cedar, are my initials, and his, and the year. Not on the outside. He didn't need anybody to admire it. He just wanted it known who made it, and who it was for.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Did he ever say why he did it that way?
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: No. He'd have said that's just how you build a chest. But that's what got me about it as a man. He didn't build a chest and then go looking for somebody to give it to. He built it for a bed. For a spot. For a kid. Before the kid was ready. That's the difference between something that's just made and something that's made for.
[TEXT: Built for a spot before the kid was ready.]

[CHAPTER: The Word]
[CAM: WES MS]

[CARD: Ephesians 2:10]

Ephesians says you're already His handiwork before you lift a finger.

[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Which is a relief, honestly. For a sales guy. My whole life is making something out of nothing. Cold calls. Fresh territory. And this says, I'm not inventing it. It's prepared. I just have to show up for it.

WES: That's fair. I'm going to sit with that. I don't have an answer for you today. I'm fifty-two. I've got some years left at it. Maybe the question isn't whether I wasted thirty. Maybe it's what I do with the next ten.

[CHAPTER: Labor Day]
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Can I say one thing about Labor Day? Because it's a strange holiday at my house. My mom worked every Labor Day I can remember. Two jobs, and the holiday shifts paid better, so she took them. She'd leave a list on the counter and a sandwich in the fridge with my name on the foil. And when I was eleven, I hated Labor Day. Everybody else's dad was at the lake. My dad was gone, and my mom was at work.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: And I think for a long time, I thought work was the thing that took my mom. That's what work was. The thing that took people. And I think that's partly why I've been so tangled up in it. I either hated it or I hid in it. Never just did it.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: How do you see her working those holidays now?
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Now? I think it was the best thing anybody ever did for me. She was making a life for a kid, by herself, with what she had. I was the good work. I just didn't know it, because it looked like a sandwich with my name on it.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: At our place, Labor Day was hay. Every year. The holiday was for town people. My dad would have us in the field by seven, and my mother would bring dinner out at noon on the tailgate, and that was the picnic. I didn't know anybody took the day off until I was in high school. And I don't resent it. I remember the tailgate better than any lake.
[TEXT: I was the good work.]

[CHAPTER: The job you hate]
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: I want to talk to the man who hates his job. Because I've been him, and a lot of what we just said sounds great until you're in a job that's eating you.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: At twenty-three I sold extended warranties at an electronics store. You know the guy. You're buying a printer, and he leans in and says, "Have you thought about protection?" That was me. And I hated it. Not because it was hard. Because I didn't believe it. I knew most of those warranties were money for nothing. And I sold them anyway, because I was good at it, and there was a bonus.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: So here's what I learned, the hard way. There are two kinds of hating your job. One is, it's hard. It's hot. The boss is a jerk. The hours are long. The work's boring. That's most hating, and that's the kind you can do for God. You can do boring well. You can do hot well. My senior tech does a hundred-thirty-eight-degree attic well.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: The other kind is, the job's asking you to do something wrong. Lie to people. Sell them a thing they don't need. Cook a number. And that's different. You can't do that one for God. That one you've got to get out of, or change.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: How'd you get out?
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Badly. I quit in the middle of a Saturday. Walked out. No plan, no next job, no savings. Because somebody is. Tell your wife before you tell anybody. Talk to a man who'll tell you if you're running. Line something up. And don't burn the place down on your way out. You might need a reference, and you'll definitely need your dignity.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And for the first kind, the hard kind, I'd add one thing. Look at who's next to you. A lot of men who hate their job are working next to another man who's going under, and they're too busy hating it to notice. The guy in the next truck. The kid on his first job. You might be the only Christian he works beside. That's a good work prepared in advance, right there in the job you hate.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: The best man I ever worked beside hated the job we had. Said so every Monday. And every Monday he'd still be the first one to ask the new hire if he'd eaten. He brought an extra lunch for two years for a kid who never had one, and never said a word about it. He hated the job and did a good work in it every single day. I've never forgotten him. I don't remember a thing about the job.
[TEXT: Hard isn't the same as wrong.]

## Reading narrative

# You Were Made for a Work
### Walk Steadfast, Episode 69 · August 31, 2027

On the last call of a hot Thursday, an 84-year-old widow had been sitting in her running car in the driveway because her air conditioner had died. It was 138 degrees in her attic. The senior tech found a bad capacitor and a burnt wire and fixed what she had. Jonathon's job is selling new units. "Thank you for not selling her one," he said. The tech looked at him like he was crazy. "She didn't need one."

Wes's granddad built a cedar chest for each grandchild, measured to the foot of that child's bed. He built Wes's two years early, for the bed he'd grow into. When Wes moved into the bed, the chest was already there.

> "For we are God's handiwork, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance for us to do."
> Ephesians 2:10 (NIV)

Grace first, then work. You don't work to get in. You work because of who made you, and He measured the spot before you got there.

## Hard isn't the same as wrong

Some jobs are hard: hot, boring, under a bad boss. You can do that work for God. Some jobs ask you to do something wrong. Those you change or leave, carefully, with your wife in on it.
