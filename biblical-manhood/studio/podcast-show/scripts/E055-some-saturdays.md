[CHAPTER: Intro]
[CAM: 2S]

[LOWER THIRD: Wes Hart]
JONATHON: I'm Jonathon. I've had a flat year or two myself.
[LOWER THIRD: Jonathon]

[CHAPTER: Pull up a chair]
[CAM: WIDE]
WES: Pull up a chair. Which days?
JONATHON: I didn't have an answer. I still don't. That's why I wanted to start there.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: It's VBS week at a church down the road. Ruby's in it, Theo's in the little kids' room. And every day she comes home with a new song, and this year's big one has the word "fire" in it about forty times. There's hand motions. She does the hand motions at the dinner table. And Wednesday at supper, in the middle of the hand motions, she stops and looks at me and says, "Daddy, are you on fire for Jesus?"
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: And I said "Some days," because it was true and I was tired. And she said, "Which days?" And Dani put her fork down and looked at me, because she wanted to hear it too. And I said, "Eat your chicken." (beat) Which is what my mom used to say when she didn't have an answer.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And later, after the kids were down, Dani said, "I wanted to hear your answer." And I said, "I know. I didn't have one." And she said, "Mine's some days too." And that was it. We didn't make it a talk. She's a nurse on nights. She's been on fire at four in the morning holding somebody's baby, and flat at four in the afternoon trying to sleep through Theo. She just wanted me to know she's in the same boat. I didn't know that. I thought she was the steady one.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And here's the thing. I know what she means by fire. I know the feeling. I had it on Thursday at the planning meeting when my VP said, "Great idea, Jonathon," in front of the whole company. I felt it in my chest. Warm. Big. On fire. For about an hour. And that's the same feeling, Wes. I'm not proud of it. The feeling I get when a VP praises me in front of eighty people and the feeling I got in that church parking lot at twenty-nine are wired to the same place in me. I can't always tell them apart. So when my daughter asks if I'm on fire, I don't trust my own answer.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: Here's mine. The truck. Second week. I've been in that cab every day at noon. And I'll tell you the honest report, because it fits today. It's boring. It's twenty-five minutes of the Rangers and the heat. He doesn't open up. I don't push. Some days we don't say ten words. And I've caught myself on the walk out to the parking lot thinking, what am I doing? This isn't doing anything.
JONATHON: Did anything change?
WES: Wednesday he had two bottles of water on the dash when I got in. Cold ones. He handed me one and didn't say anything about it. That's it. That's the whole change. Two waters. (sips coffee) I'll take a water.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: And I keep thinking about Mr. Abernathy. Every other Thursday, for six years, a man in his seventies and eighties got up early, drove to a bad diner, and sat across from a forty-year-old who mostly talked about himself. I guarantee you he didn't feel like it most of those mornings. I never once asked him. I just assumed he loved it. He probably loved it some Thursdays. He came on all of them.
[CAM: 2S]
WES: So today. Most men are waiting to feel something before they'll do the thing God already put in front of them. And the mark today is Work for God's Pay. Because if you only work when you feel it, you're working for the feeling. That's a paycheck too. It's just not His.

[CHAPTER: The flat year]
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Can I tell the flat year? Because I've never told it, and Ruby's question dug it up.
WES: Go.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: When I came to faith, at twenty-nine, it was big. That first year. I'd sit in the truck in the church parking lot after the service and cry. Not sad. Just, I couldn't believe it. Somebody wanted me. I read from Matthew to Revelation in about two months. I'd text Dani verses from work. I went to a men's conference in Dallas and came home and told her we were going to change everything. I was on fire. That's the word I'd have used. If Ruby had asked me then, I'd have said "every day."
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And then about a year in, it went flat. Not gone. Flat. Ruby was born. I was getting up at three in the morning with a baby. My numbers were down because I was home more, and I hated that. And I'd sit in church and feel nothing. I'd read my Bible and it was words. I'd pray and it was me talking to the ceiling fan. And I thought I'd lost it. I genuinely thought, it was real for a year and now it's gone, and maybe it was never real. Maybe it was just a high, like the leaderboard. Like a good quarter.
WES: What kept you there?
JONATHON: Honestly? Not much I did. Dani kept getting up on Sunday, so I kept getting up on Sunday. There was a man at that breakfast who kept asking me to come back on Thursdays, and I kept going because I'd said I would. I didn't feel it. I went. For about a year. And somewhere in there, I can't tell you the week, I noticed I was different. I wasn't drinking every Thursday anymore. Not stopped. That came later. But less. I'd quit yelling at the TV. I was home for bath. And I hadn't felt one thing while it was happening.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: So when Ruby says, "Which days?" the honest answer is, the days I feel it aren't the days it's working. The flat year was when it took.
[TEXT: The flat year was when it took.]

[CHAPTER: My forties]
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And I'll tell you mine, because I think I had the opposite of your flat year. I had a fire decade. And it wasn't what I thought it was.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: I came back to life at thirty-six, at Mr. Abernathy's table. And for the next eight or ten years, I was on fire. That's what anybody at my church would've told you. I was on three committees. I coached a ball team. I taught a class. I was the man they called to pray. I worked fifty-five hours a week and then went to church three nights. And I felt great. I felt useful. I felt close to God. People said, "Wes is on fire."
JONATHON: That sounds like the dream.
WES: It sounded like it. And one night, I was about forty-five, my wife and I were in the truck coming home from something, a meeting, I don't remember which, and she said, "Where are we going?" And I said, "Home." And she said, "No. Where are we going? You and me. Our family." And I didn't have an answer. I'd been on fire for ten years and I couldn't tell my wife where we were going.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: Here's what I figured out later. A lot of that fire was for me. It felt like God. And some of it was. But a lot of it was a man who'd been asleep for years finally feeling useful, and he liked the feeling, and he kept saying yes to anything that gave it to him. The committees fed the feeling. The class fed the feeling. Being the man they call to pray fed it. My wife and my kids didn't feed it. Supper at home on a Tuesday doesn't make you feel on fire. So I was gone.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: So when I hear "on fire for Jesus," I get a little nervous. Not because the feeling's bad. Because I've seen what I'll do to keep it. I'll burn down my own house to feel warm.
JONATHON: (beat) If Ruby had asked you then.
WES: Every day, Ruby. Every single day. And I was half gone.
[TEXT: I'll burn down my own house to feel warm.]

[CHAPTER: The Word]
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: There's one verse I want for this, because I think it tells you where the fire actually is. Paul's talking about why he does what he does. Why he keeps going when it costs him. And he says this.
[CARD: 2 Corinthians 5:15]
WES: "And he died for all, that those who live should no longer live for themselves but for him who died for them and was raised again."
[CAM: 2S]
WES: Look at what's not in that verse. There's no feeling in it. Not one. It's all facts. He died. He was raised. You live. And because of those facts, you don't live for yourself anymore. The fire's not in your chest. The fire's what He did. It's a fact about a cross and an empty tomb, and it's just as true on a flat Tuesday as it was at the conference in Dallas.
JONATHON: "No longer live for themselves." That's the part that gets me. Because the feeling was for me. When I was on fire, I liked how I felt. It was about me feeling close. And this says the whole point is it stops being about me.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: And here's the turn I want men to hear. If your destiny depends on how you feel about it, it's about you. It's about your chest. "No longer live for themselves" means the thing God has for you doesn't need your feelings' permission. It's for Him. You can do it bored. You can do it tired. You can do it in a truck talking about the bullpen. Paul got beaten and shipwrecked and thrown in jail, and I don't think he felt on fire in most of those places. He kept going because of what was true, not because of what he felt.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: So the feeling's not bad.
WES: The feeling's a gift. When it comes, thank God for it. Ruby's hand motions are a gift. Just don't make it the engine. It's the weather. You don't decide whether to go to work based on the weather.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: And look at the first three words. "He died for all." All. Not for the men who feel it. Not for the men having a good year. For all. The man in the truck at noon. Virgil. The kid who doesn't feel called. Me in the flat years and me in the fire years. Same cross. That's why it doesn't move when the feeling moves.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: I've got a card in my wallet, I've told you about it. "Love God out loud, keep the people He gave me, and finish." And I was looking at it this week, and I noticed something. There's not one feeling on it. Love's a verb on there. Keep's a verb. Finish is a verb. I didn't plan it that way. But I think the man who wrote it had already been burned once by the fire decade.
[TEXT: The fire's what He did.]

[CHAPTER: Virgil]
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: So Saturday was the crew again. The July one. And I was excited, because last time was the ramp, and the lady at the mailbox, and Theo's prayer. That was a fire day. I wanted another one.
WES: And?
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: And it was a rotten deck board. One. At a house where nobody was home. The owner's in the hospital, and her son called the church and asked if somebody could fix the board before she came home with a walker. So six of us drove out there, a hundred and one degrees, and replaced one deck board, and checked the rails, and tightened some screws on a storm door. Forty-five minutes. Nobody saw it. Nobody laughed at a mailbox. There was nobody there.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And here's what I noticed this time, because there wasn't a lady at a mailbox to look at. I watched Virgil. He runs the whole thing, and he never says he runs it. He's got a little notebook in his truck with every job they've done, in pencil. The address, what they fixed, the date. Pages of it. He checked the board, then he checked the rails nobody asked about, then he went around and tightened every screw on that storm door, one at a time, like Theo with the can. Nobody was home to thank him. He didn't look like he was waiting for anybody to.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: And I learned the screw man's name. Virgil. He's seventy-seven. I asked him while we were loading the trailer, because I'd prayed for him for two weeks as "the man with the screws" and I felt dumb about that. And he told me his name, and I told him I'd been praying for him, and he looked at me like I'd said something in another language. And then he said, "Well, I appreciate that," and went back to loading.
WES: How long has he been doing it?
JONATHON: I asked. He said, "Since my wife died." I said, "When was that?" He said, "Ninety-eight." (beat) Twenty-nine years, Wes. Every Saturday. Ramps, boards, rails. I asked him if he likes it, and he thought about it for a while, and he said, "Some Saturdays." That's all. Some Saturdays.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: That's my answer to Ruby. Virgil's. Some Saturdays. And he goes on all the other ones too.
[TEXT: "Some Saturdays."]

[CHAPTER: The barn in July]
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: I'll tell you where I'm doing this right now, without the feeling. The barn. You remember the first Saturday I fed with my brother. Back in May. I pulled in at five fifty-five, and he said "Same time next week," and I talked about it on here for a week. That was a fire day. Two months later, it's feeding cows. In July. At six. It's already hot. He doesn't talk. I don't talk. We throw hay, we check water, he says, "Well," and I go home.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: And Saturday, I didn't want to go. I'll just say it. I was up late Friday, my knee was talking, and the alarm went off at five-fifteen and I lay there and thought, he won't care. He fed alone for thirty years. One Saturday won't matter. And I lay there for about five minutes doing the math on whether my brother would notice.
JONATHON: Did you go?
WES: I went. Pulled in at six-oh-two. Late, for us. He was already in there. And he looked at his watch, and he didn't say anything, and we fed. That's it. Nothing happened. No talk. No moment. I drove home. And I'm telling you because that's what it's going to look like most Saturdays for the rest of our lives, if I keep going. Two brothers throwing hay. And I'm going to keep going. Not because I feel it. Because he's my brother, and I'm done making him feed alone.
[TEXT: Two brothers throwing hay.]

[CHAPTER: Pushback]
[CAM: 2S]
WES: Pushback.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: Twenty-two. "Everybody at church talks about finding your calling. I'm graduating, and I don't feel called to anything. I'm scared I'm going to miss it."
JONATHON: You take him. I'll take the next one.
WES: You're not going to miss it. It's not hiding. Here's the way I'd say it. You don't find your calling the way you find your keys. It's not lost. It's already the next thing in front of you. A job you can do well. A church that needs a man on Wednesdays. A grandmother who needs a ride. Do the next thing for Him, all the way, and then the next one. Look back in ten years and you'll see a road. You won't see it from the front. Nobody does.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Divorced dad. "I thought my calling was my family. My family's gone. What am I for now?"
WES: Go.
JONATHON: I'm sorry, brother. I'm not going to pretend that's small. But your family's not gone. It's changed shape. Your kids are still yours. Every-other-weekend is still fathering. And this verse says you were bought for a reason bigger than any family, even a good one. "For him who died for them." That didn't end when the papers got signed. Some of the most useful men I know got that way in the worst year of their lives. And here's a practical thing. Find one thing to do for somebody else this month that has nothing to do with your situation. A crew. A kid at church. A widow's deck. It won't fix it. It'll remind you that you're still for something.
[CAM: WES CU]
WES: Sixty-one. "I used to feel God close. Every day, for years. The last three years, nothing. I'm afraid I lost it, at my age. I want it back."
JONATHON: That one's yours.
WES: That one's me. I had years like that. In my forties, after I came back, there were years I felt everything, and then three, four years where I felt nothing and kept going to Mr. Abernathy's table anyway. You haven't lost it. Feelings get quiet as men get older. That's not God leaving. That's God trusting you with the quiet. Keep doing what you did when you felt it. Read. Pray out loud. Show up for somebody. And tell an older man, or a pastor, exactly what you just told us. Don't carry the quiet alone.
[CAM: WES MS]
WES: Forty-four. "My wife's on fire. She's at every Bible study, she's got verses on the bathroom mirror, she cries in worship. I don't feel any of that. I feel like the spiritual loser in my own house."
JONATHON: You take that one.
WES: Because I've been that man some years. Brother, she's not your scoreboard. Her feelings aren't the bar you're failing. She's wired one way and you're wired another, and God's not grading on tears. Here's what I'd ask. What do you do? Do you get up with the kids? Do you keep your word? Do you pray for her, even quietly? That's not losing. And one more thing. Tell her. Not "I'm a loser." Tell her, "I don't feel it the way you do, and I'm still here." She might be more relieved than you think. She might've been wondering.
[CAM: JON CU]
JONATHON: Twenty-eight. "The only time I feel God is at a concert. Lights, the band, everybody singing. Then Monday, nothing. Is my faith fake?"
WES: Go.
JONATHON: No. I've been to those. I've cried at those. That's real. But you might be living on the concert like I lived on the conference. Here's a test. What did you do on Tuesday for somebody who'll never be at a concert? Faith that only shows up under the lights is a faith that hasn't been asked to do anything yet. Go find something to do on a Tuesday. Then go to the concert and enjoy it. It'll mean more when it's not the only thing holding you up.
[CAM: JON MS]
JONATHON: Thirty-five. "I quit a good job last year to follow my passion. It's not working. I feel like a failure and a fraud."
WES: Go.
JONATHON: I'm not going to tell you to quit your passion. Here's the question I'd ask. Was it for God or was it for the feeling? Because I'd have done the same thing at your age, for the feeling. And if it was for the feeling, it's okay to go back and get a regular job and do it for Him. That's not failure. That might be the most Christian thing you ever do. Work you don't feel, done for somebody else.
[TEXT: You won't see the road from the front.]

[CHAPTER: Going first]

## Reading narrative

# Some Saturdays
### Walk Steadfast, Episode 55 · July 13, 2027

At supper during Vacation Bible School week, in the middle of the hand motions to a song about fire, Jonathon's daughter asked him if he was on fire for Jesus. "Some days." "Which days?"

His first year of faith was all fire: crying in the church parking lot, Matthew to Revelation in two months, a conference in Dallas. Then his daughter was born and it went flat for a year. He kept going because his wife kept getting up on Sundays and a man at a breakfast kept asking him back. "The flat year was when it took."

Wes had the opposite: a decade when everybody at church said he was on fire. Three committees, a ball team, a class. Then one night in the truck his wife asked, "Where are we going? You and me." He didn't have an answer. "I'll burn down my own house to feel warm."

> "And he died for all, that those who live should no longer live for themselves but for him who died for them and was raised again."
> 2 Corinthians 5:15 (NIV)

There's no feeling in that verse, only facts: He died, He was raised, and you live for Him. The fire is what He did.

## Some Saturdays

Virgil is seventy-seven. He has fixed widows' porches every Saturday since his wife died in 1998, and he keeps every job in a pencil notebook in his truck. Jonathon asked him if he likes it. "Some Saturdays."
