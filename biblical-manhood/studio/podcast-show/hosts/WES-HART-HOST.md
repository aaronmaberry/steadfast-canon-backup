## Backstory (for writers, revealed slowly over the year)

- **Grew up** on his granddad's place. His dad was steady, quiet, at every game, and almost never said what he felt. Wes loved him and never once heard "I'm proud of you" until his dad was in a hospital bed. His dad died nine years ago. This is not a father-wound story. It's a "good dad who never said it out loud" story, which is a different ache, and Jonathon's story covers the absent-father ground.
- **Thirties.** Wes made a decent living, coached Little League, sat in the third row at church, and was gone in his own living room. His wife called it "the fog years." She is never the villain in these stories. She saw it before he did.
- **The turn.** At 36, an older man at a men's breakfast (Wes calls him "Mr. Abernathy" on air, never by first name) asked him, "What are you waiting on?" Wes didn't have an answer. Mr. Abernathy met him every other Thursday for six years. He died at 88. Wes still has his coffee mug. That mug is on the set (prop lock).
- **Since then.** Twenty-plus years of men's tables. Kitchen tables, church fellowship halls, deer camp. He wrote the book because the same questions kept coming up across those tables.

## On-air persona

- **Dry, slow, warm.** A Hill Country cadence: he lets a sentence finish, smiles with his eyes before his mouth, and lands a joke flat.
- **Tells on himself first.** Every main episode has at least one Wes self-own.
- **Doesn't preach.** When he hears himself winding up, he stops: "I'm getting preachy. Jonathon, stop me."
- **Scripture.** He knows it well and quotes it plainly, with the reference said naturally ("James four, seventeen"). He's never showy about it.
- **What he's not.** A guru, a life coach, an alpha. He doesn't have every answer and says so.
- **Humor.** Domestic and dry. His truck, his knees, his sixteen-year-old's driving, being out-negotiated by his wife. Never wife-as-dummy, never mom-as-villain, never punching at young men.
- **Tells.** He turns his coffee mug a quarter turn before something serious. He says "Well." as a whole sentence.
