Good to have you here. Welcome back to Steadfast Men. This is Lesson Three. It's called The Four Marks. Here's how this goes. The word under all of it. Mark. Then four things a man does, said the way I'd say them at a kitchen table. Then the last of those four, held longer. Who the work is for. Then a shut door, a tired morning, and a harvest on his clock. Then your life as it actually is, and the move that follows.

In the lesson before this, I said it straight. You are not crazy. Those orders you were given fight each other. A plain answer is a good thing to want. You heard the four at the close, and I promised the extra time to the last one. I also gave you a move. Say the orders to one man, name the one you've been living, and trade sentences with him. So did you do it? If it only got half done, the rest is still yours. If the coffee came from your kitchen, I get it. You've met church coffee. If you hang back, I did too. I sat in the back for about ten years. I know the excuse. New to this? Start here. You can catch the earlier lessons after. You and I go one lesson at a time.

I will not hand you twelve fixes before you reach your car. You've had those lists. I've written them. I've lost most of them. A list feels great for about a day. Then an ordinary Tuesday shows up, and the list is on the counter, under the mail. I found one of mine the other day. Seven things. I'd done one of them. And the one I'd done was buy stamps. I didn't even mail anything. I just owned stamps. So no list. Four handles. One of them, held long enough that it gets into your hands.

Miss the word, and the four turn into a poster. You've got enough posters. The word is mark.

There's a doorframe in our house nobody's allowed to paint. The pantry door. The inside edge. Every birthday since my daughter could stand, she backs up against that wood. I lay a book flat on her head and make a pencil line. Her name. The date. A little crooked some years. I never see her grow on a Tuesday. Not once. I see her every single day, and every single day she looks like yesterday. Then I open the pantry for the peanut butter, and there it is. Six inches I never watched happen. Her grandparents see it. They come twice a year and say, look how tall she's gotten. And I want to say, has she? I've been standing right here. That's how a mark works on a man too. The people closest to it notice last. One year there's a note by the line, in her handwriting. It says, Dad pushed the book down. I did not push the book down. She was on her tiptoes.

A mark is what's left on a man by what he does over and over. What you feel on a good morning is not a mark. A title from a banquet is not a mark. A man who's short with his kids at supper doesn't feel a mark being made. He calls it one bad night. Then it's a long string of one bad nights. His kids are the ones holding the pencil. It cuts both ways.

So a real man has marks. Four I keep coming back to. Do them enough and they quit being a mood, and they hold up on an ordinary day. I've laid them over my own life for years. They're not always kind to me. They've always been right. Here's how I'd say them across a kitchen table. A real man doesn't sit on his hands when something's his to do. He picks up what's his, even when somebody else dropped it. He goes first, scared or not. And he's working for God's pay, not the room's.

Four marks. Every one of them is something you do, on a day you don't want to. Agreeing with me will not put one on you. The first three each get a lesson of their own, later, taken one by one. I won't crack all four open here and send you home with sermons and no practice. This lesson stays with the one a man skips. Last on the list. Easy to read it like fine print, the lines you skim to reach a signature. Stay with me. This part surprised me. A decent man can keep the first three a long time and leave God out. He moves. He carries the load. He goes first. I respect that. The fourth mark shows who he is actually working for. I had not really asked. Maybe you haven't either.

Hebrews chapter eleven, verse six. "And without faith it is impossible to please God, because anyone who comes to him must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who earnestly seek him." Two things in that verse. That he is. And that he rewards. You'd sign the first one without blinking. God exists. Sure. Reward sounds a little mercenary. Like you're only in it for the paycheck. Like the kid who asks what he gets before he'll take out the trash. The verse doesn't blush. A man who comes to God has to believe God pays. Not maybe. Must. If you believe God pays, you can do a costly thing, and the room doesn't have to settle it. You work for some kind of pay. So do I. Nobody repeats the hard thing for nothing. The question is who you expect the pay from. If the answer isn't God, you go collect somewhere else. The man you report to. A good review. The group text. A nod from family. None of that is evil. Thanks is a gift. Thanks makes a lousy wage. When thanks is the wage, you quit the day it stops. And it stops. It always stops. People get busy. They get used to you. The good you do turns into furniture. Nobody thanks the couch. A man needs a pay that doesn't run out.

I'll tell on myself. My daughter needed a desk a while back. The flat-pack kind. About a thousand pieces, and one tiny wrench. I built it after she went to bed. Took me till almost one in the morning. Put a lamp on it. Squared up the chair. Hid the leftover screws, because there are always leftover screws. Next morning I'm in the kitchen, hovering. Coffee in my hand. Standing right where I can see her door. Waiting. I refilled that coffee twice. I didn't want the coffee. I wanted a reason to be standing there. My wife walked through and read the whole thing in about a second. She patted my arm. Didn't say a word. Then my daughter comes out, walks straight past the desk, and asks if we have any more of the good cereal. I felt this little drop. Grown man. Pouting over particleboard. I said it out loud, to nobody. Huh, looks like somebody's got a new desk. She did thank me later that day. I hadn't built that desk for her. Not all the way. Part of me built it for the moment she'd see it. The moment came late. I wanted a refund. Have you hovered like that? Fixed a thing, then hung around until somebody found it? Yeah. Same. That's the wrong pay. It looks exactly like a good dad. It runs out a lot faster.

Lonnie sat in a group with me for years. He was not a talker. Big hands. Quiet voice. The kind of man who fixes your mower and won't let you pay him. Same corner every time. About four sentences a night, and every one of them was worth the wait. Lonnie's dad got sick. Kidney trouble. Dialysis. Monday, Wednesday, Friday. Six in the morning. Lonnie drove him. Every one of those mornings, a little over four years. Four hours in the chair each time. Then Lonnie went to work and made the hours up on the back end. His wife held the house together on those mornings. He'd tell you that first.

At first, people noticed. Church asked about his dad. Somebody brought a casserole. A good one, he said. Chicken and rice. Two friends told him he was a good son. That helps. I won't pretend it doesn't. By the second year, nobody asked. They hadn't turned cold. It had stopped being news. His dad was never an easy man. The route. The radio. The turn into the clinic lot. Four years of that, and I don't think he heard thank you twice. Fair to his dad, though. Sick, scared, and hooked to a machine. I'd complain about the turn too. One night Lonnie said, it's not the driving. I can do the driving. I'm just the only one who knows I'm doing it.

Then he told what happened next. One of those mornings he got to the clinic early. The doors weren't open yet. His dad was dozing in the passenger seat. Engine running. Heater on. Still dark. A nurse sat in her car two spots over, eating a breakfast sandwich, waiting on the same doors. Lonnie said he started talking to God. Not a pretty prayer. He said, Lord, nobody knows I'm here. Right in the middle of that sentence, it hit him. That's not true. One does. He sat there till the lights came on inside. Didn't say anything else. He just knew it. He told it in maybe six sentences, then went quiet. The mornings didn't get shorter. His dad didn't get sweeter. Church never threw him a dinner. He quit driving for a thank you he was never gonna get. He started driving for a Father who was already watching. His dad passed a while after that. At the service, nobody said a word about the driving. Not one person. I asked him later. I figured it would sting. He shrugged. He said, Wes, I didn't need them to say it. It's already written down. Lonnie didn't get that mark by feeling holy. Most of those mornings he felt tired, and a little mad. He got it by pulling into that lot something like six hundred times. That's a mark.

Matthew chapter six, verse six. "But when you pray, go into your room, close the door and pray to your Father, who is unseen. Then your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you." Close the door. Jesus assumes you've got a life behind a door. A part nobody claps for. He doesn't treat that part as second string. He says that's where the Father is paying attention. Your Father sees. Your Father rewards. Same verse. Same breath. That door is not a hiding place. Last lesson, hiding was what you did with the wrong. You shut a door so the wrong stayed covered. This is the other door. You shut it on the good you did, so the only one who pays you is him.

Most of what makes a man happens behind some door. A kitchen at five thirty. The drive home, when you pick which version of you is going to walk in. The apology nobody else knew you owed. The text you sent a man who's been quiet too long. The screen you left shut. I'm going to ask you something. Keep the answer to yourself. Did you do something good lately that nobody knows about? Keep it quiet. Saying it spends it. That quiet is the fourth mark. You might be thinking it wasn't much. It doesn't have to be big. It has to be his. Behind the door is not the leftover part of your life. It may be the realest part. Your Father has been watching that part the whole time.

I'll be honest about mine. Ask me which of the four is thinnest on me, and it's this one. I'll do the good thing. I'm pretty reliable about the doing. Then I go looking for the receipt. I mention it sideways. I'll say, man, long day, had to run over and jump Jim's battery. True. I didn't need to say it. I was fishing. Worst part, I usually catch something. Somebody says, man, that was good of you. I feel great for about ten minutes. My wife has a look for it. Not a mean look. The look you give a dog who brings you the same stick for the fourth time. I've done it at church. Stacked chairs a little louder than the job required. Clang. Anybody see this? Clang. I trade what the Father was keeping for a nod over supper. Bad trade. I make it all the time. Maybe that's you. Maybe another of the four is the thin one. I went first with mine, so yours has a place to land.

Galatians chapter six, verse nine. "Let us not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up." Paul says weary like he's already met you. Doing good gets old. The same drive. The same dishes. The same prayer, for the same kid, who doesn't seem to be listening. He is not scolding you for being tired. Tired is not the end of the story. Notice what he leaves out. He never says you will see the harvest right away. He says, at the proper time. His time. Sometimes not in this life at all. I've got a prayer like that. Same man in my family. Same request. A long time now. Some mornings I say it and it feels like I'm talking to the ceiling. I say it anyway. I don't say it because I feel holy. I say it because the proper time is his to pick. It's that pencil line on the pantry door. Nothing shows until somebody opens it. He names one condition. You don't quit. He does not require a perfect record, and he does not require a feeling. That fourth mark holds the other three up. Moving when you'd rather wait. Picking up what's yours. Going first once the talk dies down. Each one costs you on a day nobody says thank you. Drop the fourth and they quit when the pay quits. Keep it, and the reason to continue doesn't ask who's watching.

This hits a different spot, depending on the life you're in. If you're young, a lot of the good goes unseen. Your boss may not see it. Home may not. Do it anyway. You're building a man nobody has met yet. If you're single, don't wait on a shared house. The hidden work counts now. An apartment has a door. If you're a new dad, a lot of the best work happens at three in the morning. Nobody awake but you and a very small critic. If you're married, she doesn't owe you a parade. If she thanks you, take it. Don't build the house on it. Love her with the Father keeping the score. If you've been hurt, maybe nobody ever saw you. Not your dad. Not anybody. I won't rush past that. Your Father has never once looked away. You were never invisible to him. If you're older, and the world has quit handing you credit, that may be the best news in a long while. You get to work for the only one who was really paying. A younger man is watching how you carry that. If the job is paying well, be careful. That pay can turn into the only pay you want. A strong year can still leave a man unfaithful. I've known both in one life, and I've known the paycheck alone. He was the loneliest one at the cookout.

Let me land it. A steadfast man works for God's pay, not the room's. God exists, and he rewards. He sees behind the door. The harvest comes at his time, if you don't quit. Don't leave guilty for wanting thanks. I wanted it. I hovered over a desk. Send the bill to another place. The late night. The early drive. The dishes. The good nobody saw. None of that was wasted. None of it went unseen. Lonnie was right. Somebody is keeping the record. Better than a program, and better than a toast the day you retire.
