The question God asked a man who was already found. The nicer names a wait borrows. Patience. Peace. Getting ready. Even faith. What the people near you learn from it, and a man who moved three years late. One name, one small move, and the noon you do it by.

Lesson Three stayed on the last mark, the question of who pays a man. The other three were left for their own hours. This is the first of them. You met the name on a quiet Tuesday. Today it gets the hour. Did you do the quiet thing? If you did, keep it quiet. Don't tell me. First time through? Jump in. The earlier lessons are not a test you failed. Pull your chair in a little. Closer than feels natural. You and I stay with one lesson at a time.

I've got to start with a confession about my garage. There's a stack of floor tile out there, enough to redo our downstairs bathroom. I bought it on sale, forty percent off. It's been out there so long that nobody in my house calls it the tile anymore. My wife and I call it the table. She sets the groceries on it. Last month she set a lamp there. I think the lamp means I lost.

That stack is a small picture. Nothing's broken. Nothing's lost. It's waiting on me. I've let it wait so long it quit looking like a decision. I'd bet you've got a stack too. Maybe not in a garage. Maybe a conversation you keep walking around.

When you hear passive, you probably picture a man on a couch. Remote on his chest. Blinds down. Anyone can see him. He has the better shot, because he knows he's on the couch. The man I worry about is up before the alarm. Good at his job. The one people call. Jumper cables in his truck, a tarp, and a spare everything. If your car dies in a parking lot, he's the one you want walking by. A short list in his life has been waiting for years. Not the gutters. The big ones. The call back to the doctor. The apology he owes his sister. The friend who's drinking again, and doesn't know that this man knows.

One of those is in your head right now. No details. Just notice it. Yeah. Hold it a second. That man is busy. He isn't lazy. You'd drive across town at midnight for a buddy with a flat. Let it go. Busy is the best hiding place I know. Nobody gets in trouble for being needed. Busy gets you thanked. Busy gets you a plaque. I've never seen a plaque for the call a man finally makes to his brother. I answer work email in about ninety seconds. Kind of proud of that. My brother left me a voicemail back in the spring. I still haven't called him back. I've listened to it six times. Six times I could have called him.

One of the first questions God ever asked a man is in Genesis chapter three, verse nine. "But the Lord God called to the man, Where are you?" Here's what gets me. God wasn't lost. He wasn't squinting through the trees, going, Adam? Buddy? God knew exactly where the man was standing. The question wasn't for God. It was for the man. Notice who God called. Not the serpent. Not the tree. The man. God came looking for the one he had put in charge of that garden. When something's drifting in a house, God doesn't usually start with the kids. You ask a question you already know so the other person hears himself say it. You want the ownership.

I do it with my kids. Where are your shoes? I know where they are. They're in the middle of the hallway, right where I'm about to step in the dark. I'm not asking for directions. I'm asking them to walk over there and deal with it. That's how God comes after a man who's parked. You can argue with a lecture. A question, you have to answer. Quit standing behind all the good stuff, and answer from where you actually are. You may be standing in a very full life. On one thing, you haven't moved in years. Not because you don't care. Moving would cost you something, and standing still hasn't sent a bill yet. God knows which thing. You already know it too.

Waiting hardly ever calls itself waiting. It borrows a better word, something that sounds good on a Sunday. Most days the word is patience. I'm giving it time. Sometimes that's true. Some things do need a minute. A fence post needs a minute to set. A lot of the time a man asks the calendar to do a job only he can do. The calendar can't apologize to your sister. It turns the page. Your sister is still sitting on the other side.

Or the word is peace. I don't want to stir anything up. The house stays calm, and a little lonely, like a waiting room with nice magazines. If you're married, you know this one. She brought a thing up once, maybe twice. You nodded and said, yeah, we should talk about that. Three Christmases ago. She hasn't brought it up since. It didn't get fixed. She figured out where it goes when she does. You call that peace. She calls it something else. Settled is peace. Shelved is this.

Maybe you went quiet for a reason. Maybe you grew up in a loud house, where the next thing said out loud was always the wrong thing. You spoke up once, it went bad, and quiet started to feel like the smart play. I'm not going to bark at you for that. It kept you safe once. I get that. If the danger is gone and the quiet stayed, what's left is a habit with good manners.

Sometimes the word is humility. Who am I to bring it up? I'm not the expert. Somebody else is closer to it. You're her husband. That's who. You're his dad. You're the one who saw it. If you saw it, you're already in it.

Sometimes the word is getting ready. This one is mine. I researched kayaks for about two years. Read the reviews. Watched the videos. Knew the weight of every model down to the ounce. I own zero kayaks. I have never been more prepared to not be on a lake. I could tell you which paddle to buy. I just couldn't tell you what the water feels like. A man can get really good at studying a thing he never does. Kayaks can take that. A marriage can't. A kid can't. The friend who's drinking can't. I've known a man who read three books on being a dad and hasn't asked his kid a real question since spring. Some do the same with church. They're going to come back. They're looking at a few places. They've been looking for three years.

If you're young, or you're single, there's a special version. It sounds like, once I. Once I get the better job. Once I find the right girl. Once I've got a little money saved. Once I've got my head on straight. Somehow that sentence never gets to the second half. Nobody is coming with a permission slip that says you can start your own life now. You started it a while ago. The man you'll be in ten years is getting built right now, on the days you keep calling practice. It's already running.

Then there's the one with the best excuse of all. Faith. I'm waiting on the Lord. Scripture says to wait on the Lord, and I won't take that from you. It's all over the Psalms. Maybe you honestly don't know the next step. That's real. Ask the Lord. Ask a brother. Stay on your feet while you ask. There's another kind. The next step is sitting right there, and a man pins God's name on the delay. That's a heavy name to pin on something. God never signed it.

Joshua chapter eighteen, verse three. "So Joshua said to the Israelites: How long will you wait before you begin to take possession of the land that the Lord, the God of your ancestors, has given you?" Hear that last part. Has given you. The land was already theirs. God had done his part. The promising part. The going-ahead part. All of it. They were camped out, waiting to feel like it. They were waiting on themselves.

Here's a test. You can do it in the truck. When you say you're waiting on God, ask whether some piece of it already has your name on it. If it does, do that piece. The piece you can see. Not the whole map. You can trust God for the rain and still put the seed in the ground. Ask yourself Joshua's question. How long. Not a mood. A number. Months. Years. I did this with the tile. Eleven months. For tile. A thing I could have finished in a day. I had to go dig up the order confirmation. Be honest. Rounding down is just one more way to wait.

The people near you learn your wait, even if you never say a word about it. My daughter asked me to put a shelf up in her room. Simple job. Two brackets, one board. I've hung heavier things with worse tools. I said, Saturday, kiddo. I said it a few Saturdays in a row. More than a few. One morning my phone buzzes, middle of a meeting. A calendar invite, from my own kid. Subject line. Dad, the shelf. Saturday, ten in the morning. She even set a reminder, fifteen minutes before. I laughed, and then I didn't. She is twelve years old, and she had already figured out that asking me wasn't enough. She had to manage me. She's better at it than my boss.

They start guessing about the man who won't move, and they stay quiet. Is he mad? Is he tired? Does he even care about this? That man was me. I did care. I cared from a distance. After a while the guessing stops, and a life gets built around the hole where you should be. Kids do it. Wives do it. Friends do it fastest. A friend who can't reach you stops trying and calls it being busy too. I put the shelf up that Saturday, ten in the morning. She stood in the doorway the whole time, handing me screws like a surgical nurse. It took twenty minutes. Twenty minutes. She didn't need a shelf that bad. She needed to know that when she asked, I'd move. I'd been protecting that for a month.

A man I'll call Curtis coached youth baseball for close to twenty years. If you had a kid in that town, Curtis probably taught him to quit flinching at a fastball. Good man. Honest man. Every practice, a bucket of balls and a thermos. A few years before I met him, his wife asked if they could see a counselor. Nothing was on fire. She felt like they'd turned into roommates. Nice roommates. Curtis said sure, after the season, and he meant it. Then fall ball. Then a travel team needed a coach. Then spring came back. Every time she asked, the kids needed him, and they did. The worst day wasn't a fight. It was the day she quit asking. He came home and she had moved her things into the guest room. No yelling. She said, I'm tired of waiting on the season to be over.

Curtis sat on it two days. He told me he kept waiting to feel ready, for a morning he'd wake up knowing exactly what to say. That morning never showed up. A Thursday did. He got to the field early, sat in the dugout, phone in his hand, ball bag at his feet, and called the counselor's office. His hands were sweating so bad he almost dropped the phone in the dirt. The lady said, we've got Monday at four. Curtis said, I've got practice. Then he heard himself. He said, no. We'll be there. He told his wife that night. She didn't cry. She didn't hug him. She said okay. The first appointment was, his word, awful. Long silences. Curtis said he'd rather take a line drive off the shin. They went back, and they kept going back. About a year in, she said something he wrote down. She said, I quit waiting on you a long time ago. I'm glad you came anyway.

Curtis wasn't short on love. He was short on the first move. He had been calling that shortage by a nice name. The kids need me. He waited to feel ready, and ready never came first. It doesn't. Ready shows up after you move. Usually a while after. Sometimes a year after. Late still counted. If you feel late, hear that. Curtis made that call three years behind, and it still mattered. The call took four minutes. Three years of waiting, and the move itself was four minutes on a dugout bench. Most of what you're waiting on is like that. The dread is huge. The move is small. Lopsided. A man measures the dread, and skips the move.

James doesn't give you a soft place to land on this. The seventeenth verse of James chapter four. "If anyone, then, knows the good they ought to do and doesn't do it, it is sin for them." Hard verse. It takes the middle away. A man likes a middle, where he skips the wrong thing, skips the right thing, and holds. James says there isn't one. If you know it and you're sitting on it, God has a word for that. The word isn't careful. Name the thing small enough that a morning could hold it. Your whole marriage is too big for a Thursday. A four-minute phone call can fit. Find the smallest move that is still a real move. Finding the number doesn't count. The call does. Do it before noon. By about three in the afternoon, my excuses have had all day to get dressed, and they sound responsible. You've practiced waiting for years. The first real move will feel like you cut in line. You didn't. You finally took your turn.

The same wait shows up in every kind of house. It changes its name at the door. In a young man's apartment, it's called someday. Someday stays busy. He's never home. With a new baby, it's called when things settle down. They won't. I checked. With a big career, it's called next quarter, and next quarter has a next quarter. For the man worn out at church, it's called let somebody else. There's always a somebody else, until one day there isn't. After a divorce, it's called when I'm not so angry. You might wait a long time to feel fair about it. If you're hurting, go slow. One small true thing is plenty. A text. A coffee. Frozen and healed are two different things. You don't have to thaw all at once.

If you're older, nobody asked me. The job's done. The kids are grown. You're waiting on somebody to invite you to matter. That invite is not coming. You still matter. People don't think to ask. A younger man doesn't know how to knock, so go find one. Ask him how he's actually doing, and stay for the answer. I know a man in his seventies who got tired of waiting to be asked. He started showing up early to set out the chairs. Within a month, three younger ones were showing up early too. They didn't come for the chairs. Different houses. Same wait. Your Father is the only one in the house allowed to wait on you, and he has been more patient than you or I deserve.

If you hold on to one thing, hold on to this. Waiting almost always wears a nicer name. Patience. Peace. Humility. Getting ready. Even faith. Under the nice name is something you already know is yours, and you haven't touched it. You could find it with the lights off. Four marks. A man who won't let his life idle. A man who shoulders his own load. A man who walks into the hard thing ahead of the people he loves. A man content to be paid by God. Today was the first. A steadfast man stops waiting. The other three get stuck behind this one.

Leave the guilt here. Guilt is heavy, and it doesn't move anything. It just sits there with you. Walk out with one name and one move. Small enough to do the minute you walk out. You don't need a new you. You need one thing you quit walking past. The tile comes off the table this Saturday. My wife has heard that before. More than once. So now you've heard it too.

Let me pray, and then the rest of this is yours. Father, you heard the name the man in front of me is holding. You know how long. The wait has gone on so long it feels like furniture. Give him four minutes of courage. On a real day, before lunch. Give him the first move before he feels ready, and meet him on the other side of it. If he isn't sure about you yet, be patient with him the way you've been patient with me. Let him see you're worth moving toward. Amen.

If a man tells you his number, don't flinch. Don't fix it. Don't top it with your own. He just did the hard part. You don't have to agree it's a big deal. You just have to hear it.
