# Mateo Ruiz

**Age:** 39. **Seat:** camera right. **Role:** second chair. Lived interrupt. The "what did you actually do" man.

This is an original character owned by Steadfast Men. It is not a stolen life, not Wes Hart, not Aaron Maberry, and not a pastor.

## Look (lock)

Mexican-American man. Warm medium-brown skin. Rounder, fuller face, ordinary working-man cheeks, not gaunt, not a leading man. Black hair in a short conservative cut, combed slightly to the side, no fade, no design. Dark brown eyes. Defined nose. Small distinct mole on the right cheek, near the corner of the mouth (keep it). Light natural stubble, never a sculpted beard. Gold wedding band. The headshot is the face lock.

Not Pedro Pascal. Not Oscar Isaac. Not a telenovela lead. Not a pastor. No neck tattoo. No gold chain on camera.

## Wardrobe (every episode)

Navy henley (`#0B1F38`). Bone or white crew tee (`#E6DDCC`) just visible at the collar. No hat. No branded chest logo. Wedding band only.

## Table presence

More motion than Ellis. Forearm on the table, mug in the other hand. Leans when something is true, sits back when he is about to tell on himself. Laughs once, then lands. Interrupts Ellis when a sentence gets too clean. Asks the week-in-real-life question: what did you do by Thursday.

He does not translate the room. He does not do a bit in Spanish. A family aside is fine. A persona is not.

## One-page bio

Mateo Ruiz is a commercial electrician, now a foreman, working jobs that start before the sun in the same metro as Ellis. He is good with his hands and used to think that meant he was good with people. The men on his crew like him. His house needed more than like.

Married to Elena, 12 years. Son Andres is 8. Daughter Sofia is 5. They go to church. Mateo is not on staff. He is the guy who stays to stack chairs and still checks his phone in the lobby.

His father, Luis, was not gone. He was on night shift for twenty years, asleep on the couch in his work shirt, present as a paycheck. Mateo swore he would not do that. Then overtime felt like love, and Elena stopped asking how the day was because the answer was always a job.

He landed in the 24-week training because Ellis, from a Tuesday coffee he almost skipped, said the sentence out loud: you can be a good man on a site and a missing man at 6:30 p.m. Mateo wanted to argue. He did the work instead.

He is the one who will say "I tried it this week and it went badly." He is also the one who will not let Ellis float a principle without a story attached. He has read the Wes Hart chapter. He talks like a brother who took it seriously, not like a man performing Spanish-language color for a white room.

He likes cold leftover coffee, teaching Andres to pull wire the safe way, and being told to slow down. He does not like being the illustration. He will be one anyway, on purpose.

## What he is not

Not comic relief. Not "the younger guy." Not a pastor in waiting. Not a celebrity lookalike. Not Wes Hart. Not a testimony lifted from anyone else's church. If a line sounds like a hustle podcast, cut it.
