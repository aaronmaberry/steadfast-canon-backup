# 2. You Are Not Crazy

A man in his early thirties sat across from me at a diner a few years ago and said something I've heard in one form or another from men of every age since. He said, "I feel like I've been handed six different instruction manuals for being a man and every one of them contradicts the other five, and I'm supposed to act like that makes sense."

He wasn't angry when he said it. He was tired, in the particular way a man gets tired when he's been trying to hit a target that keeps moving. He'd been told to be strong and to be vulnerable. He'd been told to lead and to get out of the way. He'd been told that his instincts as a man were dangerous and that his failure to act on them was the problem with his marriage. He'd been told by one voice that manhood was a costume the culture put on boys, and by another that it was the most important thing about him, and by a third that it was whatever he wanted it to be, and by a fourth that it had been settled long ago and he was simply failing at it. He'd sat in churches that treated him as a problem to be managed and in locker rooms that treated him as an appetite to be fed, and somewhere in all of it he'd started to wonder whether the confusion was in the world or in him.

I told him what I'll tell you. The confusion is in the world. You are not crazy.

## The picture you were handed

Every man alive today was handed a picture of manhood that doesn't hold together, and most of us have spent a lot of energy trying to make it hold together anyway, and blaming ourselves when it wouldn't. I want to spend this chapter looking at the picture plainly, so you can stop blaming yourself for the fact that it doesn't work.

Start with what you got from home. Chapter 8 will go deep on this, but for now it's enough to say that whatever sat in the chair at the end of your childhood table taught you a picture of a man before you could read, and for most of us it was a partial picture at best. A father who was there and silent taught you that a man is a presence without words. A father who was gone taught you that a man is an absence with a name. A father who was a storm taught you that a man is a weather system to be read. Even a good father taught you a picture with gaps in it shaped exactly like the places he couldn't reach. So you started with a fragment, and then you went out into the world to fill it in.

The world had plenty to offer, none of it consistent. The culture at large told you, more or less, that manhood is a problem. That the things in you that want to build and compete and protect and take charge are the residue of something the species should have outgrown. That your strength is suspect and your silence is suspect and your anger is proof, and that the best thing you can do as a man is become less of one. Then that same culture turned around and mocked men who were passive, who couldn't commit, who wouldn't grow up, who lived in basements, and it never once seemed to notice it had asked for exactly that.

The entertainment you grew up on gave you a man who was either a fool, a brute, or a lone hero who needed no one and answered to nothing. The fool was the father in every sitcom, well-meaning and useless, managed by his wife and outsmarted by his kids. The brute was the villain, or sometimes the hero, and the difference was mostly whose side he was on. The lone hero was the one every boy wanted to be, and the trouble with him is that he has no wife who's tired, no father who calls twice a year, no son who won't talk, no church, no brothers, and no Tuesdays. He is a man with no house, and you were being trained to admire him while you were living in one.

The marketplace told you a man is what he earns and drives and owns. The internet told you a man is what he watches and how much of it. Some corners of the church told you a man is a spiritual leader, and then gave you a picture of leadership so soft and so vague that you couldn't have told a grown man what to actually do on a Tuesday if your life depended on it. Other corners told you a man is a warrior and a king, and gave you a picture so loud and so wild that it had no room in it for a man with a mortgage and a sick mother and a job at a wastewater plant.

And then there's the shelf of books for men, and I want to be careful here because some of those books were kind to me when I was young. Many of them start inside the chest. They tell you about your heart, your longings, your wildness, the adventure you were made for. Some of that is true. But a great many men have read those books and felt every wild thing they promised and gone home and let their wives carry the whole house alone, because what's in a man's chest doesn't leave a mark on his life by itself. Only what he does leaves a mark. A picture of manhood that lives entirely in the interior is a picture with no Tuesdays in it, and a man can't live in a picture with no Tuesdays.

Put all of that together, the fragment from home and the six contradictory manuals from the world, and you have the picture most men are trying to live up to. It's no wonder you couldn't live up to it, because nobody could, and the reason is that it was never a picture at all. It's a pile.

## What the confusion does to a man

I've watched the pile do three things to men, and you've probably felt all three.

It makes a man wait. When you don't know what a man is supposed to do, the safest thing is to do nothing and see what happens. A lot of male passivity, which we'll spend a whole chapter on, isn't laziness at all. It's a man standing very still because every direction he could move has been declared wrong by somebody. He isn't on the tailgate because he's lazy. He's on the tailgate because he's confused and he's decided that confused men shouldn't drive.

It makes a man perform. If you can't find the real target, you learn to hit whatever target the room in front of you seems to want. You're one man at church and another at work and another with your buddies and another with your wife, and none of them is quite you, and you're exhausted from the costume changes. A performer isn't a liar, exactly. He's a man who never got a stable picture and so learned to borrow one from whoever was watching.

And it makes a man quietly ashamed. This is the one I see most. A man who's been handed a contradictory picture and can't live up to it doesn't usually conclude that the picture is broken. He concludes that he is. He carries a low hum of failure under everything, a sense that other men must have figured out what he can't, and he doesn't talk about it because talking about it would confirm it. The man at the diner had that hum. Most of the men I sit with do, and it took me a long time to realize that it wasn't a sign of anything wrong with them. It was the sound of a good man trying to obey six manuals at once.

## What's actually true

Here is what I want to say to that hum, and I'll say it slowly.

You are not crazy for sensing that manhood is a real thing and not a costume. It is a real thing, and God made it. In the first chapter of the Bible He made human beings in His image, male and female, on purpose, and He called it good before anyone had done anything with it. Your manhood is not a problem to be solved or a residue to be outgrown. It's a way of carrying the image of God that was His idea, and the instinct in you that says it matters is right.

You are not crazy for sensing that most of what you've been told about it doesn't hold together, because it doesn't. You were handed a pile, and you've been trying to build a house out of it, and the fact that the house keeps falling down is not evidence against you. It's evidence against the pile.

You are not crazy for wanting somebody to just tell you, plainly, what a man does. That's the right want. It's the want of every boy who ever looked at his father and the want of every man who ever sat across a table from an older one and asked a real question. The wanting is not weakness; it's the design, because men were meant to be handed a picture by other men, and most of us weren't, and we've been guessing ever since.

And you are not crazy for suspecting that the answer, when it comes, is going to be simpler and harder than anything on the shelf. Simpler, because it can be said in one sentence. Harder, because the sentence has to be lived on Tuesdays, and no feeling in your chest and no burst on a big day can do that for you.

## The pile at every age

The contradiction lands differently depending on where you're standing, and I want you to see that you aren't alone in it, whatever your age.

At seventeen a boy I know is being told by his school that his restlessness is a disorder, by his phone that a man is whatever gets the most attention, by his youth pastor that a man is a servant leader, and by the older boys that a man is whoever's had the most girls. He's trying to be all of that at once and he thinks the fact that he can't is a problem with him. It isn't. He's a boy standing under a pile, and the pile is the problem.

At thirty a young father is being told to be present and to provide, to lead his home and to defer to his wife's instincts, to be strong for his newborn and to be in touch with his own feelings about becoming a father, all in the same week, all by people who mean well. He's failing at most of it because it can't all be done, and the hum of failure under his exhaustion is not evidence that he's a bad father. It's the sound of a good man trying to obey six manuals with a baby on his shoulder.

At forty-eight a man has quietly given up on the question. He's decided that manhood is whatever he happens to be doing, which is mostly working and coming home tired, and he's stopped listening to anyone who claims to know what a man is because every one of them has contradicted the last one. His resignation looks like peace, and it isn't peace. It's a man who set down the pile and sat on it.

At seventy a man who's lived by a picture handed to him in the 1960s is watching that picture get mocked on every screen his grandchildren look at, and he's begun to wonder whether the whole thing he built his life on was a mistake. It wasn't. Some of what he was handed was true and some of it wasn't, like every generation's picture, and the true parts are still true. He needs somebody to sort it with him, not somebody to tell him it was all wrong.

Four men under the same pile. None of them is crazy, and none of them is alone.

## What this book is going to do about it

I'm going to hand you a picture. Not the whole thing at once; it'll take the rest of the book to fill in. But the frame of it is one sentence, and I'll give you the sentence in the next chapter and then spend four chapters taking it apart piece by piece so you can see how each piece looks on a man in the middle of an ordinary week.

The sentence doesn't start in your chest. It starts with what you do. It's the kind of picture a boy could hold up against his own father and a man could hold up against his own week and get a straight answer. It has room in it for the man with the mortgage and the sick mother and the job at the plant, and for the single man at the back table, and for the divorced man in the apartment, and for the old man who just turned in his badge. It doesn't require you to feel wild or to feel anything in particular. It requires you to get up.

And it's older than any of the manuals you were handed. Men have been living it for a very long time in every century and every kind of house, and the reason it has survived is that it's true, and true things survive. You weren't given it because the chain of men who were supposed to hand it to you got broken somewhere back there, and nobody's fault is the point right now. The point is that the chain can be picked up.

I want to say one more thing to the man at the diner, and to you if you're him.

The tiredness you feel isn't the tiredness of a man who's failed. It's the tiredness of a man who's been carrying a contradiction. When you set the contradiction down, which is what this chapter has been trying to help you do, you're going to find that you have more strength than you thought, because you've been spending most of it holding up a pile. Set it down and let the pieces fall, and then come with me to the next chapter and pick up something that holds.

## Before you turn the page

If the hum has been under your life for a long time, I'd like you to let it get quieter tonight, not because you've solved anything but because you've seen the pile for what it is. You're a man who was handed a contradiction and carried it honestly, and that isn't failure; it's strength spent on the wrong load. Set it down. The next chapter hands you something that holds, and I've watched it hold for men of every age you just read about. May God give you rest from carrying what was never yours to carry, and the courage to pick up what is.
