# Write the Next Move

Hollis carries a notebook in his shirt pocket, about the size of a deck of cards, and he's carried one like it since he was nineteen. Every night before bed he writes down tomorrow's first three jobs. Not a to-do list. Three things, in order.

I asked him once why he still does it at seventy-four.

"Because if it isn't written down, it isn't going to happen," he said. "A man who knows what he's going to do tomorrow gets up and does it. A man who's going to figure it out in the morning figures out that he'd rather have another cup of coffee."

He flipped it open and showed me: fix gate latch, call Cole, read Proverbs 14. Fifty-five years of the next thing, written down the night before, and it's why he's Hollis.

If you close this book with a feeling and nothing written down, you'll have a marker with no Tuesday after it, and by Christmas you won't be able to say what it said. So before you close the cover, write the next move. Not a life plan. The next move.

## Make it plain

God tells men to write things down. He told Habakkuk to.

:::margin
Habakkuk 2:2 (NIV)
"Then the LORD replied: 'Write down the revelation and make it plain on tablets so that a herald may run with it.'"
:::

Write it down, make it plain, make it so someone can run with it: a plan in one sentence. Men fail at plans in two directions. Some write nothing, leave the retreat with tears in their eyes and resolve in their gut, and the resolve lasts until the first hard week, because nothing was written and so nothing was real. Others write forty pages of life vision and mission statements, and it goes in a drawer and dies of its own weight, because no man can run with forty pages.

The Bible's version is a tablet, one surface, plain words. Nehemiah rebuilt a wall in fifty-two days because he prayed for months, walked the wall at night to see exactly what was broken, asked the king for exactly what he needed, and put every family on its own section. When men tried to pull him off, he had a sentence ready.

:::margin
Nehemiah 6:3 (NIV)
"I am carrying on a great project and cannot go down. Why should the work stop while I leave it and go down to you?"
:::

A man who has written his plan knows what the project is and what's a distraction from it, and can say so.

## One page

One page, or an index card if you write small. Every line is something you've already been asked to name at the end of a stretch. You've done the thinking. Now make it plain.

Start with the mark you're faintest on, in one line: "Of the four, the one I'm missing most is ___." The four are reject passivity, accept responsibility, lead courageously, and expect God's reward. Pick one.

Then your exit, in one word: anger, the itch, or the drift.

Then your season and its assignment, in one sentence with a verb in it. "I'm retired, and my assignment is to assist: two younger men and a grandson." "I'm single again in year one, and my assignment is to drive straight and slow and call my kids every week."

Then your brother, as a name and a day and a time: "Dale, Thursdays at noon, by phone." If you don't have a name yet, the line reads "Ask ___ by Friday." That's the move.

Then your man ahead, as a name and the first question you'll ask him. If you're the older man, the line flips: the younger man's name and what you'll offer him.

Then your rhythm: five to ten unwitnessed things that don't move, scaled to your season.

Then your house's next move: one thing for her, or for the wife who isn't here yet; one thing for each child, or for a fatherless kid if you have none; and a date for the day, if you have a son or daughter approaching the crossing.

Then your work's next move, in one line. "Do the plant with all my heart for the Lord." "Tell my wife the truth about my hours."

Then the drawer you're opening, in one line with a name attached: what's hidden, and which Nathan you're telling. Men skip this line. Don't.

And then the next move: one thing, this week, the single first action from everything above. Not the most important thing; the first. "Ask Dale." "Put the phone in the kitchen tonight."

At the bottom, sign it and date it. Men need markers, and this is one.

## What a move is

I keep saying move rather than goal, and the difference matters. A goal is a destination: be a better husband, get closer to God, fix things with my dad. Goals are fine and useless, because you can't do a goal. You can only do a move.

A move has three parts: a verb, a person, and a day. "Ask my wife what she most needs from me, Thursday, after the kids are down." "Call my father Sunday and tell him one thing I'm grateful for." Every one of those you either did or you didn't.

Hollis has never written "be a better man" in fifty-five years. He has written something like twenty thousand small moves, and the better man is what they added up to. Solomon knew the difference.

:::margin
Proverbs 21:5 (NIV)
"The plans of the diligent lead to profit as surely as haste leads to poverty."
:::

The plans of the diligent, not the dreams of the inspired. Diligence is a weekday word.

## Why plans die

I've watched what kills a page like this.

Plans die when they're too big. Write ten things, or five, that read in ninety seconds in a truck.

Plans die when nobody sees them. A plan in a drawer is a wish. Show yours to your brother and give him the right to ask about it.

Plans die when there's no date. "Soon" is not a day. Thursday is a day.

Plans die when they were made in a feeling, the retreat high, the last page of a book. That's all right, as long as you review the page on a plain weekday when the feeling has gone. Most of it will hold. Cross out the rest.

Plans die when nobody reviews them. Every ninety days, sit down with the page and your brother and ask what got done, what changed, and what the next move is now. Then write a fresh page.

And plans die when God isn't in them, which is the one men miss most.

:::margin
James 4:15 (NIV)
"Instead, you ought to say, 'If it is the Lord's will, we will live and do this or that.'"
:::

If it is the Lord's will isn't a disclaimer for the bottom of the page; it's the posture of the whole page. You're handing the page to God, and He's allowed to change it. A plan held loosely in an open hand is a plan God can use. A plan gripped in a fist is an idol with bullet points.

:::margin
Proverbs 16:9 (NIV)
"In their hearts humans plan their course, but the LORD establishes their steps."
:::

You plan, on paper, in plain words. He establishes the steps, deciding which of your moves land and when. Write the page, then pray over it and let Him hold it.

## The page scales

If there's a baby in your house, your page is three lines, and that's wisdom, not failure: the counter time with her, the shift you take, the brother on the phone at nine.

If you're single again, year one's page has a rule at the top: no turns. Then the floor: the weekly call to the kids, the weekend that never gets traded, the brother, church even when it's awkward. Year two's page can have the rebuild on it.

If you're the older man, your page has three lines a younger man's can't: the hill you're asking God for, the younger man you're walking toward, and the conversation you've been putting off with a grown child, with a date. Roy's page said "Say it to my son on the porch" for two months, and he did it because it was written and Hollis asked him about it every Thursday.

If you're a widower, your page may be the shortest of all. Mr. Pruitt's first page after his wife died said, in its entirety, "Coffee with Ed Wednesdays. Trees."

## As for me and my household

Joshua, at the end of his life, gathered the whole nation and made them choose, then told them what he'd chosen, in a sentence men have put over their doors for three thousand years.

:::margin
Joshua 24:15 (NIV)
"But as for me and my household, we will serve the LORD."
:::

Choose this day, he told them, not this season or when you're ready. That's a man writing his next move in front of witnesses, declaring a direction for everyone under his roof, out loud where men can hold him to it.

Your page is your version of that sentence. It needs to be plain, dated, signed, shown to a brother, and held loosely before God. Then it needs to be run with.

## Tomorrow's three

On his porch, I asked Hollis the hardest move he'd ever written down.

"Probably 'Tell Cole he's a good man.' That was on the page about three weeks. Finally I thought, if it isn't written down it isn't going to happen, and it was written down, so I'd better." He shrugged. "Took thirty seconds. He sat in his truck and cried for twenty minutes. I'd have put it off another year if I hadn't had to look at it every night."

That's the whole method: write the move, look at it every night, do it because you wrote it, and let God establish the step. One page, one move, this week. Go make it plain.

## Before you turn the page

Writing the page will feel exposed, because a written move can be checked. That's the point, and it's a mercy: a man who hands his move to a brother has stopped carrying his intentions alone. May the Father who establishes a man's steps establish yours this week, and may you do the move at the bottom of the page and find, as Hollis has for fifty-five years, that the next thing was always enough.
