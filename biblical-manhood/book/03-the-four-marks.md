# 3. The Four Marks

Set those six maps on the floor and you're left with a sharper question than the one you started with. What is a man, then?

I want to give you the answer as one sentence you can carry in your pocket. Then we'll open it up one mark at a time, at the size of an ordinary week. But first a picture, because a sentence without a picture ends up back on the shelf.

## What a mark is

Walk into a shop where a man has worked for twenty years and the room tells you about him before he says a word. His saw has cut the same line so many times that the bench is grooved where the blade runs. His coffee cup has left a ring in the one spot where it always sits. The hooks on the wall follow the path his hand already takes to find a wrench. The ring on my own bench came from a mug I reheated twice and still walked off without. Wanting to be called a craftsman didn't cut those grooves, and neither did a Saturday class on tools. Work did, on days that ran together.

That's what I mean by a mark. Thinking well of yourself leaves nothing anyone can see, and neither does a title on a business card. Work repeated for years leaves a mark on a man. The fire you feel at dawn is usually gone by lunch. What cuts the groove is the hour you walk back into the house and do the thing again.

I told you at the start of this book where the sentence came from. It isn't mine. Men keep passing it on because it holds up in a real week. I've laid it over my own life, and over the lives of men from the youngest season to the oldest I know, and it hasn't come up false yet.

**A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.**

Read it again, slowly. It holds four marks, and each one is an action. Keep doing them and they become things people can see on a man, as plain as the grooves in that bench.

On a man, that mindset looks like the four marks. It shows up in his house, with his crew, and in a week with nothing special in it.

## He rejects passivity

Every town has a tailgate with a decent man sitting on it. He'll give you his full opinion on the weather, the school board, and the city water while the job he drove out there to do waits behind him. He's waiting for the right time or the right price. Lately he's just waiting for the right mood. He's waited so long that he's started to trust the waiting.

Left alone, a man drifts toward passivity. It rarely looks lazy. It looks like patience, or like keeping the peace. Its favorite line is "I didn't want to overstep," and the man who says it usually believes he's being humble. I've said it in my own house.

The first man in Scripture did the same kind of nothing. The serpent came into the garden and began twisting the one command God had given, and the man was standing right there with his wife the whole time. He didn't step in. He didn't name the lie. Then he took the fruit from her hand and ate. The world broke while he said nothing.

Being loud won't meet this mark, and neither will pushing people around. The mark is movement. The job is clear and you're the one standing there, so you get down off the tailgate. You start the hard conversation while it's still yours to start. You begin before you feel like it. "Stop Waiting" stays with this mark until you can spot passivity in the week you're actually living.

## He accepts responsibility

The second mark is weight. One August I helped a friend move a gun safe. Four hundred pounds, a hallway that barely cleared our shoulders, and air so thick you could lean on it. Once the two of you have a load like that off the ground, there's a moment when backing out stops being an option. The hallway has no place to rest it. Halfway through, you've lost the right to announce that the safe is somebody else's problem. You finish the carry, and then you set it down.

A boy waits to be assigned the weight and then argues that the assignment isn't fair. A man looks at what's in front of him and says, "That's mine." The fault might belong to someone else. He picks it up anyway. The unopened bills. The kid nobody in the house is talking to. The apology he still hasn't made.

Scripture says it's good for a man to carry a yoke while he's young. The carrying is what makes him. "Pick Up the Weight" shows you how to take up what's yours and how to leave what was never given to you.

## He leads courageously

Picture a row of boys on the bank of a pond on the first warm day of spring. The water is colder than any of them guessed, and every one of them is waiting for somebody else to go. The boy who jumps in is the leader, because now the others have someone to follow. Nobody gave him a title.

Houses and crews and churches all stall until somebody moves. After three quiet days in a marriage, somebody has to say "I was wrong" first. Somebody has to pray out loud at a table that has never heard a prayer. A brother who's drifting needs somebody to make the first call. A meeting full of nodding heads needs somebody to say the first true sentence. I've held back on every one of those and called it caution.

A man can be afraid and still put his boots on. That's courage. When God told Joshua to be strong and courageous, He gave him the reason too. The Lord would be with him wherever he went. "Go First" picks up that charge.

## He expects the greater reward

The fourth mark is where good men and godly men part ways. It comes last in the sentence, and it's the one men skip.

A pecan tree keeps its own schedule. Plant one this fall and you'll water it for years before it gives you a nut worth cracking. Pecan orchards have always been gifts to people who come later. The man who plants one spends his strength on a harvest he may never taste.

That's the fourth mark. A real man expects the greater reward, God's reward, and that hope shapes his ordinary days more than anything else in this book. It's what makes hidden obedience possible. It's what gets a man through a hard decade of marriage. It lets a man give money that never comes back and hours nobody thanks him for, because his Father sees what's done in secret.

Hebrews says that anyone who comes to God has to believe two things, that He exists and that He rewards the people who seek Him. God isn't embarrassed to use the word reward. A man who expects nothing from God will go shopping somewhere else. A crowd's approval. Money. A woman. A newer truck. A son who thinks well of him. A father who finally does. "Live for a Reward That Lasts" follows that search. I've come to believe the other three marks only survive a costly day because of this one.

## Why four, and why these

Men tend to come apart in four ways, and the marks answer them one for one. Doing nothing is passivity. Refusing the weight is blame, or dodging. Hanging back and calling it something polite is cowardice. The fourth is slower. A man aims his whole life at the wrong prize while the town calls him a success. In every man I've sat with, the trouble has traced back to one of those four.

A decent man could try the first three with or without faith in Christ. The fourth is what ties them to Him. When the cost shows up and nobody in the room says thank you, the fourth mark is why he keeps going. Drop it and you're holding a self-help list. Keep it and you're holding a life that outlasts you.

Seasons change under a man's feet. The marks don't. A young man can get serious now, without waiting for a wife to make him. A young father can turn toward his worn-out wife in the middle of the noise. A man in his middle years can catch himself drifting toward applause and point the truck toward home. An older man can get up with nothing left to pin on his chest and still find the work. Same four marks, every season.

## Before you turn the page

The sentence is yours now. This week, leave three of the marks alone and stay with the faintest one. In the men I've watched, change came one mark at a time, on one plain day, and then the same mark again the next day. May the Father who made you in His image show you which mark He wants to press in first, and may His patience outlast your slow start, the way it has outlasted mine.
