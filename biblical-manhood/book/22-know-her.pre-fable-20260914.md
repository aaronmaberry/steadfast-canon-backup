# 22. Know Her

Roy can tell you the torque spec on the lug nuts of every vehicle he has ever owned. He can tell you the make and model of the car in every serious wreck he worked in thirty-one years on the highway. He knows the exact date he bought his first deer rifle and what he paid for it.

At sixty-seven, a few months into retirement, he sat across from his wife at breakfast on a Tuesday morning and realized he didn't know her favorite hymn.

They had been married forty-four years, and he knew her schedule, and he knew she took her coffee with too much cream and that she'd cry at the end of a certain kind of movie and that she had never liked his mother, though she'd never once said so. But sitting there with nothing to do for the first time in his adult life, he began asking himself what he actually knew about the woman across the table, and the honest answer frightened him. He knew where she'd be. He didn't know who she was.

"I'd been standing next to her for forty-four years," he told me. "I thought that was the same as knowing her."

It isn't, and this chapter is about the difference, because the first thing a man owes his wife, before he lays down his life for her and before he works out what she needs, is to actually know who he's married to.

## Live with her according to knowledge

Peter wrote one sentence to husbands, and it has carried more freight than almost anything else in the New Testament about marriage.

:::margin
1 Peter 3:7 (NIV)
"Husbands, in the same way be considerate as you live with your wives, and treat them with respect as the weaker partner and as heirs with you of the gracious gift of life, so that nothing will hinder your prayers."
:::

Be considerate as you live with your wives. The older translations put it closer to the original: live with them according to knowledge. Dwell with her knowing her; study her and understand her, and make her a subject you're an expert in.

And look at the last clause, which men skip. So that nothing will hinder your prayers. Peter is saying that a man who doesn't know his wife, who lives next to her without understanding her, has a problem with God and not only with her. His prayers hit a ceiling. That is how seriously heaven takes this. The way you know your wife is connected to the way you're heard by your Father.

Here's what I've noticed over the years. Men study what they care about. A man who loves fishing knows the water temperature that turns the bass on. A man who loves his truck knows the sound it makes when the alternator is going. A man who loves his job knows things about it that nobody else knows. We're built to learn the things we love, deeply, over years.

And most men stopped studying their wives somewhere around year two. They learned enough to get along, and then they stopped, and they've been running on that early data ever since, and she has changed completely since then, and they don't know it, and she knows they don't know it.

Proximity isn't knowledge, which we said about brothers in chapter 13, and it's truer of wives. You can share a bed with a woman for forty years and not know the woman in it.

## What there is to know

Let me be specific, because "know your wife" is the kind of line men nod at and do nothing with.

There's her story, to begin with, because she had a chair at the end of her table too. How was she fathered? What did she learn about men from watching hers? What did her mother teach her about being a wife, on purpose and by accident? What happened to her before you that she has never fully told you? Most men know their wife's story the way they know the plot of a movie they half watched. Ask her to tell it again, slowly, and listen as though it were the first time. You'll learn why she flinches at the thing you thought was nothing.

There are her fears, and not the ones you assume. Ask her what she's most afraid of right now, and then be quiet for a long time. Most wives, given room, will tell their husbands something that surprises them. It's rarely money and rarely what he'd guess. It's often about being alone, or about the children, or about becoming her mother, or about whether he actually sees her.

There's what fills her and what drains her. Some women come alive in a room full of people and some are wrung out by it. Some need an hour alone to be a person again, and some need an hour of conversation. If you don't know which your wife is, you'll keep planning weekends that exhaust her and wondering why she's short with you on Sunday night.

There's how she receives love. Men tend to give love the way they'd want to get it, which is usually by doing something: fixing, providing, handling. Some wives receive that as love, and some don't feel loved by it at all. Some need words, and some need time with no agenda. Some need to be touched in a way that has nothing to do with the bedroom. Some need you to notice and do the thing without being asked. Find out which she is. Ask her when she has felt most loved by you and what you were doing, and then do more of that and less of what you assumed.

There's her body, and I'm going to say this straight because too many Christian men are squeamish about it and it costs their wives. A husband should know his wife's body and its seasons without embarrassment. Her cycle, and what it does to her energy and her mood and her patience, so that he can carry more in the week she has less. Pregnancy, and what it did and is doing. Postpartum, which is a season all its own and which I'll come back to. The changes in her forties and fifties, which are real and hard and which most husbands sail past without a word because nobody ever told them a man should know about it. Illness and age, too. She lives in that body every hour. If you don't know what's happening in it, you don't know her.

There's her walk with God. How does she pray? What is she wrestling with Him about right now? What verse is she standing on? What's she struggling to believe? Most Christian couples I know have never had this conversation past the surface, and a man who knows how his wife is doing with God can pray for her with precision instead of generalities.

There's her Tuesday, meaning what her ordinary day actually looks like, hour by hour? If she's home with children, do you know what two in the afternoon feels like? If she works, do you know what her boss is like and which part of the day is hardest? Men who don't know their wives' Tuesdays make comments about them that land like insults, and can't understand why.

There are her people: her friends' names and what's going on in their lives, her mother and what that relationship costs her, her sister. Knowing her people is knowing the weather system she lives in.

And there are her dreams. What did she give up to be where she is? What does she still want that she's stopped saying out loud because nobody asked? A woman who's been married fifteen years and is raising children has usually set down some things she carried at twenty-two, and a husband who knows what they were, and honors them, and looks for ways to hand some of them back, is a husband she'll trust with anything.

:::margin
Proverbs 20:5 (NIV)
"The purposes of a person's heart are deep waters, but one who has insight draws them out."
:::

Deep waters is the picture, because she isn't going to hand you all of this on the surface. You'll have to draw it out, and drawing out takes insight, and insight takes time, and time takes deciding that she's worth studying.

## How a man learns his wife

Ask, and then wait. The waiting is the whole skill. Most men ask a question and start forming their answer to it before she's finished. Ask, and then count to ten in your head after she stops talking, because she usually hasn't stopped. The real thing comes after the pause.

:::margin
James 1:19 (NIV)
"My dear brothers and sisters, take note of this: Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry,"
:::

Quick to listen and slow to speak. James wasn't writing a marriage book, but he might as well have been. Listen without fixing, because when she tells you something hard your whole body will want to solve it, and you mustn't, not at first. Say, "That sounds heavy, tell me more." A woman who gets fixed every time she speaks stops speaking. A woman who gets heard tells you everything.

Watch her, too. What does she do when she's tired? What makes her laugh when she isn't trying to? What does she reach for when she's sad? Where does she go in the house when she needs to be alone? Men are good at watching things they care about. Watch her the way you'd watch a piece of water you were trying to learn.

Write it down, and I mean that literally. You keep maintenance records on the truck; keep them on your marriage: her sizes, her friends' names and their children's names, the thing she said she wanted in passing eight months ago. The date her father died. What she said her biggest fear was when you finally asked. A man who writes down what his wife tells him is a man who has decided it matters enough to keep. She'll notice when you remember. She'll notice more when you remember what she said only once.

Ask her what you get wrong. This is the bravest one. Once a season, ask, "What do I misunderstand about you? Where do I get you wrong?" And then don't argue; take notes instead, because you'll learn more in that conversation than in a year of assuming.

And take the twenty minutes home. The three questions from chapter 13, what happened this week and what's heavy and what's next, work across a kitchen table. Ask her every week, same night, and answer them yourself. Roy started doing this with his wife after the hymn morning, and he said it was like meeting her for the first time at sixty-seven.

## Knowing her under a newborn

Tomás, this is for you and for every man with a baby in the house.

The woman you married is not the woman across from you right now, and neither of you signed up for that, and both of you are a little frightened by it. Her body has been through something you can't fully understand. Her hormones are rearranging her from the inside. She hasn't slept a full night in months. Her identity has shifted under her feet; she was a woman, and now she's a mother, and she isn't sure where the woman went. She may be grieving her old self and feeling guilty for the grieving. She may be scared she's doing it all wrong. She may be more tired than she has ever been in her life and unable to explain it in a way that sounds like a reason.

Knowing her in this season means knowing that she's carrying a load you can't see, and treating her like a woman under a load. It means asking "what do you need in the next hour?" instead of "what do you need?", because the second question is too big to answer at three in the morning and the first one isn't. It means noticing when she hasn't eaten, hasn't showered, hasn't been out of the house, hasn't laughed. It means understanding that her not wanting to be touched right now is about exhaustion and a body that's been someone else's property for a year, and not about you, and not permanent.

And it means watching for the thing nobody warns young husbands about. Some new mothers go into a darkness that isn't only tiredness. It has a name, and it's common, and it's treatable, and the husband is usually the first person in a position to notice. If your wife isn't herself in a way that's lasted weeks, if she isn't sleeping even when the baby does, if she says things about herself that frighten you, you take her to a doctor, and you go with her, and you don't let anyone, including her, tell you it's nothing. That isn't overreacting; that's a man who knows his wife and keeps her.

Tomás told me the sentence that changed his first year was one his wife said at four in the morning: "I don't need you to fix it. I need you to know how hard this is." He'd been trying to fix it for four months. He stopped and started knowing, and something in the house came unclenched.

## Knowing her after forty years

Roy, back to you, and to every man past sixty. The danger for an older husband isn't that he doesn't know his wife. It's that he knows a version of her from 1985 and stopped updating, and she has changed. Her body has changed, and she's living in a body that's aging in ways she may not have made peace with, and she needs a husband who knows that and still looks at her the way Adam looked at Eve. Her fears have changed: not the children's safety anymore, but the grandchildren's, and her own health, and yours, and being left alone. Her griefs have accumulated: her parents, friends, maybe a child. Her dreams for the remaining years are specific, and she has probably never said them out loud because for forty years the calendar belonged to your work.

Know the woman she is at sixty-eight. Ask her the questions you asked at twenty-four, and expect different answers. Ask what she wants to do with the years that are left, and listen as though it were the most important intelligence you've ever gathered, because it is. Roy found out his wife had wanted to see the ocean since she was a girl and had never mentioned it because there was never a good time. They went that fall. "Forty-four years," he said, "and I didn't know that. What else don't I know?" That's the right question, and he's been asking it every Tuesday since.

And one more man belongs here, the one in the middle years, who is neither underwater with a newborn nor retired with time to study.

A man of forty-eight has usually been running on data from 1998, and the wife across from him has changed her fears and her body and her dreams without his noticing. Anthony discovered, when he finally asked, that his wife's biggest fear wasn't money at all; it was that he'd stopped seeing her the year their second child was born. The takeaway for him is that the question he's afraid to ask is the one that would change the house.

## For the man who's single again

Kenny, and every man in your seat, you may be reading this with a stone in your chest, because you know now what you didn't know then. Maybe you never really studied her. Maybe you did and it wasn't enough. Either way, hear two things.

First, if you're honest that you didn't know her, that's a piece of "I did this," and it belongs in your confession and your grief, not in a fight with yourself for the rest of your life.

Second, you still have to know her, differently. If you're raising children across two houses, you have to know your children's mother well enough to co-parent wisely, not intimately but civilly. What she's afraid of for the kids. What sets her off and what calms it down. How to say the hard thing about the schedule so that it gets heard. That's a kind of knowing too, and it's for your children's sake, and it's a man's work.

And if God gives you another wife someday, you'll start knowing her on day one, with the notebook open, because you know now what it costs not to.

## For the single man

You aren't studying a wife yet. Study women anyway, the right way, as sisters. Learn to ask a question and wait for the whole answer. Practice on your mother, who has been waiting your whole life for you to ask her something real. Practice on your sister. Learn what it's like to listen to a woman without fixing or flirting. A man who has learned to hear women as sisters will know how to hear a wife, and the man who hasn't will spend his first five years of marriage learning it the expensive way.

## How He knows His bride

Christ knows His church, not in general but specifically: every fear, every wound, every hidden thing, the number of hairs on every head. He knows us completely and isn't put off by what He knows, and He keeps knowing us, patiently, over a lifetime, drawing out the deep waters.

:::margin
John 10:14 (NIV)
"I am the good shepherd; I know my sheep and my sheep know me"
:::

I know my sheep. That's the model for a husband. Not a man who married a woman once and has been guessing ever since, but a man who knows her, and keeps knowing her, and lets her know that she's known.

Roy asked his wife her favorite hymn that Tuesday morning, and she told him. Then she asked why he wanted to know, and he told her the truth: that he'd realized he'd been standing next to her for forty-four years and had never really studied her, and that he was sorry, and that he'd like to start. She didn't say anything for a while. Then she got up and got the notebook she'd kept for thirty years with all her recipes in it, and turned to the back, and started telling him things.

He's still writing them down.

## Before you turn the page

You may have closed this chapter feeling behind, because most men are, and I'd like to say that being behind isn't the same as being too late. Roy started at sixty-seven and his wife handed him a notebook. She's been waiting for you to ask. She isn't keeping a ledger of the years you didn't; she's waiting to see whether you will. May the Father who knows His sheep by name give you the patience to draw out the deep waters, one question at a time, and may you find that the woman you've been standing next to is more than you knew.
