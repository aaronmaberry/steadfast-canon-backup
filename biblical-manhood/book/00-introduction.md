# Introduction

I've spent most of the hours that mattered in my adult life across a table from another man. A kitchen table. A folding table at the back of a fellowship hall. The cab of a truck with the engine off. A diner counter where the waitress pours your coffee before you sit down, because she's seen you on that stool before. Some of those men were young enough to be my sons, and some were old enough to be my father. They worked different jobs and had lived through different decades, and underneath all of it they were asking the same three questions. What is a man? Am I one? And if I'm not, is it too late?

This book is the long answer I give when the coffee has gone cold. It isn't the only answer on the shelf. It's the one I've watched hold up in ordinary houses over a lot of years, and I've tried to write it the way I'd say it if you were sitting across from me.

## Who I'm writing to

Every season of a man's life is already in this room. Find your seat.

You might be single, still waiting for somebody to call you a man out loud. You might be newly married, or so deep in the baby years that nobody in your house has slept through the night since March. Maybe you provide everything for your family except yourself. You may be divorced and still very much a father. The kids may be grown and the house gone quiet, or the job may be over and you're learning what a man does with a morning that has no clock to punch. Or you're standing in a kitchen where her cup is still in the dish rack. Nobody here is a guest. This book is for you.

I'm not writing only to the man with the mortgage and the minivan. When the counsel has to change with the season, I'll turn toward you in the middle of the paragraph and say so. I won't stop every chapter to take roll. Near the end, "Every Season Still Counts" sits down with each season on purpose. Until then, trust that I know you're here. If the house is loud and the page count looks impossible, start with "A Quiet Tuesday," do the four lines at the end of it, and come back for the rest when you can. The book will keep.

## What I'm claiming

One sentence sits at the center of this book. I didn't write it, and I won't pretend I did. Robert Lewis gave it this shape years ago at Fellowship Bible Church in Little Rock, in the teaching that became *Raising a Modern-Day Knight*. Men have been handing it to each other ever since. I've held it up against my own life for a long time, and against the lives of men in every season I just named, and I haven't found a place where it lies.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Those are the four marks. "The Four Marks" opens them up, and the four chapters after it take them one at a time. For now, look at what kind of sentence it is. Every verb in it is something a man does. None of them waits on a feeling. You do these things on ordinary days, and over time they mark a man the way thirty years of work marks his hands. You can see them on him, and you can see it when they're missing. No amount of feeling covers for either one.

## How the book is built

We start with an ordinary day, because that's where a man's life gets decided. "A Quiet Tuesday" names the problem. Then we tell the truth about the confusion you were handed, and we put the four marks in your hands until you can say them without looking.

From there we go back to what you inherited and what's still yours to choose. The chair at the head of the table you grew up at. Anger, the itch, and the drift. The Father who does not leave. The handoff into manhood you may never have received. Leaving home without slamming the door. Brothers, and the nerve to ask an older man for help. The problem inside you, which would still be there if your father had been perfect. Then we go further back, to the beginning of the human story, so you stop reading your life as if you were the first man to live one. Then the house. A wife if there is one, and a life with a cause if there isn't. The marriage bed. A daughter. A son. Then the long road. Work, feelings, the seasons of a life, the years nobody claps for, a plan you write down, and the men who stay.

Every chapter comes back to an ordinary day on purpose. That's where all of this gets tested.

## The four lines at the end

Every chapter ends the same way, with four short lines. I'll explain them once here so I don't have to keep doing it.

**Name it** asks you to say, out loud and in your own words, what the chapter touched. Men are good at nodding and bad at saying, "That's me." Nothing in a man moves until he says it.

**Do this week** gives you one thing to do before the week is out. Only one. If that's all you take from each chapter, you'll have done more with this book than most men do with any book.

**Tell a brother** asks you to say it to another man. A step nobody else knows about is an easy step to quit. If you don't have a brother yet, "A Man With No Brothers" is for you. Until you get there, telling a brother can mean writing it down where you'll see it every day, and telling God, who has heard worse.

**Carry this** hands you one line for the week. It's the kind of line you could text a friend without either of you feeling preached at.

I know how a tired man reads a book like this. At some point you'll reach a new chapter without having done the last one's step. When that happens, don't skip ahead in shame, and don't quit the book. Tell your brother you didn't do it. That counts as telling a brother. Then do it this week. Every man in these pages has missed a week. The ones who changed are the ones who admitted it and went again.

## The men you'll meet

You'll meet men in these pages by name. Anthony, who stands a little too long in his own doorway. Tomás, whose baby won't sleep. Roy, who turned in a state trooper's badge after thirty-one years. Walt, who built a business from one truck to eleven. Micah, who sat at the singles table at weddings for six years. Dale, who was handed an intake form on a hospital gurney and couldn't think of one friend to write down. Others too. They're composites. The conversations happened at real tables with real men, and I've mixed the names and details so nobody can be picked out. My hope is that you'll recognize yourself without recognizing anyone else.

## One more thing before you start

This book stands on its own. Read a chapter, do the one thing, and find one other man to do it with. That's enough for a man to change.

If there's no other man yet, don't wait for him to start. Write it down. Tell God. Let the brother catch up when he shows.

We also built a twenty-four-week training on the same four marks. It has videos, printable guides, and a class that a church, a small group, or a man by himself can run. In the back of this book you'll find a short map of those weeks, and the full class guide is at walksteadfast.com. The book is the book, and the training is the training. Use either. Use both. Just don't binge four chapters on a Saturday and call it finished.

Pour the coffee and pull up a chair. Let me tell you about a Tuesday.
