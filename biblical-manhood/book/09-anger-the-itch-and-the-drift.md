# 9. Anger, the Itch, and the Drift

In a single week, Owen used every way out he had. He'd have told you it was his temper, and that would have been the end of the conversation.

One night his daughter loaded the dishwasher wrong, and he shut the door hard enough that a glass jumped off the top rack. The kitchen went quiet the way his kitchen had learned to. Two nights later he spent forty minutes locked in the bathroom with his phone. Nobody could need anything from him in there, and that was the pull. On Saturday he lay on the couch with a ballgame on while his wife tried to plan July. Her sister had offered to take the kids. "Whatever you think," he said, and he said it from somewhere a long way off.

Three nights, and three ways out. Owen would have called them three bad moods. Underneath all three was a contract he'd been counting on for the fall. He'd already spent that money in his head. Three weeks earlier the job had gone to another man, and the numbers weren't going to change. He was afraid, and he hadn't told a soul.

## Where pain goes

In "The Empty Chair," I said a boy can't leave a hole that size empty. Something always sits down in it. Pain works the same way. It has to go somewhere, and it has three ways out of a man.

Anger takes it out. Appetite takes it down. Absence takes it away.

In a heavy week, most men lean on one of those exits and call it their personality. It started as a boy's way to stop feeling something. It worked, so he kept using it, and by now it moves before he does. Block the exit he likes best and the pain will find another one.

I know the pull of every one of them. I won't go easy on any of them here, and I won't use them to shame you. Shame only makes all three stronger.

## Anger

Anger is the exit men like best, because it looks strong.

Look underneath it and you'll usually find fear, or shame, or the feeling of being helpless. The money won't stretch to the end of the month. Something he did is sitting on his chest. Something he loves is breaking, and he can't get his hands on the break. In a man's body, every one of those feels like weakness. Anger feels like power, so he grabs it the way a freezing man grabs the first coat by the door. The dishwasher didn't move Owen's hand. The numbers did.

God's Word expects anger to show up. What it cares about is your next move.

:::margin
Ephesians 4:26-27 (NIV)
"'In your anger do not sin': Do not let the sun go down while you are still angry, and do not give the devil a foothold."
:::

The verse starts in the middle of the anger. Then it sets a clock, and the clock is sundown. Then it names the danger. A man goes to bed angry, wakes up still holding it, and goes to bed angry again, and now the enemy has a foot in the door of his house. Before long the anger is the weather his family lives under.

If anger is your exit, learn the ninety seconds. Left alone, the surge anger sends through your body crests and starts to fall in about a minute and a half. So walk to the mailbox and back, and leave the sharp sentence unsaid. Then come back inside and say what's underneath it. "I'm scared about the money." A wife can make a plan with a man who admits he's afraid. She can't make one with a slammed door. Settle it before the sun goes down. The man who learns this becomes the steadiest man in his house.

## The itch

Appetite is the exit that goes down. Sex has the strongest grip, so that's where I'll stay. But the same itch runs through a bottle, a shopping cart, a betting app, and a screen you can scroll all night. It offers comfort fast. It asks nothing of you, and nobody sees.

Behind that locked bathroom door, Owen wasn't chasing pleasure so much as relief. Relief from the numbers. Relief from a kitchen that had gone quiet because of him. The screen didn't need anything from him, least of all a man. That was the draw, and the lie sat right beside it, because being needed as a man is exactly what would have started to heal him.

:::margin
James 1:14-15 (NIV)
"but each person is tempted when they are dragged away by their own evil desire and enticed. Then, after desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, gives birth to death."
:::

Dragged away is the right phrase for how it feels. Nobody puts two in the morning on his calendar. And the death at the end of that road rarely makes a sound. His attention at the supper table dies first. Then his desire for a real woman, and then his ability to look his wife in the eye. She can feel that something is wrong long before she can say what it is.

Paul told the Corinthians to flee, and Joseph showed what fleeing looks like. When his master's wife grabbed him, he left his cloak in her hand and ran out of the house. Move that fast.

If this is your exit, the work starts with one man and the whole story. Tell him what you do, how often, how long it's been going on, and what it's costing you. Don't hand him "I struggle with purity." This appetite lives on staying unknown, and it starts to die once a brother knows the details. Then cut off the supply. Put a filter on every device and give him the password. At night the phone charges in the kitchen. You'd make that rule for your son without thinking twice. Make it for yourself.

Then face the loneliness underneath, because that's the real subject, and "A Man With No Brothers" takes it up soon. The itch gets smaller in a man who is known.

## The drift

Absence is the quiet exit, and it's the hardest one to catch. The house still looks fine, and nobody calls it a crisis.

A drifting man leaves while his body stays in the building. Nothing has blown up, and nothing has come out. He's just somewhere else. His wife quit asking once his answers shrank to "fine" and "whatever you think." His kids learn that a father can sink into a couch and still be gone. That's how a man builds the chair that looks full and holds nobody. Some of you grew up across the table from one.

There's almost always a hurt under the drift. Somewhere along the way he decided, without telling anyone, that caring cost too much. Or he really was tired, and every year rest slid a little further until it turned into withdrawal.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 10:18 (NIV)
"Through laziness, the rafters sag; because of idle hands, the house leaks."
:::

Nobody put a boot through that roof. The leak came from a ladder nobody climbed and a couch somebody never left. By the time anyone says the word drift out loud, the stain is already on the ceiling.

If this is your exit, say it to your wife, or to whoever shares your house. "I've been in the house, and I've been gone, and I'm sorry." She already knows. Hearing you say it changes the room. If there's no wife, tell a brother, because a drift nobody sees just keeps drifting. Then take back two small practices. One evening a week with every screen off and your mind in the room. One question a day, and you stay put until the whole answer is out. And go looking for the boy who decided caring cost too much. A brother can help you find him, and so can a good counselor. That's the ladder.

## The same root

As a boy, the angry man was hurt by fear, so he traded the fear for a feeling that passed for power. The man with the itch learned early that nobody would see him, and he went looking for comfort that didn't need to know his name. The drifting man learned what caring costs and quietly closed the account. All three are trying not to feel the chair. All three end up leaving the house.

Jesus took none of these exits. He got angry. He turned over the money changers' tables in the temple, and there was no sin in it. He was tempted in every way we are, and He never once took the way down. In the garden He asked the Father if there was another way. Then He said, "Not as I will," and He stayed. If you've already gone out one of these exits, He can meet you out there and walk you home without shame.

You've probably tried stopping by force. A man can clench his jaw for years and never move an inch. Take the pain where it can be healed, instead of where it can be numbed. There's a Father who stays. Your anger doesn't frighten Him. Your itch doesn't disgust Him. And when you drifted, He didn't.

## The exits in every season

The season changes what the exits look like. It never closes them.

When you're young and single, appetite tends to be the loudest. Watch for the drift too. A job you don't care about. Evenings handed over to a screen. At your age, numb is a warning light.

With a baby in the house, anger will catch you off guard. Tomás found that out at three in the morning, with the crying going on and on. A voice came out of him, and the last time he'd heard it, it was coming out of his father. Take the ninety seconds. Then say the true sentence, the one Tomás finally said to his wife. "I'm so tired I'm scared of myself."

The first year or two after a marriage ends can throw all three exits open at once. Anger with her name on it. Appetite, fed by a quiet apartment you never used to live in. Absence, because caring is what got you here. When recovery comes, it often looks like one older man on a weekly call who won't take "fine" for an answer.

Older men often get anger and the drift together, right in front of the television. Get up before she does. Have the coffee ready. Stay in your chair while she drinks it, ask her one question, and let the answer come.

## Owen's mailbox

One night, Owen laid the numbers on the kitchen table in front of his wife. For three weeks, he told her, his fear had been going into the dishwasher, the bathroom, and the couch. Partway through, the old heat came up in his chest. He got up, walked out to the mailbox and back, and sat down again. Then he finished.

She'd known something was wrong for a while. She could stand with him in the fear. What had hurt the house was him being gone.

The fall contract was still lost. He was done hiding from it in his own kitchen.

## Before you turn the page

Your exit has a name now, and a named thing can be carried to the Father. May He meet you at the mailbox, at the screen, and on the couch. And may one small refusal this week be the first of many.
