# 28. Give Your Son a Hill

Lyle figures he was eleven or twelve. An October morning, and the faucet over the kitchen sink gave a cough and went dry.

That house drank from a spring on the rise behind it. His grandfather had boxed the spring in concrete a generation back and run a line down through the pasture, one branch to the house, one to the cattle. No water meant somebody was walking up there.

He'd been up there before, but only as the kid holding the bucket. This time his dad looked out the window and said, "Spring's quit. Go see to it." That was it. No "come on." No "I'll show you."

Lyle told me this at seventy-something, the place long since sold. He got to the gate at the edge of the yard and froze. The lid on that box was heavier than he was. He turned to say he couldn't, and his dad had already carried his coffee outside and settled onto the back step. He wasn't getting up.

So the boy climbed. It ate the morning. The lid slipped and pinched his fingers. A possum had drowned in there and lay across the screen, and nobody else was going to move it. His arms went numb from that water while he dug out the mud. He fit the pipe wrong, saw it, did it over. Every so often he looked back toward the house. His dad hadn't moved.

Before he reached the yard he could hear water running into the trough. He walked up soaked, filthy, a couple of knuckles split open. His dad studied him a long second. "Well. I figured you could."

It took Lyle years to understand why the man sat. If his dad had walked up with him, Lyle would have come home believing he needed help. Because his dad stayed put, he came home knowing he didn't.

## Lyle's rule

He's been sending boys up hills ever since: his own, his grandkids, a Sunday class full of other men's sons, some with nobody ever waiting at the bottom. He says grandsons are easier. He's got time, tools, and a patience he didn't have at thirty. I asked him what he'd learned. "Pick the right hill. Stay on the step. Say the right thing when he comes down." Then he grinned. "And don't tell his mother what was in the box."

You named your boy. Now hand him something steep enough to make the name true. Calling a son strong and never giving him anything heavy is just being nice.

## Sitting on the step

Start with your part, because it's the hard one. Hand it over. You'd be faster. That's the point. If you finish it, he doesn't get it. The man who fixes things for a living has it hardest. You could solve it with one call, and he'd learn that Dad is the answer.

Let it be hard for him. Let the lid pinch. That starts in diapers: let him wrestle the toy a minute before you rescue it. A man who can't bear the baby's crying usually can't bear the teenager's sulking either. His mom may find this harder to watch, and that's love in her, not weakness. Decide it together, gently. Rescue a safe boy and he learns he needs saving. Leave him to work and he finds out he can manage. Sitting there empty-handed is the work, even when it looks like you've wandered off.

That's Stop Waiting, and here it flips. The thing that's yours to do, starting now, is to give him the job and keep your seat. The passive father is the one who grabs the shovel. Everybody's more comfortable. Nobody grows.

Losing a little now is a gift. Forty bucks gone because he didn't plan is cheap tuition. Shield him from every scrape and his first real one shows up alongside a wife, a house payment, and nobody watching from the porch.

When he gets back, ask. How'd it go up there? What did you figure out? What would you change? Let him think. Then name it. I figured you could. You stuck with it. Without that sentence, a hard day is only a hard day.

Then make the next one steeper, and the young man who leaves your house will have a man's hands.

:::margin
**Hebrews 12:11 NIV**
No discipline seems pleasant at the time, but painful. Later on, however, it produces a harvest of righteousness and peace for those who have been trained by it.
:::

Neither of you should expect pleasant. You're both waiting on later.

## Why he needs it

Boys turn into men by facing things slightly too big for them while a man they trust watches and refuses to jump in. Protect a boy from all of it and you starve him. Watch Abraham and Isaac going up the mountain.

:::margin
**Genesis 22:6 NIV**
Abraham took the wood for the burnt offering and placed it on his son Isaac, and he himself carried the fire and the knife. As the two of them went on together,
:::

The heavy, awkward load is on the boy. The father keeps the dangerous things, fire and blade, and stays alongside. Then there's Jesse, who didn't give David a pep talk. He gave him sheep.

:::margin
**1 Samuel 17:34-35 NIV**
But David said to Saul, "Your servant has been keeping his father's sheep. When a lion or a bear came and carried off a sheep from the flock, I went after it, struck it and rescued the sheep from its mouth.
:::

The lion wasn't a drill. It came because the job was real. By the time the giant showed up, David had a history of hard things behind him. Even Jesus grew up learning a trade under Joseph's eye, and Luke tells us He grew in wisdom and stature. Growing takes something to push against.

A boy who gets affection and nothing difficult goes hungry, and he'll feed himself. What boys pick on their own tends to be dangerous. The fight. The dare. Gravel at night with the headlights off. Or he disappears into his room for years, because nobody showed him how to climb and every slope looks bottomless.

## How you know it's a hill

Somebody besides him has to need it. The house really has no water until he's done. Boys know when a chore is fake. Make him sweep a spotless garage for his character and he'll sweep it and roll his eyes.

It should stretch him without breaking him. Harder than what he handled last month, nowhere near a grown man's full weight. If it's easy, nothing happens. If it's impossible, he learns to quit.

He has to be able to fail. A pinched finger is allowed. Coming back down with the job undone is allowed. Take away every way he could fail and there's no hill left. Lyle's dad knew that box, and he knew the snakes were already holed up for the cold. He picked the hill. Then he picked the step.

The finish has to belong to him. A boy says, "I tried." A man says, "It's done," or, "It isn't yet, and here's my plan for tomorrow." Up there he learns that trying isn't a finish line. The trough fills or it doesn't.

And somebody has to speak when he comes down. Whether he nailed it or made a mess, you say something. Before the climb, "you can do this" was your hope. After it, he's got proof in his arms.

Fit the hill to the actual kid, not the number on his cake. Two brothers a year apart might need very different ones. Your daughter needs hills too, and a dad who calls her strong when she comes down. Hers may look different. The way you hand it over doesn't. It could be hauling in the groceries, every last bag, however many trips. It could be something that's his start to finish: a row of tomatoes, a dog, the trash to the curb on Thursday with nobody reminding him. It could be a summer working for a man who won't grade on a curve. Money he could lose. A choice you'd have made differently that you let stand. A night in the woods that feels scary and that you've quietly made safe. Someday the hill is his whole life, and your part is answering the phone.

Some things only look like hills. A sport where it's really the dad who needs the win. And screens, which hand him the rush of climbing with none of the weight. He can beat level after level and the sink is still dry.

## Where fathers miss

Some dads start with a cliff, and the boy decides climbing means getting crushed. Some hand their sons the dream they never finished. Some swoop in at the last minute, calling the coach or covering the bill, and every swoop tells the boy, you couldn't. Some mock the stumble, and from then on he steers around anything hard. A divorced dad has his own version: weekends only, guilt in charge, anything tough edited out so the boy will want to come back. Give him real work at your house instead, and leave something half-built so it's waiting for him. The lid caught Lyle's fingers. He set the pipe crooked. His dad didn't comment on either.

## I figured you could

Paul told Timothy, his son in the faith, to expect hardship the whole road.

:::margin
**2 Timothy 2:3 NIV**
Join with me in suffering, like a good soldier of Christ Jesus.
:::

That's who hills make. A tested man who can walk up alone and fix what's broken.

Your boy is standing by that gate right now, looking back to see what you'll do. Sit down. Stay put.

## Before you turn the page

Keeping your seat is hard obedience, and most of us stand up too soon. If you have, he isn't ruined. Boys are tougher than we think, and it's not too late to hand him one. The Father who watched Abraham load wood onto Isaac, and who one day laid a cross on His own Son, knows what it costs to watch a son carry weight. May He help you choose the right hill this week, give you the stubbornness to sit through it, and put Lyle's dad's words in your mouth when your boy comes down.
