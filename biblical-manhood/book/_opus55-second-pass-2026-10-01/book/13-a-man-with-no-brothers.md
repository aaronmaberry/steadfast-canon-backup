# 13. A Man With No Brothers

The form wanted two names.

It was a little past two in the morning, and Dale was sitting on the edge of an ER bed in a paper gown that didn't close. For three hours the tightness behind his ribs had seemed like the end of the world. Then the tests came back, and it was nothing. A nurse handed him the intake sheet on a clipboard and went to find a blanket. He wrote his wife's name on the first line without thinking. The second line asked for an emergency contact who wasn't his spouse. He held the pen over it. Nothing came.

His phone had hundreds of contacts. Guys he sold to and bought from. Guys from deer camp. Guys from church. His wife's brother, a couple of states away. His pastor. Any of them would've shown up with a truck to help him move a couch. Not one was a man he could call at that hour and say, "I'm scared."

Years later he told me about it, and his voice went flat. "I had no idea I was alone until the night I needed someone."

## Buddies and brothers

If you're thinking this chapter's for some other guy, it's probably for you. Most men don't have brothers. Not in the way that counts. They have buddies, and that's a different job. A buddy is the guy next to you while you're doing something: hunting, fishing, eighteen holes, a game on the big screen. Good men, good times, and none of it is the same as being known. In a lot of marriages the wife ends up as the only person who really knows her husband, and that's more than one person was ever meant to hold. Then he retires from the crew he saw every day, or he buries her, and finds out how quiet a house can get.

Try this on. If your phone rang in the middle of the night, whose name could be on it, and whose number could you dial? Who has permission to tell you, face to face, that you're wrong? Who knows what actually scares you? Ask a room full of capable men and listen to it go quiet, even though every phone in there is full.

None of this was the design. On the Bible's second page, the first thing God calls not good is a man by himself. His first answer was a wife, and she stays the first answer. But all through the story, men walk next to other men too. David had Jonathan. Jesus had twelve, and three of them closer still. The Teacher in Ecclesiastes says it plainest.

:::margin
**Ecclesiastes 4:9-10 NIV**
Two are better than one, because they have a good return for their labor: If either of them falls down, one can help the other up. But pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up.
:::

That last line was Dale, pen in hand, on a gurney.

## Even Jesus asked

Look at those three closest men on Jesus' last night. He took them along to pray, knowing one of them would swear he'd never met Him before sunup. He brought them anyway, and He asked them for something.

:::margin
**Mark 14:34 NIV**
"My soul is overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death," he said to them. "Stay here and keep watch."
:::

The Son of God, who needed nothing from anybody, wanted three friends awake beside Him in the darkest hour He'd ever face. He wouldn't go through it alone. You don't get an exemption He turned down.

## The reasons we give

I've asked a lot of guys why that second line stays empty, and I've used most of their answers myself. "I'm fine." That's pride in work clothes. Nobody's fine. "I don't have time." You do; nobody's asked you for a slice thin enough. "I don't want to be a burden." Under that there's often a boy who learned that needing people wasn't safe and decided he'd sink before he'd reach. "Men don't do this." For most of history they did, and when they quit, a slow, quiet dying set in that we decided to call independence. "I got burned." Some of you really did, and I'm sorry. But one bad meal is a thin reason never to eat again. "I've got my wife." Keep her close. She's called to know you, just never to be the only one, and carrying that by herself wears a woman down over the years. Ask her whether it has.

## What a brother does

David is on the run, a king hunting him, exhausted and hiding out in the woods. And somebody comes looking for him.

:::margin
**1 Samuel 23:16 NIV**
And Saul's son Jonathan went to David at Horesh and helped him find strength in God.
:::

Jonathan didn't bring an army, and he couldn't call off the hunt. The hunter was his own father. He walked out to Horesh regardless, and he pointed David back to God. That's the job. A brother can't fix your life. He can walk you back to the One who holds it.

He also knows where you go when the day turns sour, whether that's anger or a screen or just going silent, and he asks about it. He doesn't steer around the locked rooms: the money, the marriage, the bedroom, whatever your eyes landed on this week, whether you've prayed. You tell him the truth because he asked. If the hospital calls at two in the morning, he's in the waiting room, because to him brother isn't a figure of speech. And when it's his turn, he tells you the truth, kindly, once, with no watered-down version.

:::margin
**Proverbs 27:17 NIV**
As iron sharpens iron, so one person sharpens another.
:::

Iron on iron throws sparks. Something he says is going to sting. You walk away with an edge.

One man like that. Two, tops. He might send you to a counselor and still be your brother after. And the guy you have the most fun with isn't always the guy you can trust with the truth.

## Kenny's phone

Kenny found out what one man is worth the first year after his marriage ended. He was in a one-bedroom apartment with a mattress and a folding chair, and most of the friends had gone with the house. They'd mostly been hers, or the guys didn't know what to say. An older man from church started calling him once a week. Same time. He'd ask how things were, what was weighing on him, what Kenny planned to do before they talked again, and then he'd pray. Most weeks Kenny said "nothing." The man called again the next week anyway.

These days, every Tuesday, Kenny calls his son. You can draw a line from that older man's phone to Kenny's.

If you're divorced and your church has not known which row to put you in since, don't wait around for someone to fix the seating chart. You don't need a program. You need one man who calls.

## Twenty minutes

Most men want this. What they don't have is a version that fits the week they're actually living. Everything on offer is too big: a six a.m. breakfast, a group with workbooks, a weekend retreat. A guy with a newborn or a business going under looks at that, knows he can't, and does nothing.

So shrink it. One man, twenty minutes, every week, on the same day at the same hour. The sameness matters; a call you keep rescheduling eventually stops happening.

You don't need a script, but you need a direction, or you'll be talking trucks and ball scores inside eleven minutes. Catch each other up on what actually mattered, not the highlight reel. Then get to the part that earns the call, what's heavy and the fear underneath it, while whoever's listening just listens; fixing isn't tonight's job. Before you hang up, each of you says out loud one thing you'll do by next time, and next time that's the first thing you ask. Spend the last couple of minutes praying for each other, out loud.

There'll be weeks when one of you is falling apart and the heavy part eats all twenty. Fine. That's why it's a floor, and the floor doesn't move.

Twenty minutes fits inside April for an accountant, a double shift, a round of chemo, a funeral week. A dad with a newborn doesn't have thirty, which is why it's twenty; he can make the call with his hands in the dishwater. I've never known a guy who truly couldn't spare twenty. I've known lots who couldn't spare two hours, so they spared nothing. Twenty is a seed.

## Finding him

Look for someone who's mostly living what this book is about. He's probably already at your church or on your crew, and nobody's ever asked him anything real. A guy can share a kitchen with three roommates and still have nobody who knows him, so don't assume the one across the hall counts until you've asked. Same age is fine. A little older is often better. Skip your boss, anybody who reports to you, and your brother-in-law; you need to say anything without a paycheck or a Thanksgiving riding on it. And the team you lead at work, good as they are, isn't this.

Then ask in plain English. "I'm working on growing up as a man, and I've realized nobody really knows me. Could we talk twenty minutes a week for three months and just see?" A three-month window makes yes easy, and makes stopping easy if it flops. Usually it doesn't, and by the end neither of you remembers there was an exit.

It'll be awkward, then it won't. He's been hoping somebody would ask. Men are lonely, and almost none of them will make the first move. You will. That's Go First, with a phone in your hand.

## The second line

Dale has a brother now. It took him most of a year to ask. The man had been sitting a few pews over for twenty years, and in all that time they'd never had one real conversation. Now they talk Thursdays, on Dale's drive home. For the first month Dale was sure he'd signed up for the dumbest thing he'd ever done. Last spring he went in for a scheduled surgery and somebody handed him another clipboard. When he got to the second line, he didn't stop writing.

## Before you turn the page

Asking might be the most awkward sentence you'll ever say out loud. But nearly every man who's said it gets some version of the same answer: "I was hoping somebody would ask." May the Father give you the nerve to make the call, and may the man who answers turn out to be a brother who shows up when it's bad.
