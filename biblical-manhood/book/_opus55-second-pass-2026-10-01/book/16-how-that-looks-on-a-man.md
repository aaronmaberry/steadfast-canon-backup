# 16. How That Looks on a Man

Watch him in the church foyer. He wants to know what you thought of the message. He spots the new guy alone by the coffee and walks over, and the welcome is warm because he means it. Now follow him home. Somewhere between the front door and the stove his voice crosses a border. Same shirt. Same ring. The weather in that house is something else entirely.

That's what the trouble from the last chapter looks like on a man. It doesn't need a wreck. A nice finish works fine, and being capable works even better. Sin is cut to fit men in particular ways, and if you can't recognize the pattern, you'll stand at the bathroom sink every morning and tell the guy shaving that he's all right.

## What it wears

Here are the shapes I run into most. One of them fits you.

The locked drawer. A second set of books. A search history. A friendship he describes at home with the wrong word. Sometimes there's no object, just a fear he's posted No Trespassing over. A boy's secret corner becomes a man's bolted room, and that's where things go bad. If you're single, give a brother your passwords and permission to ask, and empty the drawer while it's nearly empty.

The fortress. How are you? Fine. Need anything? No. The whole family leans on him; nobody gets leaned on in return. It's pride in a work jacket calling itself strength. From the road it can pass for Pick Up the Weight. But a man carrying his own load still lets a brother grab the other end. The fortress exists so that never happens. Gray in the beard makes it worse, because long years make a man expert at needing nothing. Around a table of brothers, "What do you need from us?" may be a question nobody has ever asked you, and your answer could change the table.

The scoreboard. In his head he's always tallying. Whose truck, whose title, whose wife, whose kids, whose bonus. Another man's good news stings, and his own life never feels like enough. Saul kept that tally, and it had him throwing spears at the young shepherd who'd just won his war for him. After a divorce the scoreboard moves right in: her place against yours, whose holiday table looked fuller. Put the board down. Your Father sees in secret, and He isn't comparing your table with hers.

The podium. Sin in its Sunday best, the one Jesus seemed angriest at. Gentle in the lobby, a thunderhead on the drive home. Jesus said men like that perform for people who barely know them, and that audience is all they've got.

The throne. Every disagreement at home ends with him on top, by volume or a cold shoulder or a line that bruises. He calls it leadership. His wife would tell you her real job is handling him. The kids read the mood of his truck pulling in the way ranch kids read a sky. Paul fences the whole thing with one line.

:::margin
**Colossians 3:19 NIV**
Husbands, love your wives and do not be harsh with them.
:::

He knew that husband was coming. With a new baby, the throne gets loud. Worn-out men turn sharp, and she's more worn out than you, so apologize before bed every time.

There's a quieter version of the throne. The smirk. The eye roll while she's telling a story at dinner. Looking down on a gentler man in the next pew, or a son who cries.

The recliner. Laziness disguised as rest: a tired guy cashing in an evening he figures he earned, while the house pays in loose shingles and quiet kids. Boil it all down and some men skip the house, some run the room, and some answer "who are you" with a job title. If you're winning at work, notice whether your title has started answering questions only your soul should.

## Holding up a mirror

Sin lies to whoever has it on, so use checks that don't run on feelings.

What would you hate for a brother to see? Phone, history, accounts, calendar, that one friendship. Whatever is inside, having a drawer at all is the problem. Hide nothing and there's nothing to defend.

When did someone last tell you no and you took it? If you can't think of a time, you're up on a throne.

Married men, pick a calm hour and ask your wife where you hide and where you come across harsh. Don't argue. Listen, then thank her. Single men, ask the brother who knows you best, or your mom.

What do you only do when someone's watching? Who are you in the lobby, and who are you alone in the cab at eleven on a Tuesday night? The distance between those two men is the act.

Leave respect out of it: who in your house is scared of you? If any name comes to mind, step down. Get on your knees in front of the people who've had to learn your weather.

## The king on the roof

One man in the Bible wore several of these in a single season, and nobody in his generation stood taller. If David could go down this road, you can. It started with staying home.

:::margin
**2 Samuel 11:1 NIV**
In the spring, at the time when kings go off to war, David sent Joab out with the king's men and the whole Israelite army. They destroyed the Ammonites and besieged Rabbah. But David remained in Jerusalem.
:::

Spring was fighting season, and David had always ridden out with his men. This year he sent his general and left his own boots by the door. Nobody in the palace would have called that sin; the king had put in his years. If you want the spot where a mark first gave way, it's here. Stop Waiting was the call, and David sat it out. Empty hands, long evenings, and one of those evenings he went up to pace the palace roof. You know what a bored man and a blank evening can get into.

He saw a woman bathing and asked who she was. Bathsheba, he was told, wife of Uriah, one of his own soldiers, who was out at the siege that very night. David sent for her anyway. When a king summons you, saying no isn't really on the table. He took her, because in that hour the hour was all that mattered to him.

Weeks later word came: she was pregnant. David pulled Uriah back from the front, poured him wine, and steered him toward home so the baby could be passed off as his. Uriah slept at the palace door instead, out of loyalty to friends sleeping on the ground near the enemy. So David sent him back with a sealed letter for Joab. Put him where it's hottest, then fall back. Uriah carried his own death order to the wall. Once the mourning was done, David moved the widow into the palace, and to anyone at court it looked settled.

:::margin
**2 Samuel 11:27 NIV**
After the time of mourning was over, David had her brought to his house, and she became his wife and bore him a son. But the thing David had done displeased the LORD.
:::

The kingdom hummed, and hardly anybody knew. For close to a year David ran Israel with a dead soldier hidden and the soldier's wife under his roof, and by every outward measure he ran it well. That's where skill and holiness part company. A church, a business, or a family can look solid while the man at the center goes soft with rot.

David wrote about that year later. His body wasted. God's hand pressed on him day and night. A good lock doesn't keep the drawer from working on you.

## The prophet's finger

Conscience never won on its own. God sent Nathan.

:::margin
**2 Samuel 12:7 NIV**
Then Nathan said to David, "You are the man! This is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: 'I anointed you king over Israel, and I delivered you from the hand of Saul.
:::

Nathan began with a story: a rich man with whole herds took a poor neighbor's one pet lamb to feed a guest. David came out of his chair. Then Nathan pointed. You. Four words, spoken to a king's face in his own hall.

Men rarely blow their own cover. Usually a wife stops keeping quiet, or a brother won't accept "fine," or a bank statement lands on the counter. David's road was the ordinary one.

Everybody needs a Nathan, and every man ought to be willing to be one. That's why you sit with brothers: someday one of them will have to look across at you, enthroned and comfortable, and point.

Then David answered.

:::margin
**2 Samuel 12:13 NIV**
Then David said to Nathan, "I have sinned against the LORD." Nathan replied, "The LORD has taken away your sin. You are not going to die.
:::

One short sentence, and a period. No mention of where she chose to bathe. No complaint about Uriah. No speech about the weight of the crown. A confession ends where the sentence ends. Forgiveness came on the next breath. The consequences came too, and they were brutal, but David didn't have to outlast them before he was right with God. The drawer opened, and he was restored.

## Nothing locked

Jesus had no drawer. Nothing in Him was held back from His Father. On the night of His arrest He reminded the high priest that every word He'd taught was spoken in public, and His accusers had to go looking for false witnesses. Nobody could find a hidden room in Him, and He had nothing to be ashamed of.

That's the man God is shaping in you. Private and public start to match. Your wife, your brothers, and your God can look straight at you without wincing, since what you hid has been said out loud and forgiven.

Let your Nathan in. Confess, and stop at the period.

## Before you turn the page

You've likely spotted your shape. A prophet pointed, David answered with one short sentence, and he was forgiven before he could draw another breath. May the Father seat a brother beside you who'll point when you need it, and give you the plain nerve to say, I have sinned, and stop talking.
