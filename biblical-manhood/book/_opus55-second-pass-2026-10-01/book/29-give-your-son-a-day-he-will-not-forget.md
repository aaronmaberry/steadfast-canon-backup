# 29. Give Your Son a Day He Will Not Forget

There's a boy I know who keeps a brass plumb bob in his pocket. It's small and surprisingly heavy, with his name and a date cut into the side. His dad says he'll pull it out in a grocery store parking lot for no reason, just to hold it. A few miles from his house, on a gravel bar under a county bridge, sits a little pile of creek rocks nobody has moved. Some afternoons he rides his bike down there to look at them.

His dad told me about the morning both of those things happened. Before I pass it on, here's why it matters.

## Why give him a day

Most of us never got one. We kept waiting for somebody to tell us we'd arrived, and the waiting quietly turned into a whole life. Your son doesn't have to wait like that.

God has always given His people things to bump into. When Israel crossed the Jordan, Joshua had men haul stones out of the riverbed and stack them on the bank, and he said why.

:::margin
**Joshua 4:6-7 NIV**
to serve as a sign among you. In the future, when your children ask you, 'What do these stones mean?' tell them that the flow of the Jordan was cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD. When it crossed the Jordan, the waters of the Jordan were cut off. These stones are to be a memorial to the people of Israel forever.
:::

A pile of rocks built to start a conversation with a kid who hadn't been born yet. Passover had a child's question built right into the meal. And Jesus heard His Father speak over Him, out loud, at a river, before His work began.

Most Christian homes have nothing like this for a son. Birthdays, graduation, sure. But no morning when the men in his life look at him and say plainly, you're a man now. So he grabs a marker from whatever's lying around. A girlfriend. A drunk night. A fight. Hand him the real thing instead.

## The creek

His voice had started to crack that year, and he'd taken to studying grown men the way younger boys study trucks. His dad told him to be dressed by five, and wouldn't say why. They drove out in the dark and parked by the bridge where he'd been baptized two summers before. Nobody talked on the walk down.

Five men stepped into the creek. Alton, his grandfather, went in ahead. His dad. His dad's brother. The friend his dad meets with every week. A neighbor who'd taught the boy to fish, a single man who'd never been asked to anything like it. The water barely covered their ankles and it was cold enough to knock the air out of you. Each man bent, came up with a stone, waded back, and set it at the boy's feet.

Alton read the Joshua passage. Then the men talked, one at a time. His uncle told him the worst thing he'd ever done, and what he did the morning after. When it came around to his dad, he laid his hand on the boy's shoulder and said the name he'd been saying over him since the hospital: my son. Then something new. "You're becoming a man. That's what this morning is about." Then what he meant by it, in his own plain words. "A man does what's his to do, even on the days nobody's looking. That's most of it." He'd written it all out by hand on a card so the boy could keep it.

Alton reached into his coat, came out with the plumb bob, put it in the boy's palm, and told him what it was for. After that, every man laid a hand on him and prayed, and they all went to breakfast, where the men talked to him like a man. His dad says the boy heard the difference.

## A line that doesn't lie

Alton had hung a plumb bob against every wall of the house the boy's father grew up in. You already know what a plumb line is for, and Alton didn't give a lesson on it. He kept it to a few words. "The weight's on purpose. A light one drifts in any breeze. Be the heavy kind, hard to push off true." The name on the side, he said, is for the day the boy forgets who he is.

His dad says it's already come out of that pocket a few times, once over going along with the crowd at school, once over the truth about a grade. Your boy's keepsake doesn't have to be brass. A Bible with the date inside, a great-grandfather's watch, a good tool. Pick weight over price, and say what it means when you hand it over.

## Putting the morning together

The turn in him tells you when: the voice, the shoulders, the way he's started watching men. If you've missed it, give him the morning anyway. Keep it small, a few men he already looks up to who'll still be around long after breakfast, because a crowd turns everybody into a performer. Don't have those men yet? Start looking now. Go outside, early, somewhere that means something, and make him work a little to get there. Tell him something's coming, not what. Let his father speak last, in words that sound like him and nobody else, and if he chokes up, let it show. A boy who watches his dad cry while blessing him learns what no program could teach him. And know going in that the weeks after matter more than the morning. The leader guide at walksteadfast.com has the planning details. If you want one verse read aloud, I keep coming back to this one.

:::margin
**1 Corinthians 16:13 NIV**
Be on your guard; stand firm in the faith; be courageous; be strong.
:::

## The Monday after

The morning doesn't finish anything. I know a family that poured everything into one beautiful day and went right back to treating him like a little kid by Monday. What makes it true is how he's treated afterward. Bigger hills. His opinion asked for and then actually used. Grown-up work, grown-up correction, a grown man's standard, and mercy when he blows it. The day marks the spot. The weeks after it do the handing over.

Alton worked for the county most of his life. He says his retirement dinner was the best night of his working life, and the morning after was the worst. Applause, then nobody needing him. So the very next week he called his grandson to come help pour a footing. The creek told the boy he was a man. The footing started making him one. Granddads, that's your opening. You've got standing, maybe a keepsake in a drawer, and the years to make the weeks afterward real.

## If no one gave you one

Maybe nobody ever did this for you, and you can feel it in your throat. You may think you can't give what you never got. That's fair to feel, and it's wrong. Giving it heals the same place receiving it would have. I know fathers who never heard a word from their own dads, who stood by the water, came apart halfway through blessing their boys, finished anyway, and drove home changed. Want it closed out? Afterward, ask a man further down the road to lay a hand on you and say it. You're a man. You did right by that boy. It isn't too late for you.

## What do these stones mean

Someday a grandson will pick up that brass bob, or ask about the rocks under the bridge. The answer will be a gray morning, a cold creek and the men who waded it, a card in a father's handwriting, and the bob a grandfather hung beside every wall he built. And if we've done our part, whoever's telling it will go set up a morning for his own son.

The day is where it starts. Every week after it is the rest.

## Before you turn the page

Even if no one ever gave you a morning like this, there's a word I'd want said over you, and it isn't too late. May the Father who stacked stones beside a river give you the men, the morning, and the nerve to say the new word out loud. May your boy carry something heavy in his pocket that keeps reminding him whose he is.

**Name it**

A man nobody marked tends to leave his son to make up his own mark. Write down whether anyone ever gave you a day, and if not, what you grabbed instead. Then write the honest answer for your son: is a day coming, or is he left to guess? Ask it for your daughter too. Hers may be supper instead of sunrise, with her mom and grandmother there, but mark your son and leave her guessing and you've done half.

**Do this week**

You can decide this week even if the morning is months off. Pick the date, the men, the place, and the keepsake. Write out what you'll say with your hand on his shoulder. Divorced? You can still do this, and you should. Talk to his mom first, before he hears a word, and keep it about the boy. A good stepdad might be worth inviting. If she pushes back, use your own time and your own men, and keep the old fight out of that morning. Single, or no son? You can be the neighbor at another boy's creek. Find a dad you trust, or a mom raising a boy alone who'd be grateful, and offer to stand in the water. Talk to her before you plan a thing.

**Tell a brother**

Nobody ends up standing in that water without being asked. Ask him to wade in with you and come with something true to say to the boy. If he's got a son, ask when that boy's morning is, and offer to come.
