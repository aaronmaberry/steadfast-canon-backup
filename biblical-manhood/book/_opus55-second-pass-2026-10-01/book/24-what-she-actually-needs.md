# 24. What She Actually Needs

Russ had one boot off and the other in his hand when he heard Renee through the mudroom door, talking to her sister. "Oh, he's a good provider." She said it kindly, the way you'd talk about a furnace that has never quit on you in January.

Nothing in it was false. He'd worked the sixty-hour stretches to make it true. But standing there with the boot, he heard it two ways at once. It was the nicest honest thing she had. And if nothing ever got added to it, it was his eulogy.

"You know where people say that?" he asked me later. "About a man at his funeral, when nobody can come up with a second thing." I've sat through enough funerals to know that silence. The room waits for the next sentence, and it never comes.

## The part you can count

I will give you the whole chapter in two sentences first. She needs you. A bigger paycheck can't fill the spot where you're supposed to be standing.

"Good provider" is a furnace sentence. It tells you the house stayed warm and not much else. Good men get turned around right here, because a paycheck can be counted and being there can't. Scripture honors the work, and so do I. Pour all of yourself into what's measurable, though, and you'll end up with a lovely house and a wife who's lonely inside it.

## The trap for the guys doing a lot right

You work hard for your family. The job wants more, so you hand it more, and it feels like love. Then the signs show up. She stops asking when you'll be home. You learn what's going on in your own house from the kids. By eight at night you're too spent to be anybody. And you've decided she wants this life without ever once checking with her.

Russ checked. He sat down across the kitchen table and asked one question: "What do you need from me that you're not getting?" Renee didn't have to hunt for it. "Look up when I walk in." The man had paid for every room in that house, and somewhere along the way he'd stopped raising his eyes when she came into one.

He kept the job. What he changed was smaller and harder.

He laid down a floor, a few things that stay put whatever the job does. Supper at the table four nights a week. Sunday mornings. Bedtime prayers with the kids.

He gave her an hour and kept it. Six forty. Then six forty again the next day. When it was going to slip, he called before he was late, and he called her, not the family thread. A husband who's predictable about the hour is half home while he's still on the highway.

He quit handing her the leftovers of himself. For the first hour the phone lives on top of the fridge. She gets the opening fifteen minutes, while there's still something in the tank.

And he stopped playing to the crowd. Work claps for you. Home mostly doesn't. Russ quit chasing the clapping and started doing the job for a Father who notices a supper table.

This spring Russ came in through the same mudroom and caught Renee on with the same sister. She didn't say provider. She said, "He's here," and went right on to something about their mom, like that was the most ordinary news in the world.

## Eyes up

Being home and being present aren't the same thing. A man can sleep under that roof every night and never really be with her, and she won't be able to explain why she's lonely. Your attention has to walk in the door with your body. Phone away. Ask her something, then stay for all of the answer. She can tell a husband from a warm shape in a chair. In the newborn years, that means being awake beside her at three even when there's nothing for you to do, and her sleep beats any gift you could buy.

## Say it where they can hear

She needs words from you, out loud and specific, sometimes with the kids in earshot. There's a husband in Proverbs who says it in front of everybody.

:::margin
**Proverbs 31:28-29 NIV**
Her children arise and call her blessed; her husband also, and he praises her: "Many women do noble things, but you surpass them all."
:::

That's not a greeting card. It's a man telling his wife she's the best there is, with witnesses. Find your own version and say it before Sunday. If the marriage ended, the kids still need to hear you back their mom up, dependable and civil. Tearing her down from the other house might win a round. Your kids pay for it.

## Quit helping

Retire the word "help." The dishes are yours. So are the practice schedule and the dentist appointments. A husband who "helps out" has announced that the house belongs to her and he's a volunteer who drops by. That's Pick Up the Weight, standing at a sink: you carry what's yours without being asked. Eve was called a helper, and that's a strong title, not a small one. Christ came to serve. Neither of those gives a man a pass to sit while she runs the place around him.

## Touch that wants nothing

She needs your hands on her in ways that aren't heading anywhere. A palm on her back at the counter. Her feet in your lap after a long day. Your arms around her while she cries, with every fix you can think of left in your pocket. She already knows the bump in the hallway, and she knows the touch that's angling for the bedroom. Give her the third kind.

## Nothing to brace for

She needs to not be afraid of you, and I don't mostly mean your fists. I mean the voice that drops, the three-day silence, the sarcasm with an edge, the truck leaving the drive too fast. A wife who checks your weather before she speaks is on guard in her own kitchen.

:::margin
**Proverbs 15:1 NIV**
A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger.
:::

A soft answer takes more strength than a loud one. Once she knows your anger won't land on her, she gets a freedom a lot of wives never taste.

## No exit in pencil

She needs to know you're staying. A wife can sense an "unless" tucked behind the vows even if you've never said it, and nobody rests in a marriage with a door sketched on the wall. Erase it. Then tell her, plainly and more than once, that you're here for good and you'll face whatever comes side by side. Keep that in the weeks it costs you, and let her watch you keep it. Staying includes your body, too. Book your physical. One fear she won't say out loud is coming home to find you on the floor.

:::margin
**Malachi 2:15 NIV**
Has not the one God made you? You belong to him in body and spirit. And what does the one God seek? Godly offspring. So be on your guard, and do not be unfaithful to the wife of your youth.
:::

## Chased, and steered

The guy who first asked her out should still be asking. If chasing her was only how you won her, it quit at the wedding, and she knows what it feels like to be put on a shelf. Plan a Thursday and tell her early so she can look forward to it. Press a decent shirt. Get caught watching her across a crowded room. Decades in, she still wants to be noticed as a woman with a name and wants of her own, long after the kitchen has her routine memorized.

She also wants to know where the house is headed so she can finally fold up the map. Tell her what you see coming, and plan the later years together. Make the hard call yourself and own how it lands. Leave her as the only grown-up and she'll steer because somebody must, tired and second-guessing. Once a week, hold her hand and pray out loud. Hearing her husband bring her name to God settles something in a woman.

## Someone worth walking with

An earlier chapter did the heavy lifting on knowing her, so only this: keep wanting to, and stop acting like the first few years taught you everything.

And let her see you move. A husband who's frozen in place, same guy as ten years ago only grumpier and wider, is one more load on her back. A husband who's changing, slowly, with failures on the record, is a man she can walk next to. Much of a home's hope rides on whether he's becoming someone. If you're single, you're building that man right now. If the marriage ended, lay this chapter next to it, not to beat yourself bloody but so whoever you are next is different.

## He did it first

Look back over this chapter and you'll see Christ in every line of it. He doesn't leave. He's with His people always. He calls Himself gentle and humble in heart. He went looking for the lost and still says follow me. He knows His sheep by name. He spoke over bread, this is my body, given for you, and then He knelt with a towel and a basin. He cried at a friend's grave. Even He grew in wisdom and stature and favor.

What your wife wants is a little version of Him, under one roof, on a plain Tuesday night. It will cost you. That's the gift she's been hoping for.

## Before you turn the page

If your stomach just dropped, take heart. Hardly anything in this book turns around as quickly. Mostly she's asking you to lift your eyes. May the Bridegroom who never once left His bride give you the courage to ask her one plain question this week, and the patience to listen until she's done.
