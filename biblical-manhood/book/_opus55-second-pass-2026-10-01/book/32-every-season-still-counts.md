# 32. Every Season Still Counts

Most of us carry a picture of a man's life we never chose. It's a hill: climb, stand on top a while, roll down toward a recliner. A divorce, a diagnosis, a wife's funeral all get treated as detours, things to get past so you can rejoin the hill. You won't find that hill anywhere in Scripture. David served God's purpose in his own generation, the one he was handed, not the one he'd have picked.

Now drive by a cotton field in February. It looks abandoned. Gray dirt, snapped stalks, a wire fence around nothing. Any farmer will tell you it's busy. The soil is resting while old stalks break down into it. Winter rain is working its way deep, down where roots will reach for it in August. February has a job. Plant out of impatience and you throw the year away.

:::margin
**Ecclesiastes 3:1 NIV**
There is a time for everything, and a season for every activity under the heavens:
:::

I promised near the start not to split this book by age on every page. Here I break that once, on purpose, because our culture puts the seasons of a man's life in rank order, and that ranking has stolen years from a lot of us.

## Same marks, new ground

Passivity at twenty-five is a tailgate and a phone. At seventy-five it's a recliner and a remote. A young father stops waiting and gets up for the 2 a.m. bottle. A widower picks up the weight of a shovel and a bundle of bare-root pecans. The pay is the same in every season too: a son's inheritance, by grace, never wages. You work because you're already in the family.

The four marks hold in every season. Gardens change. Learn the motions once and your hands will find them again when the ground shifts. So let's walk the seasons: who from these pages stands in each, what God asks of you there, and what tries to pull you off it.

## Single, with time on your hands

Solomon wrote these words with white hair, looking back over everything he'd wasted and everything he hadn't.

:::margin
**Ecclesiastes 12:1 NIV**
Remember your Creator in the days of your youth, before the days of trouble come and the years approach when you will say, "I find no pleasure in them"—
:::

You're young, strong, and your evenings are your own, which can build a life or burn one. Micah sat six years at the back table, and he'll admit he spent half of them just waiting, for a wife, a career, somebody to say he'd arrived. Waiting-room thinking is one trap. The other one keeps quieter hours: free nights with nobody checking on you put a young man about as close to the edge as he'll ever stand.

So build. Meet the Father at twenty-four and you won't spend your fifties scanning bleachers for somebody's nod. Whoever you become right now gets handed to every later season. And hear me: marriage is good, but it isn't the starting gun for your life.

## New husband, new dad

A wedding takes an afternoon. The plain weeks after it pour the floor you'll live on. Study her. Give up a few preferences. Ask what she needs instead of guessing. And keep your weekly time with your brother, or your wife ends up carrying what she was never built to carry alone.

Then a baby comes, and that first stretch breaks more young couples than anything else. Tomás had a four-month-old when you met him. A tiny person needs everything, and you both secretly assume it'll feel like this forever. It's a year. Protect the marriage inside the mess; after bedtime, ten minutes together at the counter is worship enough. There's no calmer season coming. Watch her for the postpartum dark, which has a name and a treatment. When the year was over, Tomás told me he'd learned he could still act like a man on no sleep, and his wife had learned she could lean on him.

## Mid-career

Anthony lives here, frozen in the doorway between garage and kitchen. People call it the prime. Careers climb, homes slip, and the temptations wear a tie. Hiding inside provision. Labeling drift as fatigue. A middle-aged itch once the marriage gets quiet.

What God wants here is a man who's present. A steady floor at home. A wife you keep learning, because she's changed, and the woman across the table deserves to be known as she is now. Brothers, because men in this stretch have the fewest friends of anyone. Hills with your kids. And a straight answer about what the work is for. This is where Work for God's Pay gets tested, when the office applauds and the family gets leftovers. Your kids are watching which way you turn in that doorway.

## The season nobody plans

I've got one man in mind. He never wrote divorce on a calendar. This season has a job anyway, and it runs longer than anybody warns you.

Here's where it can go. Give it three to five years and the ground usually firms up. A real place to live, crayon drawings stuck to the fridge, and guys walking into the same fire start asking you to coffee. I can't promise another marriage. I can promise this stretch isn't wasted. The Father who never walks out sat in that courtroom with you, and He's still there.

Getting there is another matter. Early on, staying upright is the win. Grief ambushes you at custody handoffs and on Sunday nights. You're mad at everyone. Loneliness brings the itch with it. Everything in you wants to swerve: new town, new woman, new job, whatever makes it quiet. Don't. Steady and slow. Grieve within earshot of someone. Own your share and leave hers with her. Kids before any new relationship. One brother on the phone weekly. Keep walking into church even when church is awkward about you. That's the second Adam's road: cover your kids, own your part, refuse every exit. Men follow someone who came through.

## When the house empties

Walt sold part of the company and started chasing his wife again, and he'll tell you it's been the best stretch they've had: the first time since '94 it's been only the two of them, and he's finally present for it. That's the move for this season.

Because the danger is real. The last kid drives off, and twenty years of dinner talk about the kids goes with them. Some couples settle into polite roommates. Some men drift into an affair. Overlooked at home and admired somewhere else is a bad combination. So pursue her, and then turn outward and hand some hills to younger men. If what emptied was a co-parenting schedule, these years are still yours, and God still has work in them.

## After the job ends

You already know how Roy spends his mornings. Biblical retirement isn't stopping. It's moving from hauling the load to helping others haul theirs. The temptations are aimless fishing trips and anger pumped in through a screen. Help instead. Mentor younger men. Clean a widow's gutters. Be there for the wife who's waited decades to have you around. Be like Caleb. At eighty-five he walked up to Joshua and asked for the roughest ground in Canaan.

:::margin
**Joshua 14:12 NIV**
Now give me this hill country that the LORD promised me that day.
:::

That's an old man who still figured he had work left. Roy put it to me plainly: thirty-one years keeping the peace, five years building men, and he knows which of the two he'll be asked about.

## Burying her

God has a promise for old men, and Mr. Pruitt is living inside it.

:::margin
**Isaiah 46:4 NIV**
Even to your old age and gray hairs I am he, I am he who will sustain you. I have made you and I will carry you; I will sustain you and I will rescue you.
:::

Deuteronomy uses that carrying picture for a dad holding his small son. Here it's spoken over white hair. Fifty-one years married, and then a grave. The spring after, Mr. Pruitt put forty pecan trees in the ground, and the year of mourning before he picked up a shovel was part of the assignment too. Somebody not yet born will eat from those trees.

The lie in this season says your life went into the ground with hers. Mourn as long as it takes. Get yourself one brother; God called a man alone not good and never added an age limit. Then tend whatever garden you've got left.

## When the body gives out

I know a man who can't leave his house. For eleven years he's prayed for every young man in his church, each by name. The young men know it. Give a man a chair and a prayer list and he can carry more weight in a congregation than the guy with the microphone.

Paul begged three times to lose his thorn. God said no, and told him strength shows up best in weakness. So if your body's quit on you, or you're the one caring for someone whose body has, you aren't finished. You remember Mr. Alvarez, bathing his wife every morning. He's been at it three years now. The lie here is that you're useless. The assignment is to help however you still can. That counts.

## The final stretch

Paul's last letter came out of a cell, with the end close enough to see.

:::margin
**2 Timothy 4:7 NIV**
I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.
:::

Men dodge this season. The Bible won't. Count your days. Live like someone who'll be asked how he finished. Say what you've been putting off. Bless your children from your bed, like Jacob. Pass the plumb bob along, story included. Every man in this book reaches that line. Practice the four marks in the seasons before it and the moves will already be in your hands.

There's no month off. February counts. The ground under your boots holds work that belongs to you, and the Father who set the seasons turning sees every bit of it.

## Before you turn the page

Set the ranking down. It has cost men too many years. Whether you're nineteen or seventy-nine, may the Father who orders the seasons give you yours as a single plain sentence with an action in it, and may you look down and find the ground under you was meant for you all along.
