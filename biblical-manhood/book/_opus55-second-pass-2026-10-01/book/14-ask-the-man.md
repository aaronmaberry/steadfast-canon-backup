# 14. Ask the Man

When Travis was young, he had a theory that if you could catch a long-married man after church before he got to his truck, he could tell you how it was done in a sentence or two. One Sunday he tried it on Harlan, who'd been married longer than Travis had been alive. Travis caught him at his driver's door, and he still tells me he doesn't know where he got the nerve.

Harlan studied him for a second, the way you size up a board before you cut it. Then he said, "Ride with me Saturday."

So that Saturday Travis sat in the passenger seat of the truck Harlan hauled feed in and rode his whole route. Around noon Harlan pulled in for fuel and went to the pay phone. He called his wife and told her what town he was in and what time he'd be pulling into the driveway. Travis found out later he'd made that call every working day since they got married. Travis rode two more Saturdays. That cab taught him more about marriage than every sermon he'd sat through.

There's a man somewhere around twenty years farther down your road. He's already been through what you're walking into. He's closer than you think. And the asking is on you.

## Both of you are waiting

The young man has reasons for keeping quiet. The older guy looks too busy. A man ought to figure out his own mess. Or his dad was supposed to teach him this and never did, and going to somebody else feels like admitting the hole. The older man has reasons too. Who wants an old man's opinion? Or he's made so many mistakes that he can't imagine anything in there worth handing on. So both wait, and boys grow into men with nobody a few miles ahead to tell them what the road does next.

Nobody's going to pick you off the wall like a kid in gym class. Ask.

## Ask small

Don't open with the word mentor. It sounds like a job title, and an older man hears a promise he may not keep, so he says no, or says yes and quietly vanishes.

Ask one narrow thing instead. What's kept your marriage together all these years? He might answer with words, or he might just nod at the passenger seat the way Harlan did. How did you know it was time to leave that job? If you got your kids' early years back, what would you do differently? What did the year after your divorce really cost you? Could I come watch you work sometime? If you're single, ask the marriage question before there's a ring; a year of questions beats three years of learning by crashing. If there's a baby at your house, ask the early-years one now, while you can still use the answer.

One question is a complete ask. If the hour goes well, you've earned another one next week. If he hands you a pair of gloves, show up ready to be useful. And if you want some edges around it, put a number on it: "Could we do twenty minutes a week, by phone, for a couple of months? There are a few things that are foggy from where I'm standing, and I bet they're not foggy from where you are."

## Who to ask

You're looking for a man about two decades ahead who's living out what this book describes, unpolished. Not the most impressive guy in the building; leave him to people who need impressing. You want somebody who went down a road like yours and is still standing at the end of it.

Watch his wife while he talks. Thirty years with a faithful man and thirty years with a performance leave different marks on a woman's face, and she knows exactly which one she's got. Be careful with the guy who only tells you about his wins. Either he's hiding the losses or he never noticed them, and either way he can't get you where you need to go. You're there for the scars, and for whatever his version of the noon phone call is.

Pick somebody whose life you'd actually want at his age, and somebody you can reach. If no name comes to mind, you're looking too high. Maybe he's the deacon everybody thanks and nobody asks anything. Maybe he's the man in the back row, married longer than you've been alive, who hasn't had an honest talk with anybody your age in years. Maybe he's the retired guy who hangs around the hardware store counter a little longer than he needs to. If you're divorced, shame will tell you to skip this, and you need a man ahead as much as anyone. Find one who's lived through a marriage ending, or a long-married man who won't turn you into a project.

## After the yes

Picture the young man who helps with a fence once, then keeps showing up with nothing but a coffee and time to kill. By the third Saturday the older man is busy. Now picture the one who brings a real question every time. He gets the toolbox and the story behind the scar. The question is what honors the older man.

Then there's what you do with the answer. Plenty of older men have poured good advice into young men who nodded and changed nothing, and eventually they stopped pouring. Try what he said, even once, and come back and tell him how it went. Do that and he'll hold nothing back.

Just don't ask him to be your dad. He can't fill the exact shape your father left, and leaning that whole weight on him will wear him out.

And thank him, out loud and specifically. "I tried what you said with my dad, and something actually moved." Men his age can go years without hearing a sentence like that.

## If you're the man ahead

About half of you reading this are the older man. The young man who doesn't have anybody needs you. In the middle years you face both ways: a man ahead you've never asked about the quiet house, and a younger man behind you whose marriage is fraying.

You're not finished. Nobody graduates. Even Paul, years into his ministry, wrote that he hadn't arrived yet and was still pressing on. Somebody younger is just now entering country you crossed years ago. He doesn't need a clean record. He needs the truth about where you blew it, and the plain, everyday habits that held your house together anyway. Paul told Titus as much.

:::margin
**Titus 2:6-7 NIV**
Similarly, encourage the young men to be self-controlled. In everything set them an example by doing what is good.
:::

An example, with a few words alongside it, in an ordinary week. That's the whole plan. No program required.

He probably won't come to you; you look busy and you make him nervous. So make yes easy. "I'm hanging a gate Saturday and could use a second set of hands, if you want to learn how." Once he's there, skip the speech. Ask what work is doing to him, how things are with her, what's weighing on him. Ask, then be quiet, and a young man will tell you things he'd never tell a guy his own age.

Tell him where you failed. When a young man hears somebody he respects admit he almost lost his marriage and that it was his own fault, something unlocks, and he can quit pretending. Your scar is the lesson. And if your own father never did this for you, you're not disqualified. The life you've lived is the thing you have to give.

Work side by side; a shared job teaches more than a table. Keep it small, twenty minutes a week or a project every other Saturday. A young man who never had anyone dependable will learn more from eleven weeks of you just showing up than from the best talk you'll ever give.

You'll get more than you give. The older men who do this all say it: I thought I was helping him, and mostly he kept me going. If the badge or the hard hat is hanging on a hook now and the mornings are quiet, here's what they're for. God sets lonely people in families, young and old together.

## A chain with gaps in it

Scripture pictures this as a chain. Moses went ahead of Joshua. Elijah went ahead of Elisha. Paul went ahead of Timothy, and he made sure it didn't stop with Timothy.

:::margin
**2 Timothy 2:2 NIV**
And the things you have heard me say in the presence of many witnesses entrust to reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others.
:::

Paul, Timothy, the reliable men, and whoever they teach: four generations in one sentence. In a lot of churches and a lot of homes, the ends of that chain are just hanging loose. Your brother knows your trouble because he's standing in the same weather. The man ahead knows it because he walked out the other side and can tell you what's there. You need both, and one can't stand in for the other.

## Ride with me

That first summer turned into years of Saturdays in Harlan's truck. Hundreds of questions, Travis guesses, and a lot of the answers came as a downshift and a long look out the windshield. Harlan's been gone a while now. On Travis's wedding day he sat in the very last pew, and afterward he took Travis's hand and said, "Well. You paid attention."

Travis had asked, and Harlan had opened the door. Neither of them stood around waiting on the other to make the first move.

Ask him. Someday, be him. The chain won't mend itself just because time goes by.

## Before you turn the page

The broken links aren't some private guilt you have to fix before you're allowed to move. Mending them is the work of the men holding this book. If you're younger, bring one honest question. You'll feel foolish for about eight seconds. Then come back the next week. If you're older, open the passenger door. May the Father who gathers men together put a Harlan within reach of you, make you that man for somebody coming up behind, and show you both how thin your excuse of busyness always was.
