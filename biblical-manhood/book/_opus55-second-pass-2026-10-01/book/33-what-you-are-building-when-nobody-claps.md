# 33. What You Are Building When Nobody Claps

In July around here, concrete crews start pouring foundations at four in the morning. They have to. Pour at noon and the slab cures too fast and cracks. So they work under lights, screed and float in the dark, and by sunup they're gone. All that's left is a flat gray rectangle in the dirt.

Nobody photographs that. A family will live on it for thirty years without a thought. Kids will learn to walk on it. Couples will fight and make up on it. All of it sits on something poured while the neighborhood slept.

A man's life is mostly slab. Out of sight, holding up everything you can see.

## Thirty years nobody filmed

Start with Jesus. He lived thirty years before anybody outside Nazareth knew His name, and Luke covers the whole stretch in a single verse.

:::margin
**Luke 2:51 NIV**
Then he went down to Nazareth with them and was obedient to them. But his mother treasured all these things in her heart.
:::

The Son of God spent His twenties sanding boards in a town people made jokes about. He did what Joseph and Mary told Him. No crowd. Then He waded into the Jordan, and the Father spoke out loud: this is my Son, whom I love. One sentence of praise from heaven, followed by three years of being doubted and a cross at the end. He never went hunting for a crowd to make up the difference. He'd been seen for thirty years by the only eyes that mattered. That was enough. It can be enough for you.

## Your week, mostly unwatched

Think back over the last seven days. The 2 a.m. bottle. The hotel where you shut the laptop and went to sleep. The lunch you've packed for your wife every morning for eleven years. The true answer at work that cost you, when a lie would have been easy. Nobody clapped. Nobody's going to.

The world's answer is poison: if nobody saw it, it didn't happen. Scoreboards taught us that, and it's why men perform and drift toward whatever room is applauding. If you're single, almost nobody sees your evenings. If your job hands out applause on a schedule, home may be the only place nobody grades you. Either way, Jesus flatly disagreed with the scoreboard, more than once in the same sermon.

:::margin
**Matthew 6:6 NIV**
But when you pray, go into your room, close the door and pray to your Father, who is unseen. Then your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you.
:::

Your Father sees in secret. In the hidden thing, nobody can mistake the crowd for your motive. It's just a son and his Father. This is Work for God's Pay with nothing else mixed in, and the pay is the Father's "well done" and a son's inheritance, never wages. You know who was in the room.

## The fire

Paul looked ahead to the day every man's work gets inspected.

:::margin
**1 Corinthians 3:13 NIV**
It will be revealed with fire, and the fire will test the quality of each person's work.
:::

Everyone builds with something. Build for applause and you're building with straw. From the highway, straw can look better than gold, because straw is bright and gold just sits there being heavy. Then the fire hits, and everything done for an audience is ash in a second. What survives is what you did for the Father when nobody was looking. That four a.m. pour is gold. The 2 a.m. bottle is gold. A sermon preached to be admired is straw. So is a donation that buys a plaque. So is the dad who's all in at the ballpark and checked out by the time the truck hits the driveway.

## The barn nobody remembers

Roy's grandson rides the school bus past a barn his great-grandfather raised. The barn's still up. Nobody on that bus can tell you who built it.

That's legacy: something still standing. Towns forget, churches call new pastors, grandkids move to Denver. Build to be remembered and you'll be disappointed. Build so something stands, and you get your barn.

After Roy turned in his badge, the hardest thing, he told me, was finding out how fast the people he'd served for thirty-one years had forgotten. Then it settled on him that it didn't matter. He'd told himself it was for them. It wasn't, really. It was his to do, and God was watching. Roy found Work for God's Pay at seventy. Someday Hollis's Thursday table will be forgotten too. The two men who sit at it will still be standing.

## What's going up on your slab

A floor under your kids. They won't remember one regular dinner out of a thousand, but their bones will know whether somebody was steady.

A marriage still standing at seventy. Roy's has gone forty-four years, and it ran on coffee, not anniversaries. It got better once he retired and started making the coffee himself. When you meet an old couple who still like each other, you're looking at fifty years of pours in the dark.

A name that gets there before you do. Reputation is what people learn later about what you did unwatched. A boy's first inheritance is his father's last name. The credit a man gets at a feed store two counties over comes from forty years of bills paid on time that nobody else ever saw.

A church. It runs on men nobody thanks: the one stacking chairs, the one sitting with an old-timer in the hospital, the one who's taught fourth-grade boys for twenty-two years. The pastor gets the stage. The men who poured the floor under it are standing in the parking lot afterward, hands in their pockets, at peace.

A self. Men forget this one. Jesus said faithfulness in very little is how you get trusted with much, and the very little is the stuff nobody sees. Eli's hidden work this year was telling a customer the truth when a lie would have been free, and leaving his phone in the kitchen at night when nobody would have known.

And treasure in heaven, which is Jesus' name for where all of it went, kept by a Father who loses track of nothing.

## When nothing shows

There's a stretch in hidden work when you can't see a thing. The slab's down and no house goes up for months. A year of packed lunches and she still feels far away. Eight months of weekly calls to your son, and his answer to "what's new" is still "nothing." And the thought shows up: every hour was wasted.

:::margin
**Isaiah 49:4 NIV**
But I said, "I have labored in vain; I have spent my strength for nothing at all. Yet what is due me is in the LORD's hand, and my reward is with my God."
:::

Isaiah's servant didn't pretend the weight away. He changed where he looked. Whatever is coming to him sits in God's hand, not in the results, and not with a teenage boy who says "nothing." Before the boy finally opened up, God had watched every one of the thirty-four calls. Paul says don't quit, because harvest comes in its season. Pecan season, not yours. Those trees of Mr. Pruitt's are knee-high. He'll never eat from them. It doesn't wear him down, because he never thought the pecans were his pay.

## After the big days

You've had big days. A wedding. A baptism. A morning at a creek when you spoke a name over your son. A retirement plaque and a handshake. Those are stones on the riverbank. You live on the ordinary days after, where nobody claps and everything gets decided. So a man needs a rhythm under the moments people see.

Roy built his after the hardest marker of his life. He's still up at 5:30. The first hour goes to the Word, a chapter and a prayer that begins with "Father," before the phone gets touched. Then the floor, the family things that don't move, written where he'll see them. Twenty minutes with his brother in the same slot every week. One good thing done in secret. Before noon, a job somebody's counting on him for. A blessing, hand on a head, when there are kids in the house, and a call when they're grown and gone. A notebook with one line a day about something his wife said. At sundown he carries nothing to bed: the apology's made, the anger's handed to God. On Sunday he's in his Father's house as a man with a job there.

It shrinks to fit. Tomás has a four-month-old and ten-hour shifts. He gives the first fifteen minutes of the two-to-five feeding to a Psalm on his phone. His floor is twenty minutes after his boots come off and a prayer over the crib. At nine, dishwasher humming, he calls his brother. His secret good is letting his wife sleep an extra hour on Saturdays and never mentioning it. His sundown sounds like, "I'm so tired I scare myself, and I'm sorry, and I'm staying." No slack in that life. The rhythm lives in the cracks.

Every-other-weekend dads, your card has its own lines. The hour before the kids wake up. The midweek call. The weekend you never trade.

So write the card. Five lines or ten. That card is your slab, and everything people see sits on it.

:::margin
**Hebrews 6:10 NIV**
God is not unjust; he will not forget your work and the love you have shown him as you have helped his people and continue to help them.
:::

He won't forget. So pour. Pack the lunch, make the call, and let your Father be the one who sees.

## Before you turn the page

Long quiet grinds on a man, so let Isaiah's servant have the last word one more time: what's due me is in the Lord's hand. Keep your rhythm seven days and tell no one. May the Father who watched a carpenter's son in Nazareth watch your pour this week, and remember every day you kept.
