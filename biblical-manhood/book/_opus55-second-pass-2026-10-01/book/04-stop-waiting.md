# 4. Stop Waiting

The man I worry about isn't on the couch. He's out of the driveway before the sun, and he's good at nearly everything. Ask him to land a contract, rebuild a transmission, run the church fish fry, and it's done by Friday. But there are three or four things in his own house that would actually change it, and on those he waits. He's waited so long it doesn't feel like a choice anymore. It just feels like how things are.

This chapter is about the first of the four marks, Stop Waiting. It's simple to say and hard to live. When something is yours to do, you start now, before you feel like it.

## The stair

In one house I know, a step near the bottom of the staircase groans every time somebody lands on it. The kids learned to hop over it about the time they learned to tie their shoes. The fix is ten minutes: a couple of long screws, some glue, and the drill that's hung on the garage pegboard since forever. Their dad will tell you he plans to take care of it, and he sincerely does plan to. Those plans are the only attention that board has ever gotten.

Honestly, the stair is the least of it. What worries me is the lesson it's teaching him: that a man can see what's broken, hold the fix in his hand, and walk around it so long the detour becomes the hallway. The stair is just practice. Bigger ones are all over that house. A hard talk with his wife filed under "later." A brother he hasn't phoned since their mother's funeral. A sealed envelope from the bank. A son who went silent around fourteen, and a dad still waiting for the boy to make the first move. A pew he keeps meaning to sit back down in. It's all on his someday list, and someday is eating his years.

Here's the verse underneath this mark.

:::margin
**James 4:17 NIV**
If anyone, then, knows the good they ought to do and doesn't do it, it is sin for them.
:::

That verse locks the back door. Not doing isn't a neutral zone where a man gets to park. To God, the thing left undone is as real as the thing done.

## What we tell ourselves

Nobody's ever confessed to me, "I'm passive." Men have nicer words for it, and in a parked truck they sound almost wise.

"I didn't want to start a fight." This is the one I hear most. He's wagering that the silence he has costs less than the argument he'd get. But silence keeps its own tab, and the interest compounds whether anybody looks at it or not.

"I wasn't sure." That's the dread of being wrong, dressed in a sensible jacket. Standing still is a decision too. It has outcomes, and God will ask him about those just as surely as about a bad call.

"Nobody asked." Usually this grows out of a boyhood where speaking up got you swatted and the safest way through a room was to wait for orders. Plenty of men bring that boy along into the office, the church, and the marriage, still hoping somebody picks them. Young man, if that's you, hear it straight. Nobody is going to issue you a permit to lead your own life. The ground you're standing on is already your assignment, not "once I get my stuff together," and not "after the wedding." A wedding doesn't install anything in a man. It shows what his single years built.

"It'll work out." It won't. Weeds don't pull themselves.

:::margin
**Proverbs 24:30-31 NIV**
I went past the field of a sluggard, past the vineyard of someone who has no sense; thorns had come up everywhere, the ground was covered with weeds, and the stone wall was in ruins.
:::

Read what went wrong there. No arson. No sledgehammer to the wall. Just neglect, season after season, and thorns that never needed anyone's say-so. Distance in a marriage works like that. So does a credit card balance. So does a teenage boy who's quietly concluded his dad isn't coming.

Then there's the reason under the reasons, the one a man never says: he's comfortable. Comfort doesn't debate you. It just sets the thermostat where you like it and lets you nod off. The man who sold off the trucks or retired knows it best. His phone goes quiet, and nobody needs him unless he walks in somewhere and asks what he can carry.

## Good costumes

Passivity rarely shows up looking like laziness, because laziness is easy to spot and shame eventually gets a lazy man moving. So it dresses better.

It puts on patience: "I'm giving it some time." Sometimes that's genuine wisdom. More often he's handing time a job he won't do himself, and time has no judgment and no love for anyone under that roof.

It puts on peace: "I don't want to stir things up." So nothing gets stirred, and the peace he's protecting turns out to be a kitchen where nobody says anything costly.

It puts on humility: "Who am I to bring it up?" You're her husband. You're his dad. You're the one standing right there. That's who.

It puts on preparation. Another book, another podcast, another long conversation about the conversation. A man can become an expert on something he has never once done.

It even puts on faith: "I'm waiting on the Lord." There's an honest kind of waiting on God, and there's a counterfeit that signs His name under a pair of folded hands. We'll sort those out in a minute.

And its favorite outfit is busy. Gone before daylight, home after dark, coaching Saturdays, setting up chairs Sundays. Nobody would call that man passive, least of all him. His wife could name what he's dodging before you finished asking. Busy isn't the cure for passive. A lot of the time it's the hiding spot.

Hiding is as old as we are. When God came through the garden as the day cooled off, the first man was crouched behind the trees.

:::margin
**Genesis 3:9 NIV**
But the LORD God called to the man, "Where are you?"
:::

God knew exactly which tree. He wasn't asking for coordinates. He was asking, in effect, where's the man I set here to tend and guard this place, the one who stood beside her and said nothing? That question still moves through our houses. Tonight it'll pass your stair with your name on it.

## Waiting with your boots on

Now, Scripture really does call a man to wait on the Lord. That's a gift, and I won't pry it out of your hands. Just look at how the psalmist pictures it.

:::margin
**Psalm 27:14 NIV**
Wait for the LORD; be strong and take heart and wait for the LORD.
:::

Strong. Heart up. Those are words for somebody standing, not slouching. He does everything in reach and leaves what's out of reach to God. Think about Abraham. A quarter century between the promise of a son and the boy's arrival, and look at his calendar in those years: a move with everything he owned to a place he'd never laid eyes on, altars stacked along the way, a rescue raid with his own trained men when Lot got carried off, sitting across from kings. He never treated the promise as a reason to sit out the chores God had already handed him.

Try this the next time "I'm waiting on God" comes out of your mouth. Glance down. Hands moving on the next obvious good thing? Then you're waiting on Him. Hands in your lap until heaven sends a memo? Then it's comfort you're waiting on, and you've signed God's name to the bill.

Our Lord didn't slide into His last week by accident.

:::margin
**Luke 9:51 NIV**
As the time approached for him to be taken up to heaven, Jesus resolutely set out for Jerusalem.
:::

Resolutely. He could see the cross at the end of that road, and He pointed Himself straight at it.

## The first screw

Put the thing in calendar words. "Our marriage" can't go on a calendar. "Sit down with her Thursday about the credit card" can. "My boy" can't. "Take him for tacos Saturday, radio off, and ask how he's really doing" can. Fog can't be scheduled. A named thing can.

Next, be honest about what you're holding out for. You've got enough information. What you want is a feeling: ready, brave, certain. Those trail in behind the move like dust behind a truck. They don't lead it. The divorced man saving a call for when he's calmer will find that calm usually shows up after obedience, not ahead of it.

Then shrink the step until it's small enough to take today and still real. Opening his contact card isn't a step. Pressing call is. Lifting the drill off the pegboard isn't a step. Sinking the first screw is.

Morning is your friend. Whatever nerve you've got is freshest before lunch, and by supper the recliner has built a very persuasive argument.

Last, expect it to feel off. Years of waiting have tuned you for waiting, so the first right move will feel like a misstep for a day or so. That isn't God's verdict. It's stiff muscle getting used again.

## Seen in secret

That verse from James locked one door. Here's the one it opens. The Father who notices what we leave undone notices just as surely what a man does when nobody's around: the dark hallway at 5:45 with a drill in his hand because he's through stepping over it, the call he finally makes, the question he finally asks. God is glad over that, the way a father is glad over a son. Today, that's Stop Waiting: fix the stair, and then pick up the phone.

## Before you turn the page

Nobody taught you to skip that stair on purpose. You picked it up from the men you grew up around, the way you picked up how to stand and how to laugh. You can put it down the same way, one deliberate step at a time, and the first is the heaviest. May the God who came calling for Adam in the garden find you up and moving.
