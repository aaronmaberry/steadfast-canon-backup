# 11. When Home Never Handed You Off

Wes still spends Saturday mornings at his father's place, and he still hands the man the wrench.

He has kids in high school and a business with his name on two vans, and when the old man's disposal quits or a gutter pulls loose, Wes drives over and does the work while his father stands close enough to tell him how. His father has never once said "good." He says "should've used a longer screw." Wes texts him a photo of every job he finishes at his own house, too. The reply is always a correction. He keeps sending them.

His older brother handled the same father differently. The summer after high school he left in the middle of a fight, and he has spent the years since proving something to a man he barely speaks to. Same house, two ways out. One boy slammed the door. One boy never really left. Neither was handed off, and neither could tell you when he became a man.

Most men couldn't. They crossed from boy to man the way a river crosses a county line: no sign, nobody standing there to say so.

## What a handoff is

For most of history, boys didn't become men by accident. Men made them men, with words and a weight and a welcome. The older men took the boy, put him through something, told him what a man was, gave him a man's responsibility, and brought him into their company. He woke up a man because the men had said so and meant it.

Watch David, dying, hand off to Solomon.

:::margin
1 Kings 2:2-3 (NIV)
"'I am about to go the way of all the earth,' he said. 'So be strong, act like a man, and observe what the LORD your God requires: Walk in obedience to him, and keep his decrees and commands, his laws and regulations, as written in the Law of Moses. Do this so that you may prosper in all you do and wherever you go'"
:::

A man, older, about to leave. A word: act like a man, and here's what that means. A weight: a kingdom, with God's law inside it. Paul did it with Timothy, and he did it with his hands.

:::margin
2 Timothy 1:6 (NIV)
"For this reason I remind you to fan into flame the gift of God, which is in you through the laying on of my hands."
:::

Paul had put his hands on the young man and said, in effect, you're one of us; here's your work. Years later, when Timothy was tired and scared, Paul pointed him back to it. Remember when I handed you off. Fan it back into flame.

Four things: a man, a word, a weight, and a welcome. Most of you got none. Or you got the weight without the word, from a father who piled responsibility on you before you could shave and never said you were doing a man's job. Or the word without the weight, from a dad who said "you're the man of the house now" on his way out.

## The counterfeits

A boy who isn't handed off doesn't stop wanting it. He goes looking, and the world has counterfeits ready. Sex is the first, and it doesn't work; there's no older man in it, no word, no weight, only a girl who never signed up to make anybody a man. A paycheck is the second, and a boy with a paycheck is still a boy.

The third is the slam: the fight, the drink, the birthday that's supposed to change something, the leaving in the middle of an argument. Every one is a boy trying to hand himself off, and you can't. A handoff is received, from men.

The fourth is the quietest, and it's the orbit. A man who was never handed off can spend his whole adult life circling the house he grew up in, waiting for someone inside it to tell him he can stop. And a man who doesn't know he's a man doesn't take a man's weight, because he's still waiting to be told it's his. Everything else this book asks of a man rests on this.

## Jacob's night

There's a man in Genesis who spent half his life trying to steal a handoff. Jacob wanted his father's blessing so badly that he dressed as his brother, lied to a blind old man, and got the words. It didn't work. He spent the next twenty years running and scheming. The stolen blessing didn't make him a man; it made him a fugitive.

Then one night, alone, on the road home, a Man came and wrestled him until dawn, and Jacob did the one thing he had never done. He stopped scheming and held on.

:::margin
Genesis 32:26 (NIV)
"Then the man said, 'Let me go, for it is daybreak.' But Jacob replied, 'I will not let you go unless you bless me.'"
:::

That's a boy who has finally stopped stealing what he needs and started asking for it. He got a new name, which meant he wasn't the grabber anymore, and a limp, which meant he'd never walk like a boy again, and he walked toward his brother the next morning like a man. The handoff he couldn't take from his father, he received from God, on the ground, in the dark, well into the middle of his life. If it's dark where you are, it isn't too late.

## How a man receives it now

Hear it from the Father first. He speaks identity before performance, and He has already said the words over you: this is my son, whom I love. If you're in Christ that's a settled fact, and the handoff you never got from your dad you have from your Father. Start there or nothing else holds.

Then ask an older man to say it. It will feel awkward. Do it anyway. Find a man older than you who's living what this book describes and tell him the truth: nobody ever told me I was a man. Would you? Then let him put a hand on your shoulder and say it. More older men than you'd guess have waited their whole lives to be asked for something real.

Be welcomed by brothers, a few men who know you and count you as one of them; if no circle pulled you in, go find one that will.

Take the weight before you feel ready. David said "act like a man," and it's a command. Solomon didn't feel like one; he was young and scared and told God so. David told him to act like one anyway, and the acting was part of the becoming. Carry a man's weight for a year, and the men around you will treat you like one, and one day the feeling will have caught up.

And mark it. Write down the date: the day you decided, the day the older man said it, the day you asked God in the dark. Jacob got a limp; you'll settle for a date inside the cover of your Bible. But a man who can't point to when he became a man will always half-wonder whether he did.

## When the man in the chair won't say it

Some fathers will never hand you off. Not because you haven't earned it; because they never received it themselves, or because keeping you a boy is the only way they know to stay a man. If yours is one of them: you can honor that father and stop orbiting him in the same week. Honoring him is a road we'll walk later in this book. Stopping the orbit means you quit driving over for the sentence. You go because he's your father, not because you're still waiting. That's leaving without slamming. Nothing gets broken on the way out, and nobody stands in the old house waiting for a word that isn't coming. You take the word from the Father and from men who'll give it.

Wes heard it from an older man in his church who'd asked for help swapping out a water heater. Washing up at the utility sink afterward, the man said, "I've watched you a long time. You're a good man. I'd trust you with anything I own." Wes didn't say anything. He dried his hands twice. That was a handoff, and it didn't come from the chair. It came from a man willing to stand in for it, and God arranges men like that for the ones who ask.

## Handed off in every season

The handoff was built for the young man, and he's the one least likely to get it. It can come in pieces, from any man paying attention, so go find men who pay attention. If you're a young father, you're about to be the one who hands off, and you'll think you can't because you never got it. You can, and the giving heals something. James, the divorced seat may feel un-manned: a wedding and a role came apart, and now you're not sure what you are. The Father's word over you didn't get revoked in a courtroom, and the weight you carry now is a man's weight. Let someone older say so. And if you're the older man, you're the other half of the solution. You've watched younger men and thought, that one's got it, and never said so. Saying so is the whole job, and it needs no ceremony; most of the handoffs in Scripture were a man, a word, a hand on a shoulder, and a "go." You can do that at a utility sink this week.

## Saturday, a year on

Wes still goes to his father's on Saturdays. The old man still tells him how, and it doesn't land the way it used to, because Wes isn't there for the sentence anymore. He's there because the man is his father. He wrote a date inside his Bible, and the week after he called his brother, and it was the first time the two of them had talked about their father without the fight in it.

You're allowed in. You always were, because the Father said so first. Now go find a man who'll say it out loud, and then go be a man who does.

## Before you turn the page

If nobody ever told you that you were a man, I'd like to be one voice that does. The Father who spoke over His Son at the Jordan has spoken the same words over you. May God give you the awkward courage to ask and the plain courage to tell.
