# 10. The Father Who Does Not Leave

Stand near the doors of any grade school at three and watch the kids come out. They aren't looking at the cars. They're looking for one car, and you can tell from the sidewalk which ones find it. Their backpacks slide down their shoulders, they quit looking, and they're talking before the door is open. Some don't find it. They look at every car, and one ends up on the curb with a teacher who keeps checking her phone while the line empties.

Curtis has been in that line every school day for years, twenty minutes early, radio off, because he swore his kids would never be the one on the curb. He's kept that promise. He has also never once been the kid who comes through the doors and finds the car. Nobody was in the line for him. He learned to walk home.

This chapter is about a Father you can stop looking for. He's in the line before the bell. And because I know that for some of you the word "father" is the whole problem, that's where I have to start.

## The trouble with the word

What "God is your Father" does inside a man depends on the chair at the end of his childhood table. If the chair was empty, Father means someone who isn't coming, and you believe God exists the way you believe your dad exists: somewhere, doing something, not here. If it was full and silent, Father means someone in the room with nothing to say to you, and you pray the way a man talks to a newspaper. If it was dangerous, Father means a mood you read before you speak, and you spend your Christian life working out what God is angry about today, fairly sure it's you. If it was good, you're ahead of most, but your father got tired and missed things, so somewhere in you sits the assumption that God does too.

You've been reading God through your dad. The chair was the only picture of a father you had, so you held it up to heaven and assumed that's what was there, and that picture has been poisoning your prayers for years. Scripture turns it around. God is the original and your father was a copy. Set the copy down and look at the original, through the only Man who has seen Him.

:::margin
John 14:9 (NIV)
"Jesus answered: 'Don't you know me, Philip, even after I have been among you such a long time? Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father. How can you say, "Show us the Father"?'"
:::

Whatever Jesus is like, the Father is like.

## What He is like

He comes toward you. Jesus's most famous story about a father is about a boy who left, spent everything, and came home smelling of pigs with a speech ready. The father doesn't wait at the house with his arms folded.

:::margin
Luke 15:20 (NIV)
"So he got up and went to his father. But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion for him; he ran to his son, threw his arms around him and kissed him."
:::

While he was still a long way off. You only see somebody a long way off if you've been watching the road. Grown men in that world did not run. This one did. He isn't waiting for you to get it together.

He tells you who you are before you've done anything. At Jesus's baptism, before a sermon or a healing, the Father spoke.

:::margin
Matthew 3:17 (NIV)
"And a voice from heaven said, 'This is my Son, whom I love; with him I am well pleased.'"
:::

Three things every boy needs to hear from his father, and Jesus heard them at the start, not the finish. That's the order the Father works in: He blesses first and sends you to work second. Your own father may have made you earn what should have been given. This one doesn't; if you're in Christ that voice is over you right now.

He carries you, Moses said, the way a father carries his son, all the way, not just until the boy gets heavy. He knows what you need before you ask, and the need doesn't put Him off, which the man who learned that needing is dangerous should sit with a long time. He disciplines you, because you're His; Hebrews calls the hard season God treating you as a son, and the man with a dangerous father needs to hear that discipline and rage were never the same thing. It isn't proof He left. It's proof He stayed.

And He does not leave. Never will I leave you, He says; never will I forsake you. Jesus said it another way the night before He died, to men about to watch Him taken.

:::margin
John 14:18 (NIV)
"I will not leave you as orphans; I will come to you."
:::

Orphans is the word He chose, because that's what they were about to feel like, and some of you have felt it since you were a boy. And He says: not you. I will come to you.

## The hardest thing for a man to do

This ought to be the easiest chapter in the book, and for a lot of men it's the hardest. A man who has fathered himself since he was a boy doesn't know how to be fathered. He'd tell you he believes God is a Father. But when it comes to actually being carried, actually letting somebody else hold the weight, everything in him locks. He has held his own weight so long that leaning feels like falling.

Curtis said it plainly. "I know God's my Father. I don't know how to be anybody's son. Nobody ever showed me the receiving end." That's the honest sentence, and I'd rather you say it than fake one. Here's what works in men who learn to be sons late.

Call Him Father out loud. Not God, not Lord. Father, alone, when it's awkward. Paul says the Spirit in you already cries "Abba, Father," and Abba is a small boy's word. You're allowed to use it. It will feel strange in your mouth. Say it until it doesn't.

Bring Him your exit. Anger, the itch, the drift, whichever is yours: take it to Him first, before the brother and the counselor, and don't clean it up on the way. "Father, I'm about to blow up at my kids." "Father, I'm reaching for the phone again." He isn't disgusted. He's the one watching the road.

Let Him tell you who you are: read something He said about His sons every day and say it back as true of you. A man who was never told who he was needs telling many times, and the Father is patient.

Let yourself be carried. Sometime this year something will be too heavy, and your instinct will be to grit it out alone. Instead, say out loud, "I can't carry this. Carry me." Then let a brother help. That isn't a man failing. That's a man learning to be a son, which every real man is first.

And stay in the house: the Word, where the Father talks; the church, where His other children are; prayer, where He listens. A man who only visits on holidays never learns what His voice sounds like on an ordinary afternoon.

## What this does to your other father

Once a man begins to be fathered by God, he sees his own father differently, sometimes more clearly, which is harder. But he stops needing his father to be something the man never was, and the looking stops. He can honor what was good and name what wasn't without rage. He can forgive, because he isn't waiting on a payment anymore. He may even reach toward the man, a road we'll walk later in this book. None of it is possible while you're still trying to get from your dad what only your Father can give. The chair at the end of your table was never big enough to fill the chair in your chest. He is.

## Learning it in your season

If you're young, learn this now, when you're least likely to think you need another father, and be spared decades of looking. If there's a new baby in your house, let the baby teach you: you'll look at your child asleep and feel a love that has nothing to do with anything he's done, and that is how the Father looks at you. James, the divorced seat needs this chapter most. The Father didn't leave when the marriage did, and some of you quit praying because you figured He was as disappointed in you as everybody else seemed to be. He was watching the road the whole time. And if you're older and have never once called God Father out loud, it will feel foolish for about a week and then like heat after a long time in the cold. This long has been long enough.

## Before the bell

Curtis started saying it in the pickup line, the one place he was alone where the awkwardness didn't matter. Father. At first it felt like talking to the windshield. He kept going, one sentence a day, and somewhere in the second week it stopped feeling like the windshield and started feeling like somebody in the seat beside him.

Then one afternoon his youngest came through the doors, looked down the line, found the car, and quit looking. Curtis watched it and understood what he was being offered. He'd been the man in the line for years. He'd never once been the kid who finds the car. He was being told he could be.

That's what the Father does for a man: He lets you stop scanning and stop auditioning. Your standing with Him was never about today. It was about the Son, and it's settled. See what great love the Father has lavished on us, John writes, that we should be called children of God. And that is what we are. Not what we're trying to become. What we are: sons. He was in the line before the bell. Let your shoulders down. You've been found.

## Before you turn the page

He knows sons learn slowly. Say the word tomorrow morning, badly, and again the next day. May the Father who watched the road for the prodigal watch it for you, and may you come through the doors this week and find Him already there.
