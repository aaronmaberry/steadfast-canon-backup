# 18. Before the Fall

*Part 3: The story that names you*

Every man I've ever sat with has wanted to know the same thing under all the other things. Not "how do I fix my marriage" or "how do I handle my anger" or "how do I get my son back." Under those. The question under those is: what was I made for?

Not what should I do. What was I *for*. What's the shape of the thing, before I bent it. Because if you don't know what a thing was for, you can't tell whether it's broken. A crowbar makes a lousy hammer, and a man who doesn't know he's a crowbar will spend his whole life hitting nails and wondering why it hurts his hands.

We leave your story for a minute and go back to the beginning of the master story. This chapter is the man before anything went wrong. Not Adam as a theology lesson. Adam as a blueprint. What God built when He built a man, before the fall, before the chair was ever empty, before there was a single exit to take. If you want to know what you were for, this is where the answer is. I've never found a man whose deepest ache didn't trace back to one of the things on this list.

## He was made, on purpose, in an image

The first thing Scripture says about you is not that you're a sinner. That comes in chapter 3. The first thing it says is this.

:::margin
Genesis 1:27 (NIV)
"So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them."
:::

In his own image. Before you did anything, before you failed at anything, you were made to look like God. To represent Him. To be the thing in creation that, when the animals and the angels look at it, reminds them of Him. That's not a status you earn; it's a status you were born with, and it's why a man's life has weight even on the Tuesdays when he does nothing worth mentioning. You carry the image whether you're carrying anything else or not.

And notice: male and female. Both carry the image, fully. Your manhood isn't a step above your wife or your daughter. It's a distinct way of carrying the same image, and it was God's idea, not the culture's. When chapter 2 told you that you're not crazy for sensing that manhood is a real thing and not a costume, this is why. God made it. It's in the blueprint.

:::margin
Genesis 2:7 (NIV)
"Then the LORD God formed a man from the dust of the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living being."
:::

Formed. Breathed. That's a craftsman working with his hands and then getting close enough to breathe. You were not an accident and you were not mass-produced. You were formed by hand and God got close.

## He was blessed and given a job before he'd done anything

The first words God says to the man and woman are a blessing and a commission, in that order.

:::margin
Genesis 1:28 (NIV)
"God blessed them and said to them, 'Be fruitful and increase in number; fill the earth and subdue it. Rule over the fish in the sea and the birds in the sky and over every living creature that moves on the ground.'"
:::

Blessed first. We saw that pattern in chapter 10 with the Father at Jesus's baptism: identity before performance. It's here at the very beginning. God blesses the man before the man has lifted a finger, and then He gives him something enormous to do.

Be fruitful. Fill. Subdue. Rule. Those are big verbs. They mean that a man was made to take raw creation and make more of it. Not to sit in the garden and enjoy it, though he was allowed to. To take the wild thing and bring it to order, to take the empty thing and fill it, to take what God made good and make it *more*. A man is a maker. That's why you feel most alive when you've built something, planted something, fixed something, started something, brought some piece of chaos into order. That's not ambition. That's the blueprint showing.

Subdue and rule don't mean bully. Look at what God does with His rule in the first two chapters: He makes things, He blesses things, He calls things good, He plants a garden. Ruling like God rules means making things flourish under your hand. A man rules his acre by making it fruitful, his crew by making it better, his house by making it a place where everybody in it grows. Domination is what rule looks like after the fall. Before the fall it looked like gardening.

## He was placed

This one is easy to skip. Don't.

:::margin
Genesis 2:8 (NIV)
"Now the LORD God had planted a garden in the east, in Eden; and there he put the man he had formed."
:::

There he put the man. God didn't make Adam and let him wander around looking for the right place. He put him somewhere, on purpose, in a specific garden with specific trees and specific rivers. Place was part of the design.

You're somewhere. A town, a job, a street, a church, a house. Most men treat where they are as either an accident or a waiting room, something they'll leave for the real place as soon as they can. The blueprint says otherwise. God puts men in places. The place you're in on Tuesday, with its problems and its people and its particular dirt, is a garden God put you in, and He expects you to work it as if you were meant to be there, because you were. A man who's always looking for the next place never tends the one he's standing in.

## He was given work, and told to guard it

Here's the verse I'd put where a man couldn't miss it.

:::margin
Genesis 2:15 (NIV)
"The LORD God took the man and put him in the Garden of Eden to work it and take care of it."
:::

To work it and take care of it. Two verbs. Before the fall. Before the curse. Before anything had gone wrong, a man had a job, and it was a real job with dirt in it.

A lot of Christian men have quietly absorbed the idea that work is the curse. It isn't. The curse made work hard: thorns, sweat, ground that fights you. But work itself is in the design, and a man who doesn't have real work to do isn't resting; he's malfunctioning. This is why a man's soul goes sideways when he's laid off, or retires with nothing to do, or gets hurt and can't. It's not just the money. It's that he was made to work a garden and somebody took the garden.

And the second verb matters as much as the first. Take care of it. The Hebrew has the sense of keeping, guarding, watching over. A man is not just a worker. He's a keeper. Something's been put in his charge and he's supposed to watch it, protect it, make sure nothing gets in that shouldn't. Provider and protector are both in that one verse, and they're the same job, and the job came before the fall.

Hold that thought about guarding. Because in the next chapter something's going to walk into the garden, and the man who was told to keep it is going to stand there.

## He was given a word, and a line

Now the man is placed, working, keeping. And God speaks to him. Not to the couple. Not yet. To the man alone, because there is no woman yet.

:::margin
Genesis 2:16-17 (NIV)
"And the LORD God commanded the man, 'You are free to eat from any tree in the garden; but you must not eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, for when you eat from it you will certainly die.'"
:::

You are free. That comes first, and men forget it. The garden was enormous and almost all of it was yes. One tree was no. That's the ratio of God's design for a man: a wide field of freedom with one clear line, and a plain warning about what's on the other side.

A man is a creature under a word. He doesn't invent his own standard; that's what everyone did in the days of the Judges, and we saw how that went. He receives a word from Someone above him and lives inside it. That's not a limitation on manhood. It's the thing that makes manhood possible, because a man without a word is a man with no north star, and we spent last chapter on him.

And notice who received it. The man. Before Eve existed. He was the one who heard the command from God's own mouth. He was the one who'd be responsible to carry it, to teach it, to keep it, to guard the garden against anything that would try to talk them across the line. That's going to matter enormously in the next chapter, when the line gets crossed and he's standing right there.

## He was given words of his own

God did something with Adam that He didn't do with anything else He'd made. He let him name things.

:::margin
Genesis 2:19-20 (NIV)
"Now the LORD God had formed out of the ground all the wild animals and all the birds in the sky. He brought them to the man to see what he would name them; and whatever the man called each living creature, that was its name. So the man gave names to all the livestock, the birds in the sky and all the wild animals. But for Adam no suitable helper was found."
:::

Whatever the man called each living creature, that was its name. God brought the animals to Adam and let him speak, and what he spoke stuck. That's authority given by delegation. God, who had been naming things for six days, light and day and sky and sea, handed the naming to the man.

A man's words carry weight. That's in the blueprint. What you call your son, he'll be. What you call your wife, in front of the kids and in your own head, shapes what she becomes in your house. What you name a situation, a crisis or a challenge, a disaster or a Tuesday, is largely what it becomes. Men underestimate this because they don't talk much. But the few words a man does say land like hammer blows, because God built the world to listen when a man speaks. We're going to spend a whole chapter in Part 4 on giving your son a name. It starts here.

## He was told it was not good to be alone

Now the sentence that changes everything.

:::margin
Genesis 2:18 (NIV)
"The LORD God said, 'It is not good for the man to be alone. I will make a helper suitable for him.'"
:::

Not good. God had said "good" about everything so far. Light, land, plants, stars, fish, birds, beasts. Good, good, good, very good. And then He looks at a man by himself, in a perfect garden, with meaningful work and God's own voice to talk to, and says: not good.

The man wasn't sinning. He wasn't lonely in the way a man is lonely after a divorce. He had everything, including God, and God Himself said a man alone is not good. Your need for other people isn't weakness. It isn't a character flaw you'd overcome if you were stronger. It's the blueprint. God built you needing others, on purpose, before anything broke.

And look at what He didn't do. He didn't bring the animals and say, "Here, one of these will do." The text goes out of its way to say no suitable helper was found among them. The man's need wasn't for company. Dogs are company. His need was for a counterpart. Someone who was like him and not him. Someone who could stand across from him and see him and be seen. That's what "suitable" means: corresponding, matching, fitting like a hand fits a hand.

Now, most men read this as a marriage verse, and it is one. But hear it wider. "Not good for the man to be alone" is the reason for chapter 13 and chapter 14. It's why you need brothers and a man ahead of you and a church, not just a wife. The first remedy God made was a bride. It wasn't the only kind of not-alone He had in mind, and a single man is not exempt from the verse; he's just going to need to find his not-alone in more than one place.

## He was given a bride, and he spoke

God put the man to sleep and built the woman from his side. Not from the dust, like him. From him. Then He brought her, the way He'd brought the animals, and the man spoke again, and what he said is the first recorded human speech in the Bible.

:::margin
Genesis 2:23 (NIV)
"The man said, 'This is now bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called "woman," for she was taken out of man.'"
:::

The first thing a man ever said was a poem about his wife. Most husbands I know can't remember the last time they said anything to their wife that wasn't logistics. The blueprint has a man looking at his bride and being moved to speech. Bone of my bones. Flesh of my flesh. This one. Finally.

Then the text tells us what a man does with a bride: he leaves his father and mother and is united to her, one flesh. We spent chapter 12 on the leaving. Here I only want to point at the joining. Marriage is in the blueprint before the fall. It's not a social invention or a legal arrangement or a religious add-on. It's the first human covenant, spoken by God over the first two people, and the man was given the job of holding fast.

And then this, which is the last sentence before everything breaks.

:::margin
Genesis 2:25 (NIV)
"Adam and his wife were both naked, and they felt no shame."
:::

No shame. No drawer. Nothing hidden, from each other or from God. That's what a man looked like before the fall: fully seen and unafraid of it. Every locked drawer since is a man trying to get back to the garden by the wrong door.

## He was given rest

One more, and it's the one men skip because it doesn't look like a man's verse.

:::margin
Genesis 2:2-3 (NIV)
"By the seventh day God had finished the work he had been doing; so on the seventh day he rested from all his work. Then God blessed the seventh day and made it holy, because on it he rested from all the work of creating that he had done."
:::

God rested. Not because He was tired. Because rest is part of how good work goes. He built it into the week before the man ever picked up a tool, and He called it holy, which He didn't call the other six days.

A man is not a machine. The blueprint has him working six days and stopping one, and the stopping isn't a break from the design; it *is* the design. Men who work seven days aren't more manly. They've torn a page out of the blueprint and are wondering why the house won't stand. The drift we talked about in chapter 9 is often what happens to a man who never learned to rest: he never stops, so eventually he just goes numb, which is not rest, it's a breakdown with the television on.

## The shape of a man

So here's the man before the fall. Made in God's image, by hand, on purpose. Blessed before he'd done anything and then given a real job: make this place more than it is. Put somewhere specific. Given work with dirt in it and told to guard what he'd been given. Given a word from above with a wide field of freedom and one clear line. Given words of his own that carried weight. Told he wasn't built to be alone. Given a bride, and moved to poetry by her. Naked and unashamed. And given rest.

That's the shape. That's what you were for.

Now go back through the book with that list in your hand. Chapter 4, the man on the tailgate: that's a man who was made to work it and keep it, sitting still. Chapter 5, the man who won't pick up weight: that's a man who was given a word and a garden and won't carry either. Chapter 8, the empty chair: that's a boy who was supposed to be blessed first and named by his father, and wasn't. Chapter 9, the itch: that's a man who was built for one flesh and no shame, settling for a screen and a locked drawer. Chapter 13, the man with no brothers: God said it was not good, and the man decided he was fine. Chapter 16, the locked drawer: naked and unashamed, inverted.

Every ache traces back. Every exit is a man walking away from something on this list. And every one of the four marks is a piece of the blueprint. Rejecting passivity is a man doing what he was made to do: work it and keep it. Accepting responsibility is a man carrying the word he was given. Leading courageously is a man using the authority God delegated to him, speaking, naming, going first. And expecting the greater reward is a man remembering that the whole garden was built around walking with God in the cool of the day, and that nothing else in it was ever the point.

## In your season

If you're single, notice something. Almost every item on this list was given to Adam while he was alone. The image, the blessing, the commission, the place, the work, the keeping, the word, the naming. He was a full man before there was a bride. God's remedy for his aloneness came, but the design was complete without it. You don't become a man when you get married. You become a man by living the blueprint, and a wife is one of the ways God fills the "not good to be alone," not the thing that makes you count. Work your garden. Guard it. Carry the word. The rest is God's timing.

If you've got a baby in the house, you're standing in the middle of Genesis 1:28. Be fruitful. You did it. And it's harder than it sounded on the day. That's the curse on top of the blessing: the design is still good and the ground still fights you. Hold both. The exhaustion doesn't mean you're off the blueprint. It means you're working it.

If you're divorced, "it is not good for the man to be alone" is still true about you, and it hurts to read, and I'm sorry. God hasn't changed His mind about the design because a marriage broke. He's not asking you to rush into another one; He's telling you that the not-alone is still in your blueprint, and it has more than one shape. Brothers first. A man ahead of you. A church that knows your name. The bride question is His to answer in His time, and you can be a whole man on this list while you wait.

If you're older, look at Genesis 2:15 and find the retirement clause. There isn't one. The work-and-keep design didn't expire when the paycheck did. The garden changed; you're not the keeper of a payroll anymore. But you're the keeper of something. Grandchildren. Younger men. A wife who's carried a lot. A church full of people who need a man who's got time and has learned things. Roy told me the day he read Genesis 2:15 after retiring was the day he stopped feeling like a man who'd been let go and started feeling like a man who'd been reassigned. "Same job," he said. "Different garden."

## What's coming

The man in this chapter is standing in a garden he was told to work and keep. He's got God's word in his ear, the one about the tree, the one only he heard. He's got a bride he sang over. He's got nothing hidden. He's got authority to speak, and everything listens when he does.

And something is about to come through the fence.

The next chapter is about what he did when it came, and about another Man, a long time later, who stood in a different garden with everything on the line and did the opposite. Two men. Two ways. Everything you've ever done as a man is a version of one of them.

Before you turn the page, sit here a minute longer. You were made for this. Not for the exits. Not for the drawer. Not for the tailgate. For a garden, a word, a work, a bride, a company of others, a rest, and a walk with God in the cool of the day. That's what you're for. That's the shape you were bent from, and it's the shape you're being bent back to.
