# 5. Pick up the weight

There is a weight on the floor.

Everyone has learned the path around it. Kids step over it on the way to the fridge. She steps over it and says nothing, which is how you know it has been there a while. You step over it too, and you have a reason. Not now. After this week. After the quarter. After you feel less tired. The weight does not care about your reason. Weights don't move because the weather improved. They move when a man puts a hand on them.

I have walked around my own. I got good at it. I could cross a room and never glance at what was mine, which is a talent, and a kind of shame. People even thanked me for how easy I made the house feel. Easy is what you get when nobody is asking you to carry what is yours. I mistook that ease for peace in the house. It was only an empty hallway.

A real man accepts responsibility. That is this page. We are not reciting the four. We are not back on the wait. The wait was sitting still. This is the load with your name on it, still sitting there after you stood up. Standing is not carrying. A man can get off the floor and still leave the load right where it was.

It might be a bill you keep sliding. A boy who needs more than your leftover hour. A name you have not put on a problem at work because putting your name on it would mean it could fail in public. A body you have been treating like a rental. A church you attend like a visitor. The specifics change. The move does not. You put a hand on it.

Paul said each one should carry their own load. That's Galatians 6:5.

:::margin
**Galatians 6:5 NIV**
for each one should carry their own load.
:::

He is not crowning the self-made man. He is not telling you to refuse help. He is saying there is a load that belongs to you, and it will not become someone else's because you are tired of seeing it.

A load is not a vibe. It has edges. You can name it in a sentence if you quit decorating it. "The truck payment." "The talk with my son." "The mess I left in that friendship." If you cannot name it, you are still walking around it. Naming is the first lift. Not the whole lift. The first. A man who will not name his load will spend years being vaguely tired and calling it a mystery.

Some of you are carrying everyone else's and calling that responsibility. It isn't. That is a different sickness. Rescuing the room so you never have to face your own floor. This chapter is not about being the hero of other men's loads. It is about the one that is yours. Theirs can wait. Yours cannot.

If you're young, the weight is probably the job you already have, the money you already spend, the man you already said you'd become. You keep waiting for a more official version of your life to arrive and issue you a load. You already have one. Pick it up before you are issued a bigger one you also will not carry.

If you're single, the life is already yours. No wife is coming to inherit the pile. The dishes, the debt, the neighbor, the hours after work: those are not practice. They are the load. A man who will not carry a small life will not magically carry a larger one.

If you're married, hear this without me turning it into a speech at her. Some of what is on the floor is yours. She has been stepping around it. She may even have picked it up so the house could function, and now you think it was never yours. It was. Put a hand on it. Do not make a ceremony out of returning what you should have been carrying. Just carry it.

If you got hurt on the way here, I am not handing you a sack and calling it healing. Some loads got put on you that were never yours. You can set those down. This page is not that. This page is the one that is yours, even after the hurt. Kind is not the same as empty-handed. You can be wounded and still have a load with your name on it.

If you're older, you still have one. Age does not retire a man from his own floor. It can shrink the load. It can also hide it under "I've done my time." A 62-year-old can still pick up what is his. The younger men need to see a hand close on something, not just hear about the years he already carried. Stories about old loads do not lift the one on the floor tonight.

If the job is going well, this one will sting. Owning a company is not the same as owning the hour after you walk in. You can be responsible for forty people and irresponsible about one house. You can close a book of business and leave the real weight in the hallway. The office will call you reliable. Reliable at work and absent at home is not responsibility. It is a talent for picking up the load that applauds you, and leaving the one that would make you a husband, a father, a brother.

I have picked up the wrong weight because it made me look useful. I have left the right one because it would have made me look ordinary. Ordinary is often the holy one. The holy one is rarely the one that gets you a story.

Responsibility has a sound. It is not a speech. It is a man saying, this is mine, and then the next small act that proves he meant it. Until the act, it is still just a sentence.

Some of you are waiting for a cleaner version of the weight. A more noble load. A more public one. The floor does not offer that. It offers the one that is already there, scuffed, unpaid, unsaid. That is the one that will make you a man, or keep you a tourist in your own house.

Put a hand on it this week. Not ten weights. The one everyone is already stepping around. Name it out loud. Then do the next inch. Pay the thing. Make the appointment. Tell the boy a time and keep it. You do not have to finish the load by Friday. You have to stop pretending it is furniture. Furniture is what we call a problem we have decided to live with.

Tell one man the name of the weight, and that you put a hand on it. If you only confess the pile, you will get a nod and keep stepping around it. If you tell him you lifted, he can ask you whether you dropped it again. Let him ask. Brothers are for loads, not for hiding them in a nicer vocabulary.

The weight that is yours does not move until you put a hand on it. Accept responsibility. Pick it up.

Name it. What weight is on the floor that everyone has learned to step around?

Do this week. Put a hand on it. One inch. Not a speech.
