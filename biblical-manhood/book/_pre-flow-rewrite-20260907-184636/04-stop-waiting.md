# 4. Stop Waiting

*Part 1: The man you were built to be*

There's a smoke detector in a hallway somewhere near you chirping right now. One chirp every forty seconds or so. The battery's low. The man of the house has walked under it maybe two hundred times in the last three weeks. He knows exactly what it is. He knows exactly where the nine-volts are. He's a capable man who has rebuilt a carburetor and negotiated a raise and coached a ten-year-old's baseball team, and he cannot bring himself to spend the ninety seconds it would take to make that chirp stop.

Ask him why and he'll tell you he's going to get to it. That's the honest answer, and it's also the whole problem.

I don't care about the smoke detector. I care about what the smoke detector is standing in for. Because that same man has a conversation with his wife he's going to get to. He's got a call to his brother he's going to get to. He's got a habit he's going to deal with, a debt he's going to face, a son who's gone quiet that he's going to sit down with, a church he's going to start showing up at again. He's going to get to it. He's been going to get to it for years.

The first mark of a man is that he rejects passivity. This chapter is about what that actually looks like on a Tuesday, because the word "passivity" makes most men picture somebody lying on a couch, and that's not the man I'm worried about. The man I'm worried about is busy. He's tired. He's working hard on everything except the things that matter most, and he's waiting.

## What passivity wears

Passivity almost never shows up in its own clothes. If it did, we'd recognize it and be ashamed. Instead it borrows the wardrobe of virtues, and it wears them well.

It wears patience. "I'm giving it time." Sometimes that's true. More often, time is doing what you're refusing to do, and it's doing it badly.

It wears peace. "I don't want to start something." So the thing that needs starting never starts, and the peace you're keeping is the peace of a house where nobody's talking.

It wears humility. "Who am I to say anything?" You're the husband. You're the father. You're the one who's there. That's who.

It wears spirituality. "I'm waiting on the Lord." There's a real kind of waiting on God, and there's a counterfeit that uses His name as a doctor's note. I'll come back to that.

It wears research. Another book, another podcast, another conversation about the thing instead of the thing. I've known men who have read six books on marriage and never once asked their wife a question and then listened to the whole answer.

And it wears busyness, which is the cleverest disguise of all. A man who's out the door at 5:40 and home at 7:15 and coaching on Saturdays and fixing the church van on Sunday afternoon does not look passive. But ask his wife what he's avoiding and she'll be able to tell you without taking a breath. Motion isn't the opposite of passivity. Motion is often how a man hides from the one thing standing still in the middle of his life.

The first man did this. When God came walking in the garden after the fruit, Adam was hiding in the trees, and God asked him a question that wasn't really a question.

:::margin
Genesis 3:9 (NIV)
"But the LORD God called to the man, 'Where are you?'"
:::

God knew where he was. The question was for Adam. Where are you? Not where is your body. Where are you, the man I put here to work and to guard, the man who was standing right there when it all went wrong and said nothing? That question is still walking through gardens. It walks through your house. Where are you?

## Why we wait

I've asked a lot of men why they didn't move when they knew they should have. Nobody has ever said "because I'm passive." They say this.

*I didn't want to make it worse.* This is fear of conflict, and it's the most common one. A man figures the current quiet is better than the noise he'd make by speaking. What he doesn't figure is that quiet has a cost too, and it compounds.

*I wasn't sure I was right.* This is fear of failure. He'd rather not act than act wrong. But not acting is also acting. It's a decision with consequences, and it's one you'll have to answer for just the same.

*Nobody asked me.* This one runs deep in a lot of men, and it usually traces back to a house where a boy learned that initiative got punished. Maybe his dad ran everything and there was no room. Maybe his dad was gone and there was nobody to show him how a man starts things. Either way he learned to wait to be invited, and now he's forty-three and still waiting for an invitation that isn't coming, because grown men don't get invited to lead their own lives.

*It'll work itself out.* It won't. Fields don't weed themselves. Proverbs has a picture of this that's almost gentle in how it's told, and that's what makes it land.

:::margin
Proverbs 24:30-31 (NIV)
"I went past the field of a sluggard, past the vineyard of someone who has no sense; thorns had come up everywhere, the ground was covered with weeds, and the stone wall was in ruins."
:::

Notice what the sluggard didn't do. He didn't burn the field. He didn't tear down the wall. He just didn't do anything, for long enough, and the world did the rest. Thorns don't need your permission. Neither does distance in a marriage, or debt, or a teenager's silence.

*I'm comfortable.* Nobody says this one out loud, but it's under a lot of the others. The tailgate is comfortable. The hallway with the chirping detector is comfortable enough. And comfort is the most persuasive argument in a man's life, because it never has to raise its voice.

## What waiting on God actually looks like

Now, about the doctor's note.

The Bible does tell men to wait on the Lord, and I don't want to take that from you. But look at how it says it.

:::margin
Psalm 27:14 (NIV)
"Wait for the LORD; be strong and take heart and wait for the LORD."
:::

Be strong and take heart. That's not a man on a tailgate. That's a man on his feet with his jaw set. Biblical waiting is what you do about the things you can't control while you do everything about the things you can. Abraham waited twenty-five years for a son, and while he waited he moved his whole household across a continent, dug wells, fought a battle, and negotiated with kings. Waiting on God never once meant sitting still on the parts that were his to do.

So here's a test, and it's the one I use on myself. If you say you're waiting on God, ask: what am I doing while I wait? If the answer is "the next thing I already know is right," you're waiting on God. If the answer is "nothing, until He makes it obvious," you're waiting on comfort and calling it faith. God rarely makes the next step obvious to a man who won't take the one already in front of him.

Jesus never waited that way. When the time came for Him to go to Jerusalem, knowing what waited for Him there, Luke uses a word I love.

:::margin
Luke 9:51 (NIV)
"As the time approached for him to be taken up to heaven, Jesus resolutely set out for Jerusalem."
:::

Resolutely. He set His face and went. That's the Man we're following. Not a man who felt ready. A man who went.

## What this looks like in your season

A few of you need this turned in your direction.

If you're single, the most dangerous sentence in your life right now is "when I get married." When I get married I'll get serious about church. When I get married I'll get my money straight, deal with what I watch, learn to cook, call my dad. A wife isn't a starting gun. The man you are on the Tuesday before you meet her is the man she'll get, and if you wait for her to make you start, you'll hand her a project instead of a husband. Start now. Every bit of it.

If you've got a baby in the house, you're going to be tempted to wait for "when things calm down" to turn back toward your wife. Things aren't going to calm down; they're going to change shape. The turn toward her has to happen inside the chaos. Ten minutes at the kitchen counter after the baby's down, phone face down, is not waiting for a better season. It is the season.

If you're divorced, there's probably a call you've been waiting to make until the anger cools, a conversation with your kids' mother about the schedule or the school or the thing your daughter said. The anger isn't going to cool on its own. It cools when you act like a man in spite of it. Make the call. Keep it short and kind and about the kids.

If you're retired, you may be waiting to be asked. Waiting for the church to call, for a younger man to seek you out, for your grandkids to want your time. Roy, a friend of mine who turned in a state trooper's badge after thirty-one years, told me the hardest part of the first year was realizing nobody was going to hand him an assignment ever again. "I sat by the phone like a rookie," he said. "Then one morning I got up and went to find the work." You'll meet Roy again in this book. He stopped waiting at sixty-eight.

## How to get off the tailgate

Rejecting passivity is a habit, not a moment. Here's the habit.

**Name the thing.** Most men carry three or four things they're "going to get to," and they've never once said them out loud in a single sentence. Say them. Write them if you have to. "I need to talk to my wife about money. I need to call my brother. I need to deal with what I've been watching. I need to fix the smoke detector." Passivity lives in vagueness. It hates a list.

**Ask what you're really waiting for.** For each one: is there an honest reason to wait, or a comfortable one? An honest reason sounds like "I need to gather one more fact" or "it's better to talk when the kids are asleep, which is tonight." A comfortable reason sounds like "when things settle down" or "when I feel ready." Feeling ready is not a prerequisite for anything a man has to do. Nobody has ever felt ready to bury a parent or hold a newborn or tell his wife the truth about his debt.

**Find the smallest first move.** Not the whole conversation. The sentence that starts it. Not the whole financial overhaul. The one account you log into tonight. Not a new life. Changing the battery. Passivity is beaten by small, real motion, not by big resolutions, because big resolutions are one more way to feel like you did something.

**Move before noon.** This is a Tuesday rule. If you've named the thing and found the first move, do it before lunch. Waiting for the evening is waiting. Waiting for the weekend is waiting. Men who reject passivity have learned to hate the gap between knowing and doing, because that gap is where a whole life can disappear.

**Expect it to feel wrong.** Almost every right move a man makes against passivity feels awkward the first time. The conversation will start badly. Your voice will sound strange to you. She may not respond the way you hoped. That's not a sign you shouldn't have moved. It's the sound of a man doing something he's out of practice at. Keep going.

## The reward for moving

The fourth mark makes the other three possible. Here's how it touches this one.

There's a line in James that most men read as a warning, and it is one, but it's also a promise.

:::margin
James 4:17 (NIV)
"If anyone, then, knows the good they ought to do and doesn't do it, it is sin for them."
:::

The warning: knowing and not doing is sin. God takes your passivity personally, because He put you where you are on purpose, the way He put Adam in the garden, and He's asking where you are.

The promise hiding in it: God sees the good you ought to do. He knows about the smoke detector and the conversation and the call. Which means He also sees when you move. Nobody else may notice. Your wife may not thank you for finally bringing up money. Your brother may not call you back. But the Father who sees in secret watches a man get off the tailgate, and He's not indifferent to it. That's a reward worth working for, and it's why you can move on a Tuesday when nothing in you feels like it.

Stop waiting. Change the battery. Then make the call.

---

**Name it.** What's the one thing you've been "going to get to" the longest? Say how long, honestly. "I've been waiting ___ months/years to ___."

**Do this week.** Do the smallest first move on that one thing before noon on a workday. Not the whole thing. The first sentence, the first login, the first call.

**Tell a brother.** Tell him what you've been waiting on and what you did about it. If you didn't do it, tell him that too, and give him permission to ask you next week.

**Carry this.** Where are you? God knew where Adam was. The question was for Adam.
