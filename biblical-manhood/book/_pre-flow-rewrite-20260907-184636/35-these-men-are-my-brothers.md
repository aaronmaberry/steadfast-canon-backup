# 35. These Men Are My Brothers

*Part 5: The long road*

It's a Tuesday as I write this. Nothing special about it. Gray sky, a little wind out of the north, the kind of day nobody remembers. I've been thinking about the men in this book, where they are today, on this particular unremarkable Tuesday, and I want to tell you before I let you go.

Not because they're impressive. None of them is famous. None of them will be. If you passed any of them at a gas station you wouldn't look twice. They're the argument of the whole book, and the argument is this: the four marks are not for exceptional men. They're for men. Ordinary ones, in every season, on Tuesdays, and I've watched them hold.

## Where they are

Roy is seventy-two now. He got up at 5:30 this morning and made the coffee and sat on the porch with his wife while she drank it, and she told him about a dream she'd had, and he listened to the whole thing and asked a question at the end. Then he read Proverbs 12 out loud. Then he drove to the church to look at a leak in the fellowship hall roof that nobody asked him to look at. Thursday he'll sit at a table with two men in their thirties and ask them what's heavy, and they'll tell him. His grandson has a plumb bob in his pocket at school right now. Roy's daughter calls on Sundays. He turned in a badge five years ago and thought his work was over, and he'll tell you, if you ask, that the last five years have been the most important of his life and nobody's clapped once.

Tomás's daughter is two. He walked her around the block at six this evening like he's done since she was three months old, and she talked the whole way. His wife got five hours of sleep in a row last night because he took the first shift, and she's a person again, and the bed came back months ago, better than before, because she learned in the worst year that she could trust him with her body when it was weakest. He's tired. He works ten hours on a pipeline crew and he's twenty-nine and he doesn't feel like a man most days. He's more of one than he knows. His brother calls at nine while he does the dishes.

Kenny's kids are with their mother this week. He called his son at 7:30 tonight, like every Tuesday, and the boy talked for twenty minutes about a smoker they built over eleven weekends and a girl he's not sure about. Kenny said, before they hung up, "You're my son. I love you. I'll call you Tuesday." His daughter texted him a picture of a school project. Three years ago he was a mad, lonely ghost in a one-bedroom apartment who'd stopped praying because he figured God was as done with him as everyone else seemed to be. An older man called him every Tuesday night for a year and asked three questions and wouldn't let him hang up until he'd answered. Now the divorced men at his church talk to Kenny, because he's the only one they'll let in. He didn't choose that. He's serving God's purpose in his generation with the thing he thought disqualified him.

Micah's married. He and his wife had a couple over for dinner tonight, a younger couple, and he cooked. He still meets Dale every other Tuesday for breakfast, and he's started meeting a twenty-four-year-old on Thursdays who reminds him of himself at the back table. He wrote a date inside his Bible a few years ago, the day a man told him he was a man and to stop waiting for permission. He looks at it sometimes.

Walt sold off part of the business. He was home for dinner tonight, like four nights out of five, and he and his wife are planning a trip for the fall that she picked. He has a brother now; they talk Thursdays at noon on the drive to lunch. He told me the empty nest turned out to be the best stretch of his marriage because for the first time since 1994 it was just the two of them and he actually showed up for it. He still thinks about the bank parking lot in Abilene. He calls it the day he found out he had three hundred contacts and nobody.

Anthony walked into his kitchen tonight. Didn't stand in the doorway. His daughter looked up from her homework to see what his face would do when her mother came in from the car, and it did the right thing, and the girl smiled and went back to her fractions. He lost a fight he was winning last week. He's been working on it. He says the word "Father" in the truck every morning and it still feels a little foolish and he does it anyway.

Cole names his son every night. Three sentences, hand on the boy's head. He drove out to his father's place Sunday and they looked at a pump together and didn't say much, which is the closest thing to an embrace his father has, and Cole took it, because he stopped needing more than the man could give. A seventy-four-year-old deacon told him he was a good man by a truck four years ago and he sat in the cab and cried for twenty minutes. He's thirty-seven. He's not waiting anymore.

Hollis wrote tomorrow's three in a notebook tonight before bed. He's seventy-four. He's never had a calling and he's served God's purpose in his generation for fifty-five years by doing the next thing God put in front of him. One of tomorrow's three is "Call Roy re: roof."

Mr. Pruitt's pecan trees are a little past knee-high. He drank coffee with Ed at the gas station this morning like every Wednesday for four years, and they talked about the price of cattle and Ed's grandson and nothing in particular, and it was the most important twenty minutes of Mr. Pruitt's week. He's seventy-six. He won't eat one of those pecans. He's the most settled man in the county.

Mr. Alvarez bathed his wife this morning. She didn't know his name. He knew hers. Then he called a widower from church and asked him what was heavy. He's seventy-eight. He said "in sickness" in 1968 and he meant the whole thing.

The man at the wastewater plant is on shift right now, as I write, running his checks and praying over four thousand sleeping people who don't know he exists.

And the young man with four jobs in three years stayed at the fifth. He runs a crew now. Two of the men on it started coming to church because of how he treats them. He still doesn't have a calling. He has a purpose, and it turned out to be wherever he stopped running.

## What they have in common

Look at that list. A retired trooper, a pipeliner, an HVAC man, a help desk tech, a business owner, a guy who stands in doorways, a mechanic, a farmer, a widower, a caregiver, a plant operator, a crew chief. Twenty-four to eighty. Single, married, divorced, widowed, newborn in the house, empty nest, retired, sick. Every season in the book.

Not one of them is remarkable. That's the point. I didn't pick them because they were the best men I know. I picked them because they're the men I know, and every one of them, in his own season, on his own Tuesdays, is doing the four things.

Every one of them got off the tailgate. Roy went and found the work. Tomás turned toward his wife inside the chaos instead of waiting for it to calm down. Kenny made the call before the anger cooled. They rejected passivity.

Every one of them picked up weight that was theirs and some that wasn't. Walt sold trucks to be home. Cole reached toward a father who never reached back. Mr. Alvarez picks up a washcloth every morning. They accepted responsibility.

Every one of them went first. Anthony into the apology. Micah into the sentence at a young man's table. Hollis across a parking lot to a man by a truck. Roy into his daughter's driveway. They led courageously.

And every one of them is doing it for a Father who sees in secret. Nobody's clapping for any of them. They know exactly who's watching. They expect the greater reward, God's reward, and it's the only reason the other three have held.

:::margin
Psalm 112:7 (NIV)
"They will have no fear of bad news; their hearts are steadfast, trusting in the LORD."
:::

Steadfast. That's the word this book is named for, and that's what it looks like. Not a man who never fails. A man whose heart is fixed to something that doesn't move, so that when the bad news comes, and it comes for every one of these men, he doesn't come apart. He reads the gauge, he says the true thing to God and a brother, and he puts his hands back on the wheel.

## These men are my brothers

I've called them brothers throughout. Here's what I mean by it.

I don't mean they're my friends, though they are. I mean that we're in something together. We've said the true thing to each other at tables and in trucks and on phones at 9 p.m. over dishes. We've asked each other what's heavy and waited for the answer. We've told each other "you are the man" when we needed to hear it and "that's what a man does" when we needed to hear that. We've stood at fence lines before dawn and put our hands on each other's sons. We've buried each other's wives and held each other's babies and called each other every Tuesday when there was nothing to say.

Solomon said it in one line.

:::margin
Proverbs 17:17 (NIV)
"A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for a time of adversity."
:::

Born for a time of adversity. A brother is the man who's there when it goes bad. Not the hunting buddy. Not the three hundred contacts. The one who picks up. Every man in this chapter has one now. Most of them didn't when I met them, and the difference between the man they were and the man they are is mostly that.

And here's what to understand about the company they're in. It's not closed. It's not a club for men who've got it together. Every one of them walked in as a man who didn't: Walt from a parking lot, Kenny from a ghost's apartment, Cole from thirty-three years of waiting, Roy from a recliner, Anthony from a doorway. They walked in because somebody asked them to, or because they finally asked somebody. The door was open. It still is.

:::margin
Hebrews 10:24 (NIV)
"And let us consider how we may spur one another on toward love and good deeds,"
:::

Spur one another on. That's a company of men. Not a crowd; a company. Two, three, a Thursday table. Men who know each other's exits and ask about them. Men who go first for each other. Men who are building something nobody claps for and who know that the men beside them see it even if no one else does.

These men are my brothers. And if you've read this far, if you've named the things, done the things, told a brother, and carried the lines, then whether we ever meet or not, you're one of them too. That's not a sentiment. That's what the four marks make. A man who lives them isn't alone in it. He's in a company that stretches back to a garden and forward to a table that hasn't been set yet, and every man in it is ordinary, and every man in it is steadfast, and every man in it got there on Tuesdays.

## The last time

I've said the sentence a lot in this book. Once more, then out of your way.

**A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.**

Four marks. Not four moods. Four things you can see on a man the way you could see the fence stretcher on my grandfather's palm. They're not a personality. They're not a feeling you find in a deer blind. They're the portrait of a Man in a garden on the worst night of His life, who got up off the ground and went out to meet what was coming and covered His men and finished the work, for the joy set before Him. Every time you live one of them on a Tuesday, you're wearing His face.

You're going to fail at them. So did every man in this chapter, this week. That's not the question. The question is whether you're in the first man or the second, and if you're in the second, whether you'll get up tomorrow and turn back toward the Father who does not leave and go stand in your garden again. That's the whole life. Get up. Turn. Stand. Repeat until He says "well done."

:::margin
1 Corinthians 15:58 (NIV)
"Therefore, my dear brothers and sisters, stand firm. Let nothing move you. Always give yourselves fully to the work of the Lord, because you know that your labor in the Lord is not in vain."
:::

Let nothing move you. Your labor is not in vain. Not the slab. Not the coffee. Not the Tuesday call. Not the pecans. None of it. He saw all of it and He's keeping the books and the reward is with Him, and it lasts.

## A quiet Tuesday

This book started on a quiet Tuesday, and it ends on one, because that's where a man's life is actually decided. Not at the altar, not at the fence line, not at the retirement ceremony, not in the last chapter of a book. On a gray day with a little wind out of the north when nobody's watching and nothing's at stake except everything.

Tomorrow's a Tuesday somewhere. There's a smoke detector chirping in a hallway near you. There's a woman who needs you to know her, a kid who needs a name and a hill, a father who needs a call, a brother who needs to be asked, a job that needs doing with all your heart, a drawer that needs opening, a Father who's watching the road for you. You know what a man does. You've known since chapter 3. There's nothing left to learn from me. There's only the getting up.

I'll leave you the way the old priests left the people, with the words God gave them to say over men who were about to walk back out into ordinary days.

:::margin
Numbers 6:24-26 (NIV)
"The LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face shine on you and be gracious to you; the LORD turn his face toward you and give you peace."
:::

The LORD bless you and keep you. Keep: the second verb of Genesis 2:15, the one you were told to do for the garden, said back over you by the One who does it for you.

Go work it. Go keep it. Reject passivity. Accept responsibility. Lead courageously. Expect the greater reward.

These men are my brothers. Come be one.
