# 22. Know Her

*Part 4: The house*

Roy can tell you the torque spec on the lug nuts of every vehicle he's ever owned. He can tell you the make and model of the car in every serious wreck he worked in thirty-one years on the highway. He knows the exact date he bought his first deer rifle and what he paid.

At sixty-seven, a few months into retirement, he sat across from his wife at breakfast on a Tuesday and realized he didn't know her favorite hymn.

Forty-four years. He knew her schedule. He knew she liked her coffee with too much cream and that she'd cry at the end of a certain kind of movie and that she didn't like his mother, though she'd never once said so. But sitting there with nothing to do for the first time in his adult life, he started asking himself what he actually knew about the woman across from him, and the honest answer scared him. He knew where she'd be. He didn't know who she was.

"I'd been standing next to her for forty-four years," he told me. "I thought that was the same as knowing her."

It's not. The first thing a man owes his wife, before he lays down his life for her and before he figures out what she needs, is to actually know who he's married to.

## Live with her according to knowledge

Peter wrote one sentence to husbands, and it's carried more freight than almost anything else in the New Testament about marriage.

:::margin
1 Peter 3:7 (NIV)
"Husbands, in the same way be considerate as you live with your wives, and treat them with respect as the weaker partner and as heirs with you of the gracious gift of life, so that nothing will hinder your prayers."
:::

Be considerate as you live with your wives. The older translations put it closer to the original: live with them according to knowledge. Dwell with her *knowing* her. Study her. Understand her. Make her a subject you're an expert in.

And look at the last clause, because men skip it. So that nothing will hinder your prayers. Peter's saying that a man who doesn't know his wife, who lives next to her without understanding her, has a problem with God. Not just with her. His prayers hit a ceiling. That's how seriously heaven takes this. The way you know your wife is connected to the way you're heard by your Father.

Here's what I've noticed: men study what they care about. A man who loves fishing knows the water temperature that turns the bass on. A man who loves his truck knows the sound it makes when the alternator's going. A man who loves his job knows things about it nobody else knows. We're built to learn the things we love, deeply, over years.

And most men stopped studying their wives around year two. They learned enough to get along, and then they stopped, and they've been running on that early data ever since, and she's changed completely since then, and they don't know it, and she knows they don't know it.

Proximity isn't knowledge. We said that about brothers in chapter 13. It's truer of wives. You can be in the same bed for forty years and not know the woman in it.

## What there is to know

Be specific. "Know your wife" is one of those lines men nod at and don't do anything with.

**Her story.** She had a chair at the end of her table too. How was she fathered? What did she learn about men from watching hers? What did her mother teach her about being a wife, on purpose and by accident? What happened to her before you that she's never fully told you? Most men know their wife's story the way they know the plot of a movie they half-watched. Ask her to tell it to you again, slowly, and listen like it's the first time. You'll learn why she flinches at the thing you thought was nothing.

**Her fears.** Not the ones you assume. Ask her what she's most afraid of right now, and then be quiet for a long time. Most wives, if given room, will tell their husbands something that surprises them. It's rarely money and it's rarely what he'd guess. It's often about being alone, or about the kids, or about becoming her mother, or about whether he actually sees her.

**What fills her and what drains her.** Some women come alive in a room full of people and some are wrung out by it. Some need an hour alone to be a person again and some need an hour of conversation. What restores your wife? What empties her? If you don't know, you'll keep planning weekends that exhaust her and wondering why she's short with you Sunday night.

**How she receives love.** Men tend to give love the way they'd want to get it, which is often by doing something: fixing, providing, handling. And some wives receive that as love and some don't feel loved by it at all. Some need words. Some need time with no agenda. Some need to be touched in a way that isn't about the bedroom. Some need you to notice and do the thing without being asked. Find out which she is. Ask her: "When have you felt most loved by me? What was I doing?" Then do more of that and less of what you assumed.

**Her body.** Straight: too many Christian men are squeamish about it and it costs their wives. A husband should know his wife's body and its seasons without embarrassment. Her cycle, and what it does to her energy and her mood and her patience, so he can carry more in the week she has less. Pregnancy, and what it did and is doing. Postpartum, which is a season all its own and I'll come back to it. The changes in her forties and fifties, which are real and hard and which most husbands sail past without a word because nobody ever told them a man should know about it. Illness. Age. She lives in that body every hour. If you don't know what's happening in it, you don't know her.

**Her walk with God.** How does she pray? What's she wrestling with Him about right now? What verse is she standing on? What's she struggling to believe? Most Christian couples I know have never had this conversation past the surface. A man who knows how his wife is doing with God can pray for her with precision instead of generalities.

**Her Tuesday.** What does her ordinary day actually look like, hour by hour? If she's home with kids, do you know what 2:00 in the afternoon feels like? If she works, do you know what her boss is like and what the hard part of her day is? Men who don't know their wives' Tuesdays make comments about them that land like insults, and can't understand why.

**Her people.** Her friends' names and what's going on in their lives. Her mother, and what that relationship costs her. Her sister. Knowing her people is knowing the weather system she lives in.

**Her dreams.** What did she give up to be where she is? What does she still want that she's stopped saying out loud because nobody asked? A woman who's been married fifteen years and is raising kids has usually set down some things she carried at twenty-two, and a husband who knows what they were, and honors them, and looks for ways to hand some back, is a husband she'll trust with anything.

:::margin
Proverbs 20:5 (NIV)
"The purposes of a person's heart are deep waters, but one who has insight draws them out."
:::

Deep waters. She's not going to hand you all of this on the surface. You have to draw it out. That takes insight, and insight takes time, and time takes deciding that she's worth studying.

## How a man learns his wife

**Ask, and then wait.** The waiting is the whole skill. Most men ask a question and start forming their answer to it before she's finished. Ask, and then count to ten in your head after she stops talking, because she usually hasn't stopped. The real thing comes after the pause.

:::margin
James 1:19 (NIV)
"My dear brothers and sisters, take note of this: Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry,"
:::

Quick to listen, slow to speak. James wasn't writing a marriage book, but he might as well have been.

**Listen without fixing.** When she tells you something hard, your whole body will want to solve it. Don't. Not first. Say, "That sounds heavy. Tell me more." A woman who gets fixed every time she speaks stops speaking. A woman who gets heard tells you everything.

**Watch.** What does she do when she's tired? What makes her laugh when she's not trying to? What does she reach for when she's sad? Where does she go in the house when she needs to be alone? Men are good at watching things they care about. Watch her the way you'd watch a piece of water you were trying to learn.

**Write it down.** I mean this literally. You keep maintenance records on the truck. Keep them on your marriage. Her sizes. Her friends' names and their kids' names. The thing she said she wanted in passing eight months ago. The date her father died. What she said her biggest fear was when you finally asked. A man who writes down what his wife tells him is a man who's decided it matters enough to keep. She'll notice when you remember. She'll notice more when you remember what she said only once.

**Ask her what you get wrong.** This is the bravest one. Once a season, ask: "What do I misunderstand about you? Where do I get you wrong?" And then don't argue. Take notes. You'll learn more in that conversation than in a year of assuming.

**Take the twenty minutes home.** The three questions from chapter 13 work across a kitchen table. What happened this week? What's heavy? What's next? Ask her every week, same night, and answer them yourself. Roy started doing this with his wife after the hymn morning, and he said it was like meeting her for the first time at sixty-seven.

## Knowing her under a newborn

Tomás, and every man with a baby in the house.

The woman you married is not the woman across from you right now, and neither of you signed up for that, and both of you are a little frightened by it. Her body's been through something you can't fully understand. Her hormones are rearranging her from the inside. She hasn't slept a full night in months. Her identity has shifted under her feet: she was a woman, and now she's a mother, and she's not sure where the woman went. She may be grieving the old self and feel guilty for grieving. She may be scared she's doing it wrong. She may be more tired than she has ever been in her life and unable to explain it in a way that sounds reasonable.

Knowing her in this season means knowing that she's carrying a load you can't see, and treating her like a woman under a load. It means asking "what do you need in the next hour?" instead of "what do you need?" because the second question is too big to answer at 3 a.m. and the first one isn't. It means noticing when she hasn't eaten, hasn't showered, hasn't been out of the house, hasn't laughed. It means knowing that her not wanting to be touched right now is about exhaustion and a body that's been someone else's property for a year, and not about you, and not permanent.

And it means watching for the thing nobody warns young husbands about. Some new mothers go into a darkness that's not just tiredness. It has a name, and it's common, and it's treatable, and the husband is usually the first one in a position to notice. If your wife isn't herself in a way that's lasted weeks, if she's not sleeping even when the baby does, if she says things about herself that frighten you, you take her to a doctor, and you go with her, and you don't let anyone including her tell you it's nothing. That's not overreacting. That's a man who knows his wife and keeps her.

Tomás told me the sentence that changed his first year was one his wife said at 4 a.m.: "I don't need you to fix it. I need you to know how hard this is." He said he'd been trying to fix it for four months. He stopped and started knowing, and something in the house came unclenched.

## Knowing her after forty years

Roy, and every man past sixty.

The danger for an older husband isn't that he doesn't know his wife. It's that he knows a version of her from 1985 and stopped updating. She's changed. Her body's changed, and she's living in a body that's aging in ways she may not have made peace with, and she needs a husband who knows that and still looks at her the way Adam looked at Eve. Her fears have changed: not the kids' safety anymore, but the grandkids', and her own health, and yours, and being left alone. Her griefs have accumulated: her parents, friends, maybe a child. Her dreams for the remaining years are specific and she's probably never said them out loud because for forty years the calendar belonged to your work.

Know the woman she is at sixty-eight. Ask her the questions you asked at twenty-four, and expect different answers. Ask what she wants to do with the years that are left, and listen like it's the most important intelligence you've ever gathered, because it is. Roy found out his wife had wanted to see the ocean since she was a girl and had never mentioned it because there was never a good time. They went that fall. "Forty-four years," he said, "and I didn't know that. What else don't I know?" That's the right question, and he's been asking it every Tuesday since.

## For the man who's single again

Kenny, and every man in your seat: you may be reading this with a stone in your chest, because you know now what you didn't know then. Maybe you never really studied her. Maybe you did, and it wasn't enough. Either way, two things.

First, if you're honest that you didn't know her, that's a piece of "I did this," and it belongs in your confession and your grief, not in a fight with yourself for the rest of your life.

Second, you still have to know her, differently. If you're raising kids across two houses, you have to know your kids' mother well enough to co-parent wisely. Not intimately. Civilly. What she's afraid of for the kids. What sets her off and what calms it down. How to say the hard thing about the schedule so it gets heard. That's a kind of knowing too, and it's for your children's sake, and it's a man's work.

And if God gives you another wife someday, you'll start knowing her on day one, with the notebook open, because you know now what it costs not to.

## For the single man

You're not studying a wife yet. Study women anyway, the right way: as sisters. Learn to ask a question and wait for the whole answer. Practice on your mother; she's been waiting your whole life for you to ask her something real. Practice on your sister. Learn what it's like to listen to a woman without fixing or flirting. A man who's learned to hear women as sisters will know how to hear a wife, and the man who hasn't will spend his first five years of marriage learning it the expensive way.

## How He knows His bride

Christ knows His church. Not generally. Specifically. Every fear, every wound, every hidden thing, the number of hairs on every head. He knows us completely and He's not put off by what He knows, and He keeps knowing us, patiently, over a lifetime, drawing out the deep waters.

:::margin
John 10:14 (NIV)
"I am the good shepherd; I know my sheep and my sheep know me"
:::

I know my sheep. That's the model for a husband. Not a man who married a woman once and has been guessing since. A man who knows her, and keeps knowing her, and lets her know that she's known.

Roy asked his wife her favorite hymn that Tuesday. She told him. Then she asked him why he wanted to know, and he told her the truth: that he'd realized he'd been standing next to her for forty-four years and had never really studied her, and he was sorry, and he'd like to start. She didn't say anything for a while. Then she got up and got the notebook she'd kept for thirty years with all her recipes in it, and she turned to the back, and she started telling him things.

He's still writing them down.

---

**Name it.** Be honest: how much of what you know about your wife is from the first two years? Say the one area from the list (her story, fears, body, walk with God, Tuesday, dreams) where you know the least.

**Do this week.** Ask her one question from that area and listen for the whole answer without fixing or defending. Then write down what she said. Start a notebook if you don't have one. If you're single, ask your mother or sister one real question and practice the same listening.

**Tell a brother.** Tell him what you found out that you didn't know. Ask him how long it's been since he studied his wife. Neither of you gets to say "I know her pretty well."

**Carry this.** Proximity isn't knowledge. You can stand next to a woman for forty-four years and not know her favorite hymn. Study her. Write it down.
