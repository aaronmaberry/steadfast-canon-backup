# 31. Feelings Are the Dash, Not the Wheel

*Part 5: The long road*

The old Ford with the cracked block had a temperature gauge, and it worked. That's the part of the story I left out in chapter 15. All summer, while I was replacing hoses and thermostats, the gauge was telling me the truth. Running hot. Running hot. Every time I put the hood down and told myself I'd fixed it, the needle would climb again on the next long grade, and I'd look at it and think the gauge must be off.

The gauge was the only honest thing on that truck.

But here's the other side. I've known men who've driven into a ditch staring at the dash. A gauge tells you something true about the state of the machine. It doesn't tell you where the road goes. A man who ignores his gauges blows an engine. A man who steers by them drives off the road. You need both hands on the wheel and one eye on the dash, and you need to know which one is which.

Your feelings are the dash, not the wheel. They're real, they're telling you something, and you should read them. And they don't get to steer.

## Two ways men wreck

Two kinds of men get this wrong, and most of us are one or the other, and some of us switch.

**The man who ignores the dash.** He'd tell you he doesn't do feelings. He's not angry; he's fine. He's not sad; he's tired. He's not lonely; he's busy. He was probably raised by a man who did the same, and he's proud of it, and he thinks it's strength. It isn't. It's a man driving with tape over his gauges. The feelings don't go away because he won't look at them; they go somewhere else. Chapter 9 told you where: out as anger, down as appetite, away as absence. Every one of the three exits is a gauge that got ignored until the engine did something about it. The man who says he doesn't have feelings has feelings running his life from under the hood where he can't see them.

**The man who steers by the dash.** He's the opposite and he's just as wrecked. "I don't feel in love anymore." "I don't feel like God's here." "I feel like this job isn't my calling." "I feel like I need to be happy, and I'm not happy here." He reads a gauge, which is fine, and then he turns the wheel toward whatever the gauge says, which is a ditch. He leaves marriages because a feeling changed. He leaves churches because the feeling left. He makes his whole life a series of course corrections toward whatever the needle's doing this week, and he ends up nowhere, and he can't understand why, because he was so honest about his feelings.

The Bible's way is neither. It's a man who reads every gauge honestly, says out loud what it says, brings it to God and a brother, and then steers by something that doesn't move.

## What the dash is telling you

A vocabulary, because a lot of men can't read their own dash for the simple reason that nobody taught them the names of the gauges.

Fear says something matters to you and you're not sure you can protect it. Read it. Then ask: what am I actually afraid of losing?

Anger says something's wrong, or you think it is. Read it. Then ask what's under it, because it's almost always fear or shame or helplessness wearing a hard face. Chapter 9.

Sadness says something's lost. Read it. Grieve the thing. Men skip this gauge more than any other, and the skipping is what turns sadness into the drift.

Loneliness says not-good-alone. It's the most accurate gauge on the dash, because God Himself said it in Genesis 2. Read it, and don't read it as "something's wrong with me." Read it as "go find a brother."

Desire says you're alive. It's not sin to want. What you do with the wanting is where sin comes in.

Numbness says you've been running hot so long the gauge burned out. That's not peace. That's a warning light that's stopped working, and it's the most dangerous reading on the dash.

Shame says "I am a wrong thing." Read it, and then check it against the plumb line, because this gauge lies more than any other. Chapter 15: guilt is a working gauge; shame is a broken one.

And joy says you're where you were made to be, doing what you were made to do. Read it, and thank God, and notice what you were doing when it came.

The Psalms are a book of men reading their gauges out loud to God. David, who was as hard a man as ever lived, said things to God that most men wouldn't say to their wives. Read this one and tell me he was ignoring the dash.

:::margin
Psalm 42:5 (NIV)
"Why, my soul, are you downcast? Why so disturbed within me? Put your hope in God, for I will yet praise him, my Savior and my God."
:::

Why are you downcast. He's reading the gauge. He's naming it. And then, in the same breath: put your hope in God. He's turning the wheel. Not toward the feeling. Toward God. That's the whole method in two sentences, and it's why the Psalms are the best book a man could read who's never learned to do this.

## What the dash is not telling you

Now the other half, for the man who steers by it.

**A feeling is not the truth about God.** "I don't feel forgiven" is a gauge reading. It's not a verdict. God's forgiveness is a legal fact established at a cross, and it doesn't fluctuate with your sleep. "I don't feel like God is here" is a reading too, and it's often accurate about you and never accurate about Him. He said He'd never leave. The gauge is describing your perception; the wheel is His word.

**A feeling is not the truth about your marriage.** "I don't feel in love anymore" is a reading. Every marriage that's lasted forty years has had stretches, sometimes long ones, where that gauge read empty. The men who steered by it are divorced. The men who kept the covenant with both hands and let the gauge do what gauges do woke up, often years later, with the needle back up and a marriage that had gone somewhere the feeling couldn't have taken them.

**A feeling is not a command.** "I feel like God wants me to" is the most dangerous sentence in a Christian man's mouth, because it takes a gauge and gives it God's authority. Test it. Hold it against the plumb line. Does the Word say it? Do your brothers see it? Does it look like the four marks or like an exit? God doesn't usually lead by feelings, and when He leads, what He leads toward looks like Scripture, not like relief.

:::margin
Proverbs 28:26 (NIV)
"Those who trust in themselves are fools, but those who walk in wisdom are kept safe."
:::

Trust in themselves. That's a man steering by his dash. Walk in wisdom. That's a man with his hands on a wheel that's fixed to something outside him.

**A feeling is not permanent.** Every gauge on your dash moves with sleep, food, blood sugar, hormones, weather, the last conversation you had, and what you watched last night. A man who makes a permanent decision based on a Tuesday-night reading has handed his life to the weather. Wait. Read it again Thursday. Read it again after sleep. Read it with a brother in the room.

## What the wheel is

So what does a man steer by, if not the dash? Four things, and you've met all of them already.

The Word. Chapter 17. It doesn't move. When your feelings say one thing and the Bible says another, the Bible is describing reality and your feelings are describing you.

The covenant. What you promised, before God, with witnesses. To her. To the church. To your kids. To the men you gave your word to. Promises are made precisely so that they'll hold when the feeling doesn't; otherwise you wouldn't need to make them.

The four marks. Reject passivity, accept responsibility, lead courageously, expect the greater reward. When you don't know what to do, do the one of those four that's in front of you. None of them requires a feeling.

The next assigned thing. Chapter 20. Not the thing you feel like. The thing God's put in your hands. Do it. The feeling will follow, or it won't, and either way you did the right thing.

Here's how the two work together. The truck again: You're driving up a long grade and the temp gauge climbs. You don't ignore it; you'll blow the engine. You don't steer toward it; there's nowhere to steer toward a gauge. You read it, you name it, "running hot," and then you make a wise decision with the wheel: you pull over, you let it cool, you check the coolant, you tell somebody, and then you get back on the road toward where you were already going. The gauge changed how you drove. It didn't change where.

That's a man reading his dash and keeping his wheel.

## Elijah under the broom bush

The heavy end of the dash, because men die of it and the church has been too quiet about it.

Some of you aren't just sad. You're somewhere darker than that, and you've been there a while, and you're not sure how to say it, and you're a little afraid of what saying it means about you. Here's a man of God who was there, and what God did with him.

Elijah had just seen the greatest victory of his life, fire from heaven on a mountain, and a day later he was alone in the desert asking to die.

:::margin
1 Kings 19:4 (NIV)
"while he himself went a day's journey into the desert. He came to a broom bush, sat down under it and prayed that he might die. 'I have had enough, LORD,' he said. 'Take my life; I am no better than my ancestors.'"
:::

I have had enough. Take my life. That's a prophet of God, one of the greatest who ever lived, reading his dash out loud, and the dash said "I want to die." He didn't hide it. He said it to God.

Now watch what God did. He didn't rebuke him. He didn't tell him to have more faith. He sent an angel with bread and water and let him sleep. Then He fed him again. Then, after the man had eaten and slept, God spoke, and then God gave him a job and a companion. Food. Sleep. Presence. A word. Work. A friend. In that order.

That's the most practical passage on depression in the Bible, and here's what it means for you.

Read the gauge and say it out loud. To God first, the way Elijah did. Then to a man. If the gauge says "I've had enough," that sentence needs to leave your mouth in front of another human being this week. Not because saying it fixes it. Because a man carrying that alone is a man in more danger than he knows.

Take care of the body. God's first two moves with a suicidal prophet were a meal and a nap. Sleep, food, sunlight, movement. These aren't spiritual shortcuts; they're the way God made you, and He knows how we're formed.

:::margin
Psalm 103:14 (NIV)
"for he knows how we are formed, he remembers that we are dust."
:::

He remembers that we are dust. Your body is not separate from your soul, and a man who hasn't slept in a month is going to read every gauge on his dash wrong. Fix what you can fix in the body first.

Get help, and hear me on this: getting help is not weakness. A doctor is not a failure of faith. A counselor is not a substitute for God; he's one of the ways God sends bread and water. If your gauges have been reading dark for more than a few weeks, if the numbness has settled in, if you're not sleeping when you could, if you've thought about ending it, you see a doctor and you tell him the truth. If he says medication, you take it, the way you'd take insulin. That's not steering by your feelings. That's repairing a broken gauge so you can read the dash again.

And if you're thinking about ending it, tonight, this week: call a man now. Your brother from the twenty minutes. Your pastor. If there's no one, call 988 and talk to someone whose whole job is to sit with you in the dark. Elijah asked God to kill him and God fed him breakfast. There's no shame in the broom bush. There's only the question of whether you'll let somebody find you there.

Then, when you've eaten and slept and been found: the wheel. Elijah got up and went back to work with a new companion beside him. The feeling didn't leave first. He moved first, small, with help, and the feeling followed over time. That's how it works for most men. Not a lightning bolt. A meal, a nap, a brother, and the next small assigned thing, repeated until the gauge comes back into range.

## In your season

If you've got a baby in the house, every gauge on your dash is pegged and none of them are accurate. You're sleep-deprived in a way that changes brain chemistry, and so is she. Do not make decisions about your marriage at 3 a.m. Do not evaluate your life at 3 a.m. Sleep is a spiritual discipline this year, and so is grace: for her, for yourself, for the gauges that are lying loudest exactly when you're most tempted to believe them. Say to each other, out loud, when it's bad: "This is the sleep talking. We're fine. We'll talk about it after a nap."

If you're divorced, your dash is going to do things for a year or two that you've never seen it do. Anger, grief, relief, guilt, loneliness, in rotation, sometimes all in one afternoon. Waves at odd times: the exchange in the parking lot, Sunday night in an empty house, a song. Read every one of them. Say them to God and to your man. And don't turn the wheel for a year. No moving. No marrying. No quitting the job. No leaving the church. Kenny's older man told him: "Your gauges are broken right now. You don't make turns with broken gauges. You drive straight and slow until they settle." He did. He said the second year the needle started making sense again, and he was glad of every turn he hadn't made.

If you're an older man, you may have been raised in a generation that taped over the dash and called it manhood. Roy said he didn't know he had feelings until he retired and there was nothing left to drown them out. "I sat on the porch and all of a sudden I was sad about things from 1975." That's fifty years of gauge readings coming due. It's not too late to learn to read them. The Psalms are the manual. And the older man has gauges the younger ones don't: grief that accumulates as friends die, fear of decline, the feeling of being useless. Read them out loud to a brother. Widowers especially: the loneliness gauge isn't lying to you, and it's not a sign you're weak. It's a sign God made you for company and you've lost some. Go find the rest.

If you're single, watch the loneliness gauge and read it right. It's not telling you something's wrong with you. It's telling you the truth Genesis 2:18 told: not good alone. Steer toward brothers, not toward whatever screen or woman promises to quiet the needle fastest.

## The Man who read every gauge

Jesus felt everything. He wept at a grave. He was troubled in spirit. He was overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death, and He said so. Hebrews says He prayed with loud cries and tears. There has never been a man who read his dash more honestly than the Son of God.

And He never once let a gauge turn the wheel. Not as I will, but as You will. He read the gauge that said "let this cup pass," out loud, three times, and then He steered by the Father's will straight into the thing the gauge was begging Him to avoid.

That's the model. Not a man with no feelings. Not a man run by them. A Man who felt all of it, said all of it, and kept both hands on a wheel that was fixed to something that didn't move.

Read your dash. Tell the truth about what it says, to God and to a brother. Then put your hands back on the wheel and drive where you were already going.

---

**Name it.** Which man are you: the one with tape over his gauges, or the one steering by them? Then read your dash right now, honestly, one word per gauge: fear, anger, sadness, loneliness, numbness, shame, joy. Say the words.

**Do this week.** Every morning, before the day starts, read one gauge out loud to God in one sentence, Psalm-style: "Father, I'm ___ today." Then say the wheel: "And I'm going to ___ anyway." If any gauge has read dark for weeks, make a doctor's appointment before Tuesday.

**Tell a brother.** Read him your dash. All of it. If there's a gauge you've never said out loud to anyone, that's the one. If you've been under the broom bush, tell him today, not this week.

**Carry this.** Feelings are the dash, not the wheel. Read every gauge. Steer by the Word.
