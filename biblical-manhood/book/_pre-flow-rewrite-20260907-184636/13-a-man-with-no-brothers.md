# 13. A Man With No Brothers

*Part 2: What you inherited, and what is yours*

The year Walt almost lost the business, he sat in his truck in the parking lot of a bank in Abilene with his phone in his hand and scrolled through every contact he had.

Three hundred and some names. Customers. Suppliers. His accountant. Guys he'd hunted with. Men from church whose kids had played ball with his kids. A brother-in-law in Fort Worth. His pastor. He scrolled all the way to the bottom and all the way back to the top and then he put the phone face down on the seat and sat there for forty minutes.

There wasn't one name on that phone he could call and say, "I'm scared. I think I'm about to lose everything and I don't know what to do." Not one. He'd built a business and a family and a reputation over thirty years, and he had three hundred contacts and no brothers.

He told me this a long time afterward, and he said it the way men say things like this: flat, like it was a fact about the weather. "I didn't have anybody to call." And then he said the sentence I hear more than any other from men who are honest. "I didn't think that was a problem until it was."

## Most men have no brothers

Say this straight, because the men it applies to almost never think it applies to them.

Most men have no brothers. They have buddies. They have men they do things with: hunt, fish, golf, watch the game, work on trucks. They have coworkers, some of whom they like. They have men at church they shake hands with and know the names of. And they have a wife, if they're married, who is carrying the entire weight of knowing them, which is a weight she was never meant to carry alone.

Ask a man who knows his home exit, the anger or the itch or the drift we talked about in chapter 9. Ask him who could call him at 2 a.m. and who he could call. Ask him who's allowed to say "you're wrong" to his face and be heard. Ask him who knows what he's actually afraid of. And watch how many men, good men, men with full contact lists, go quiet.

We were not built to do this alone. That's not a self-help line; it's the second chapter of Genesis. The first thing God said was wrong with the world was that the man was by himself. He fixed it with a wife, and that was the first fix, but it wasn't the only one. All through Scripture, men are set in companies. David had Jonathan and then thirty mighty men. Jesus had twelve, and three inside the twelve, and He took those three with Him into the garden the night He needed them most. Paul never traveled alone if he could help it and wrote most of his letters with a brother sitting next to him.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 4:9-10 (NIV)
"Two are better than one, because they have a good return for their labor: If either of them falls down, one can help the other up. But pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up."
:::

Pity anyone who falls and has no one. That was Walt in the truck. He'd fallen and he was reaching around in the dark and there was no hand.

## Why we don't have them

I've asked a lot of men why they have no brothers. Here's what they say.

*I'm fine.* This is pride wearing a work shirt. You're not fine. Nobody's fine. The man who says "I'm fine" for thirty years is the man who ends up in a bank parking lot with three hundred names.

*I don't have time.* You do; you just haven't been asked for the right amount. I'll get to that.

*I don't want to be a burden.* Under this is usually the empty chair. A boy who learned that needing things was dangerous grows into a man who'd rather go under than reach for a hand. I understand it. It's still killing you.

*Men don't do that.* Men did do that for most of history. Men in Scripture did it constantly. We stopped doing it about two generations ago and we've been dying quietly ever since, and calling it independence.

*I've been burned.* Some of you told a man something once and it got around, or he used it, or he drifted off. I'm sorry. It happens. It's also not a reason to never try again, any more than a bad meal is a reason to stop eating.

*I've got my wife.* Good. Keep her. But your wife is not your brother, and if she's the only one who knows you, you've put a weight on her that will eventually bend her. She needs you to have a man. Ask her. She'll tell you.

## What a brother is

Here's the word, so you know what you're looking for.

A brother is a man who knows your exit. He knows whether you get angry or reach for the screen or go quiet, and he asks about it, and you tell him the truth.

A brother is a man who can ask you anything. Money. Marriage. The bedroom. What you looked at this week. Whether you've prayed. Nothing's off the table because you've agreed nothing's off the table.

A brother is a man who'll come. At 2 a.m., to the hospital, to the parking lot of the bank. Not because he has to. Because he's your brother.

A brother is a man who tells you the truth. Not the nice version. The true one, said with love, said once, and then he lets you decide what to do with it.

:::margin
Proverbs 27:17 (NIV)
"As iron sharpens iron, so one person sharpens another."
:::

Sparks fly when iron sharpens iron. It's not a comfortable picture. A brother is going to say things that sting, and you're going to be sharper for it.

And a brother is a man who helps you find God when you can't. There's a line in Samuel that I think is the best one-sentence description of brotherhood in the Bible. David is on the run, hunted, exhausted, hiding in the woods, and his friend comes to find him.

:::margin
1 Samuel 23:16 (NIV)
"And Saul's son Jonathan went to David at Horesh and helped him find strength in God."
:::

Helped him find strength in God. Not gave him strength. Not fixed his situation. Jonathan couldn't fix anything; his own father was the one hunting David. He came anyway, into the woods, and he helped his brother find what only God could give. That's the job. That's what you need and that's what you're for.

Notice what a brother isn't. He's not a crowd. You don't need a men's group of forty. You need one man, maybe two. He's not a counselor, though he might tell you to see one. He's not your boss or someone who reports to you. And he's not necessarily the man you have the most fun with. Some of your best hunting buddies would be terrible brothers. That's alright. Keep hunting with them. Find a brother somewhere else.

## Twenty minutes

Here's the part I promised, and it's the most practical thing on this page.

The reason most men don't have a brother isn't that they don't want one. It's that every picture they've been given of brotherhood is too big. A weekly men's breakfast at 6 a.m. A small group with homework. A retreat. A weekend. Those things are good, and I've done all of them, and if you're a man with a newborn or a business going under or a custody schedule or a wife with cancer, you look at those and you know you can't, and so you do nothing.

Here's a smaller picture. It finally worked for me, and I've never seen a season of life it couldn't survive.

**Twenty minutes. One man. Once a week. Same time.**

On the phone is fine. In a truck is fine. Walking is fine. It doesn't have to be face to face and it doesn't have to be at a table. It has to be the same twenty minutes every week, because a time that floats is a time that dies. Tuesday at 7:30 in the evening, after the kids are down. Thursday at noon on the drive to lunch. Sunday afternoon on the porch. Pick it, and treat it like a client meeting you'd never cancel.

And here's what you do with the twenty minutes. Three questions and a prayer.

*What happened this week?* Two or three minutes each. Not a report. The two or three things that actually mattered: the fight, the win, the call from the doctor, the thing your son said.

*What's heavy?* This is the one that matters. What are you carrying? What's the fear under the week? Where's your exit showing up? You each answer, and you each tell the truth, and the other man does not fix it. He listens. He might ask one more question. He does not fix it.

*What's next?* One thing each. One move before next week. The conversation you're going to have, the thing you're going to stop, the call you're going to make. Say it out loud so a man heard you say it. Then next week, the first question is going to be "did you do it?"

Then two minutes of prayer. Out loud. For each other. It'll be awkward for about three weeks and then it'll be the best part.

That's it. Twenty minutes. Some weeks it goes thirty. Some weeks one of you is a wreck and you spend all twenty on his "what's heavy," and that's fine, that's what it's for. But the floor is twenty, once a week, same time, and the floor doesn't move.

Kenny's older man, the one who called him every Tuesday night the first year after his divorce, was doing exactly this. Three questions and a prayer. Kenny told me he didn't realize until much later that the man had a method. He just knew that every Tuesday at 7:30 his phone rang and a man wanted to know what was heavy, and for the first time in his adult life somebody did.

## Why twenty

Some of you are thinking twenty is too small to matter. It isn't.

Twenty minutes is small enough to survive anything. A newborn. Tax season. A twelve-hour shift. A custody weekend. Chemo. Retirement. Grief. I've never met a man who honestly couldn't find twenty minutes a week; I've met a lot who couldn't find two hours, and so found nothing.

Twenty minutes with a structure does more than two hours without one. Most men's gatherings drift toward sports and trucks and politics within eleven minutes because nobody's steering. Three questions steer. You'll go deeper in twenty minutes with the questions than most men go in a year of breakfasts without them.

And twenty minutes grows. Once two men have done this for six months, they start doing other things. They show up at each other's kids' games. They help with the move. They call on the off days. The twenty isn't the ceiling; it's the seed. But if you try to plant the whole tree at once, you'll plant nothing.

## How to find him

This is where most men stall, so be specific.

Look for a man who's living the four marks, mostly. Not perfectly. Nobody is. But you want a man who's off the tailgate, who carries weight, who goes first sometimes, and who seems to be working for something bigger than applause. You'll know him when you see him. He's probably already in your church or your crew or your neighborhood and you've never asked him anything real.

He can be your age or he can be older. Older is often better for this, and that's the next chapter. But another man in your own season who's serious about being a man is a fine brother, and sometimes the only one available.

He should not be your boss, your employee, or your brother-in-law, if you can help it. You need to be able to tell him the whole truth without a job or a Thanksgiving hanging on it.

Then ask. Plainly. Here's the sentence: "I'm trying to be a better man, and I've realized I don't have a brother. Would you be willing to talk with me for twenty minutes a week for the next three months and see how it goes?"

Give it a time-box. Three months. That makes it easy for him to say yes, and easy for either of you to let it go if it's not working. Most of the time it works and nobody remembers to stop.

Expect it to be awkward. The first three weeks you'll both feel like you're doing something strange. You are. You're doing something men used to do and stopped doing, and your body doesn't have the muscle memory yet. Push through the awkwardness. It goes away and what's on the other side is worth it.

And expect that he's been waiting too. Almost every man I've ever asked has said some version of "I've been wanting somebody to ask me that." Men are lonely. They're not going to go first. You're going to have to. That's chapter 6.

## In your season

If you're single, don't assume your roommates or your buddies are brothers. Proximity isn't brotherhood. You might have a brother already and just need to add the twenty minutes and the questions. Or you might have a house full of guys and no one who knows you. Find out this week.

If you've got a baby in the house, twenty minutes is all you've got, and that's exactly why it's twenty. Do it on the phone at 9 p.m. while you're doing the dishes. Your wife will bless you for it, because a man with a brother is a better husband, and she'll see it inside a month.

If you're divorced, your friends probably vanished. Couple friends do that; they were mostly her friends or they didn't know what to do with you. You need one man, more than any other man reading this does. Find him. If the church men's group feels too big and too married, skip it for now and get one man on the phone on Tuesday nights.

If you're older, the working years gave you a built-in company of men, and when the work stopped, so did they. Roy said the first year of retirement was the loneliest of his life, and he'd worked alone in a patrol car for thirty years. He built the Thursday breakfast table because he needed it, not because he was being generous, and it turned out a dozen other men needed it too. If you're the widower, and you've lost the couple friends along with your wife, the same is true. You need one man. Mr. Pruitt found his in a retired schoolteacher who also drank his coffee alone, and the two of them now meet every Wednesday morning at a gas station and have for four years.

## The garden

The night before He died, Jesus took three men into a garden. He knew they'd fall asleep. He knew one of them would deny Him before sunrise. He took them anyway, and He asked them for something.

:::margin
Mark 14:34 (NIV)
"'My soul is overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death,' he said to them. 'Stay here and keep watch.'"
:::

Stay here and keep watch. The Son of God, who needed nothing, asked three men to stay with Him in the worst hour of His life. If He didn't do it alone, you don't get to either.

Walt has a brother now. It took him a year to ask, and he asked a man from church he'd known for twenty years and never once had a real conversation with. They talk every Thursday at noon for twenty minutes on the drive to lunch. Walt told me the first month he thought it was the dumbest thing he'd ever agreed to. The sixth month, when the business hit another rough patch, he made a call from the same parking lot in Abilene, and this time somebody picked up.

Find a brother. Twenty minutes. Same time every week. Start with the sentence. Go first.

---

**Name it.** Say it out loud, honestly: "If I fell tonight, the man I could call is ___." If the blank is empty or it's your wife's name, you know what this chapter is for.

**Do this week.** Pick the man. Say the sentence to him before next Tuesday: "I'm trying to be a better man, and I don't have a brother. Would you talk with me twenty minutes a week for three months?" Set the day and time before you hang up.

**Find a brother.** This week the third line changes, because you may not have one yet. If you do, tell him about the twenty minutes and start. If you don't, the "do this week" is the whole job. Don't skip it because it's awkward. It's supposed to be.

**Carry this.** Twenty minutes. One man. Once a week. Same time. Three questions and a prayer: What happened? What's heavy? What's next?
