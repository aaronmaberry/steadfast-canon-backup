# 14. Ask the Man

*Part 2: What you inherited, and what is yours*

When I was twenty-three I asked a man named Buck how he'd stayed married for forty years. He was standing at the back of the church parking lot with his hands in his pockets, looking at nothing in particular the way old men do, and I don't know what made me ask. I just did.

He looked at me for a second like he was deciding something. Then he said, "Come by Tuesday. I'll show you."

I thought he was going to tell me. Sit me down at his kitchen table, pour some coffee, lay it out. He wasn't. When I showed up Tuesday he handed me a paintbrush and pointed at a fence, and we painted for three hours, and at some point during the third hour I realized his wife had brought us tea twice and he'd stopped both times to look at her while she walked back to the house. He never gave me a speech. But I went back the next Tuesday, and the one after that, and by the time the fence was done I'd learned more about marriage than I'd learned in twenty-three years of watching everybody else's.

There's a man about twenty years ahead of you on the road you're walking, and he knows things you need to know, and he's standing at the back of some parking lot with his hands in his pockets waiting for somebody to ask. Ask the man.

## The two men who are waiting

Here's the situation on both sides.

The younger man doesn't ask. He figures the older man is busy, or he'd be bothering him, or he doesn't know him well enough, or he should be able to figure this out himself, or his own father should've told him and since he didn't there's something shameful about asking anyone else. So he stands on his side of the parking lot and doesn't move.

The older man doesn't offer. He figures the younger man doesn't want an old guy's opinion, or he'd be presumptuous, or he doesn't have anything to offer because he made too many mistakes, or nobody asked him when he was young and he turned out alright, or he's not sure what he'd even say. So he stands on his side of the parking lot with his hands in his pockets.

And a whole generation of men grows up without anybody twenty years ahead telling them what's coming.

This isn't how it's supposed to work. Scripture's picture of manhood is a chain. Moses handed to Joshua. Elijah handed to Elisha. Naomi walked Ruth through a whole culture she didn't understand. Paul spent years on Timothy and Titus and then told them to go do the same.

:::margin
2 Timothy 2:2 (NIV)
"And the things you have heard me say in the presence of many witnesses entrust to reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others."
:::

Four generations in one sentence. Paul, to Timothy, to reliable people, to the others they'll teach. That's the chain. And it's broken in most churches and most families in this country, and the break is on both sides of the parking lot.

Last chapter was about brothers, men beside you. This one's about a man ahead of you. You need both. A brother knows what you're going through because he's going through it too. The man ahead knows what you're going through because he went through it and came out the other side, and he can tell you what's on the other side, which is a thing a brother can't do.

## What to ask for

Don't ask him to mentor you. "Mentor" is a big, formal, frightening word, and when you say it to a sixty-year-old man he hears a commitment he's not sure he can keep and a role he's not sure he's qualified for, and he'll probably say no or say yes and then dodge you. The word gets in the way.

Ask him something specific instead. That's what I did with Buck without knowing it. "How did you stay married forty years?" is a question a man can answer, or can say "come by Tuesday" to. Here are some others.

"How did you decide when to leave that job?"

"What do you wish you'd done differently with your kids when they were small?"

"How do you handle it when you and your wife aren't talking?"

"How did you get out of debt?"

"What did you do the first year after the divorce?"

"How did you keep going after she died?"

"Can I come watch you do that?"

One question. That's the ask. If it goes well, ask another one next week. If he says "come by Tuesday," go by Tuesday, and bring your hands. Some of the best things I've ever learned from older men I learned while we were both doing something else.

And if you want to give it a shape, use the twenty minutes from last chapter. "Could I call you for twenty minutes on Thursdays for the next couple months? I've got some things I'm trying to figure out and I think you've already figured them out." Older men can say yes to twenty minutes and a time-box. They struggle to say yes to "will you mentor me."

## How to pick him

You're looking for a man about twenty years ahead who's living the four marks. Not a famous man. Not the most impressive man at church. A man who's actually walked the stretch of road you're on and is still standing.

Here's how to tell. Watch his wife's face when he talks. A woman who's been married to a real man for thirty years looks at him a certain way, and a woman who's been married to a performer for thirty years looks at him a different way, and you can learn to tell the difference in about a minute. Listen to how his grown kids talk about him, if you can. Notice whether he tells the truth about his failures. Be careful of the man who only has wins. He's either lying or he hasn't been paying attention, and either way he can't help you, because what you need isn't his wins. It's his scars and his Tuesday habits.

He doesn't have to be in your line of work. He doesn't have to have the same personality. He does have to be a man you'd want to be like at his age, in the things that matter. And he has to be reachable. A man you could see face to face once a month, or call.

If you can't think of one, look harder. He's in your church. He's the deacon nobody asks anything of. He's the man three pews back who's been married forty-five years and shows up every week and nobody under fifty has ever had a real conversation with him. He's the retired guy at the hardware store. He's the older man at the Thursday breakfast sitting alone. There are more of them than you think and almost all of them are waiting.

## What to do once you've asked

Come with a question. Every time. An older man will give you the rest of his life if you come with a real question, and he'll drift off if you come to hang out. The question is the respect.

Listen more than you talk. You're there to learn what he knows, not to tell him what you know. Ask a follow-up. Let him finish.

Then do what he says. At least once. If he tells you to sit down with your wife and ask her a question, go do it, and come back and tell him what happened. That report is how you earn the next hour. Older men have wasted a lot of advice on young men who nodded and did nothing, and they've learned to stop giving it. Be the young man who does it and comes back. He'll give you everything he's got.

Don't make him your father. That's a weight he can't carry and it isn't fair to ask. He's a man ahead of you on the road. He'll give you a lot, but he can't give you what the empty chair didn't, and if you go looking for that from him you'll wear him out and end up disappointed. That's chapter 10's job.

And say thank you, out loud, specifically. "What you told me about how you handled your dad, I did that, and it changed something." Older men don't hear that enough. Some have never heard it.

## To the man who's ahead

Half of you reading this are the man in the parking lot with his hands in his pockets.

You're the man. I don't care that you don't feel finished. Nobody finishes. Paul was writing about his own unfinished race until the week he died. You've walked a stretch of road that a younger man is just starting, and you know where the washouts are, and that knowledge is worth more to him than you can imagine. He doesn't need you to have gotten it all right. He needs you to tell him the truth about where you got it wrong, and what you'd do differently, and what you did on Tuesdays that held.

:::margin
Titus 2:2 (NIV)
"Teach the older men to be temperate, worthy of respect, self-controlled, and sound in faith, in love and in endurance."
:::

:::margin
Titus 2:6-8 (NIV)
"Similarly, encourage the young men to be self-controlled. In everything set them an example by doing what is good. In your teaching show integrity, seriousness and soundness of speech that cannot be condemned."
:::

Paul's letter to Titus is largely a set of instructions for older men and older women to hand off to younger ones. That's the plan. Not a program. Older men teaching younger men by example and by word, in the ordinary, on Tuesdays.

How to start.

Go first. He's not going to ask. He thinks you're busy and he thinks he'd be bothering you and he's probably a little scared of you. So you walk across the parking lot. "I've watched you handle a few things around here and I think you're serious about being a man. Would you want to get breakfast sometime?" Or "I'm fixing a fence Saturday and could use a hand, if you want to learn how." Give him something to say yes to.

Don't lecture. The temptation for a man with forty years of experience is to download it. Don't. Ask questions. "What's going on with work?" "How's it going with her?" "What's heavy right now?" Young men will tell an older man things they'd never tell a peer if the older man asks and then actually waits for the answer.

Tell the truth about your failures. This is the thing that makes it work. When a young man hears a man he respects say "I nearly lost my marriage at thirty-five and here's what I did wrong," something unlocks. He stops performing. He starts asking real questions. Your scars are the curriculum.

Give him work. Invite him into something you're doing. A job. A project. The Thursday table. Men learn beside each other more than across from each other. Buck taught me marriage with a paintbrush.

Keep it small and steady. Twenty minutes a week, or a breakfast a month, or a job every other Saturday. Something he can count on. The steadiness is the teaching. A young man who's never had a reliable man in his life learns more from you showing up eleven Tuesdays in a row than from anything you say on any one of them.

And expect to get more than you give. Every older man I know who's done this says the same thing. Roy told me that the two men at his Thursday table gave him back a reason to get up in the morning that he'd lost when he turned in the badge. "I thought I was helping them," he said. "I was, some. Mostly they were saving my life." That's not a side effect. That's the design. God sets the lonely in families, and He does it in both directions.

:::margin
Psalm 71:18 (NIV)
"Even when I am old and gray, do not forsake me, my God, till I declare your power to the next generation, your mighty acts to all who are to come."
:::

Till I declare your power to the next generation. That's a prayer for a job. Not for rest. For a job that lasts until the end. If you've turned in your badge or your keys or your hard hat and you're wondering what a man does with his mornings now, this is what. The younger men are in the parking lot. Walk over.

## In your season

If you're single, find a married man ahead of you and ask him about marriage before you're in one. You'll learn more in a year of asking than in your first three years of doing, and your future wife will thank you for it without ever knowing why.

If you've got a baby in the house, find a man whose kids are grown and ask him what he wishes he'd done when they were small. He'll tell you it goes fast and to be there. He's right. Get that from him now while there's still time to do something about it.

If you're divorced, you need a man ahead of you more than most, and you may be tempted to skip this because you're embarrassed. Don't. Find a man who's been through it, if you can. If you can't, find a man who's been married a long time and isn't going to judge you, and ask him to walk with you anyway. Kenny's Tuesday-night man had never been divorced. He'd just decided that Kenny was worth a phone call every week, and that turned out to be enough.

If you're the older man, and your own father never did this for you, and no man ever did, you can still do it. You're not disqualified by what you didn't get. You're equipped by what you lived through. Go find him.

## Come by Tuesday

Buck's been gone a while now. I painted his fence a few more times over the years, and sat at his table, and asked him a few hundred more questions, and he answered most of them by handing me a tool and starting to work. When I got married he stood at the back of the church with his hands in his pockets looking at nothing in particular, and afterward he shook my hand and said, "Well. You paid attention."

I did. Because I asked. Because he said come by Tuesday. Because neither one of us stayed on our side of the parking lot.

Ask the man. Be the man. The chain's been broken long enough.
