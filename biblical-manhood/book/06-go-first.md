# 6. Go First

Sometime after midnight the wind took down an old oak. By morning its roots were in the air and its crown was lying across the street in a yard that wasn't its own. The power was out. Up and down the block, people stood at their windows and looked at the tree, and then at the other windows, and did the same quiet math.

The math came out to nobody. Everybody on that street cared. Nobody moved. Caring and moving had come apart. In every house the same three thoughts ran. Somebody needs to deal with that tree. Whoever does it should know how to cut it. And that somebody is probably someone else.

Then one door opened. A man came out in the clothes he'd slept in, with a coat thrown over them, gloves on, and a chainsaw in his hand. He didn't stand on his lawn and think it over. He walked to the trunk and started looking for the place to make the first cut. For a while he had the whole street to himself.

Then a second door opened. Within fifteen minutes there were six men, a second saw, a truck backed up to the curb, and coffee going on a camp stove. What the block had been waiting on was a door.

I've been the face in the window. I've decided the saw belonged to some more qualified man and left the trunk lying in the road. The third mark says a real man leads courageously. When men hear the word leadership, they picture the head of a table, a title, a voice that fills a room. Picture the first open door instead. One man moves while everyone else is still looking. He takes the exposed stretch so the next man has somewhere to go.

## Watch me

Jesus spent three years with His twelve, and the lesson on leading that He made hardest to miss involved a towel around His waist. Washing feet was the lowest job in the house. Nobody in that upper room was volunteering. Every man was hoping the silence would pass him by. Jesus got up, filled the basin, and knelt.

:::margin
John 13:14-15 (NIV)
"Now that I, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also should wash one another's feet. I have set you an example that you should do as I have done for you."
:::

He gave them an example, in the flesh. A lecture on serving would have been easier. He took the job nobody wanted before any of them did, and what He said afterward was simple. Now you.

Gideon's version fit in one order. He was on a hill above an enemy camp, in the dark, with three hundred men.

:::margin
Judges 7:17 (NIV)
"'Watch me,' he told them. 'Follow my lead. When I get to the edge of the camp, do exactly as I do.'"
:::

Watch me. Those two words do the leading, and they also explain why so few men volunteer to say them. A man behind a desk or a podium has cover. The man out front has none. He feels the cold water before anyone else does. If he makes the wrong cut, everybody sees it. If nobody follows, he's standing alone in the road with his plan. He goes out with the order and without a guarantee. That's the job.

## Where first happens

Your street may never lose an oak. The places where a man has to go first are usually smaller.

The apology. Make it clean, and make it early. I'm sorry I raised my voice. Stop there. The moment you tack on a second clause, the one about how she gets, the apology turns into a closing argument. I've tacked it on and called the result peace. She hears the argument coming before you finish the sentence.

Prayer at the table. In a lot of houses the food lands and a silence follows that everyone understands. Every eye goes to one chair. Plenty of men, me included, have prayed out loud for strangers and then gone quiet in front of their own last name.

Naming the rubble. Nehemiah went out at night with a few men and looked over what was left of Jerusalem's wall before he told anyone why he'd come. Then he stood up and said out loud what everyone could see and nobody had said.

:::margin
Nehemiah 2:17 (NIV)
"Then I said to them, 'You see the trouble we are in: Jerusalem lies in ruins, and its gates have been burned with fire. Come, let us rebuild the wall of Jerusalem, and we will no longer be in disgrace.'"
:::

We haven't been to church since spring. We're behind on the truck payment. Our son is drinking, and this whole house has agreed not to notice. Once the rubble has a name, you can build. Leave it unnamed and it becomes the view.

Turning around. A cold spell in a marriage is two people standing still, each waiting for the other to take the first step. Take it. She may carry more of the blame. You're still the man. A kitchen looks small until a husband tries to cross it. I've stood on my side of one and let the distance win.

Direction, given by going. Nehemiah didn't lead from the porch. "Come," he said, and his feet went to the wall with the word. Saying "come" and then moving is what leading is. On a Sunday morning when a family has decided to go to church, it looks like a man sitting in a running car while everyone else is still looking for their shoes.

Every season has its own version of the door, and the requirement doesn't change. A single man is the one who says Sunday is real and then offers the ride. A new father prays over the crib, out loud, where he can hear his own voice, and he does it again the next night. A divorced man gives his children's mother the straightest apology he's ever managed, and he leaves the outcome with her. A silent teenager gets one question on a drive. An older man crosses the church lobby toward a younger one, because the younger man is never going to start that walk.

## What we're afraid of

Four fears keep a man's hand off the door, and I've given in to all four.

We're afraid of looking foolish. The prayer comes out clumsy. The apology sounds rehearsed. He gets to the tree and can't find the cut.

We're afraid of being refused. He turns around in the kitchen and she stays where she is. He says, "Son, I've noticed something," and gets a shrug and a closed door.

We're afraid of being wrong. So he picks no direction at all and calls it humility.

And we're afraid of the cost, because the cost is real. The first man on the street gives up his morning. The first apology gives up an argument he could have won. The man who goes first is the one who pays.

Scripture is honest about fear. It just never tells a man to wait until the fear leaves before he moves.

:::margin
2 Timothy 1:7 (NIV)
"For the Spirit God gave us does not make us timid, but gives us power, love and self-discipline."
:::

A man who's afraid and stays planted is timid. A man who's afraid and moves anyway is courageous. The man walking toward that oak with his heart pounding is what courage looks like, and the pounding is what it feels like from the inside.

## You're never actually first

The street looks different once you learn who went out ahead of you. Moses was handing the nation over to Joshua. A river lay in front of them, and walled cities waited on the other side. Moses told Joshua to be strong and courageous, and then he told him why he could be.

:::margin
Deuteronomy 31:8 (NIV)
"The LORD himself goes before you and will be with you; he will never leave you nor forsake you. Do not be afraid; do not be discouraged."
:::

Goes before you. Your door opens onto a road He's already walking. The hard thing you have to say at your table will land in a room He's already standing in. Your real place in line is second. The cost is still real, and so is the exposure. But He's already out in the street, and He isn't leaving.

## How to go first

Bring a first line. Men stay behind the door by trying to script the whole conversation in advance, and I know that stall well. Here are three openers. Pick one and stop. I've been wrong about something. Let's pray. Son, I've noticed something. Once that first line is out, the people in the room will carry a lot of the rest.

Practice where the room is small. A man who wants to lead at church but can't speak up at his own supper table has the sizes backward. Start in the truck, in the kitchen, in a text thread with three friends. The big rooms are just those small rooms added together.

Lead with questions. Ask your wife what this year of being married to you has actually been like. Ask what she's been worried about and hasn't said. A question opens a door, and you keep your hand on it while she walks through. If there's no wife yet, the questions still work with a friend. What's it been like being my friend this year? What have you been carrying that I haven't asked about?

Expect about eight seconds. You open your door, and then there's a gap before anyone else opens theirs. Standing alone in that gap is normal. Most men retreat at about four seconds. The gap is simply what going first feels like. Stay in it.

Pick an ordinary day. Oaks don't fall very often. Supper is tonight.

## The pioneer

:::margin
Hebrews 12:2 (NIV)
"fixing our eyes on Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith. For the joy set before him he endured the cross, scorning its shame, and sat down at the right hand of the throne of God."
:::

A pioneer is the man who goes into the hardest stretch ahead of everyone else and leaves a path behind him. Eight seconds alone would have been a mercy for Jesus. His friends fell asleep, and then they ran, and His gap stretched from the garden to the cross. The writer of Hebrews tells us how He kept going. Joy was set in front of Him, and He moved with that joy in view. The reward is why He went first.

The third mark rests on the fourth. Nerve runs out, usually by the time the second door opens. Confidence in the reward doesn't run out. You move first because the One who went ahead of you can be trusted to make the cost worth it.

## Before you turn the page

You don't have to be the bravest man on the block. Open the door with your heart still pounding. Even that puts too much on you, because the street already has Him in it. The tree has been lying in the road long enough. May He give you the first line, hold you steady in the gap after it, and let you hear the next door open.
