# 3. The Four Marks

If the maps don't hold, you've got a harder question than the one you started with. If that isn't what a man is, then what is?

I want to answer that here, in one sentence you can carry in your pocket. Then we take the sentence apart, one mark at a time, so you can see how each piece looks on a man in the middle of an ordinary week. Before the sentence, a picture, because the picture is what keeps the sentence from becoming one more thing on the shelf.

## What a mark is

Walk into a shop where a man has worked for twenty years and you can read him without asking a question. The workbench has grooves from the same cuts, repeated until the wood itself remembers the job. There's an oil stain where the coffee cup always sat. The wrench hooks hang empty in the order his hand reaches for them. Nobody got those grooves from wanting to be a craftsman, or from a weekend about tools. He got them from the work, on ten thousand days he couldn't have told apart.

That is what a mark is. A mark isn't a feeling you have about yourself, and it isn't a title somebody gives you. A mark is what gets left on you by what you've done over and over again for a long time. Feeling wild at dawn doesn't leave a groove on a life. What you do when you get home does.

So here is the sentence. I didn't invent it. Men have been handing it to one another for years because it holds. I've held it up against my own life for a long time, and against the lives of men in every season I can name, and I have never found a place where it lies.

**A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.**

Read it again, slower. It names four marks, not four moods and not four qualities you either have or don't, but four things a man does, which over time leave four things you can see on him the way you can see the grooves on that workbench.

The four marks are how that mind looks on a man, in a house, on a crew, in an ordinary week.

## He rejects passivity

Every town has a man on a tailgate. He is not a bad man. He has opinions about the weather and the school board and the way the city runs the water department, and he'll share all of them while the work he came to do waits behind him. He's waiting for the right time, or the right price, or the right feeling, and he has been waiting so long that the waiting has started to look like wisdom.

Passivity is the default setting of a man. It almost never looks like laziness. It looks like patience. It looks like keeping the peace. It sounds like "I didn't want to overstep," and the man saying it usually believes he's being humble. The first man in the Bible was passive in exactly this way. When the serpent came into the garden and started twisting the one command God had given, Genesis says the man was with her. He didn't grab the fruit and he didn't argue for it. He stood there, and his standing there was enough to break the world.

Rejecting passivity doesn't mean becoming loud, or pushy, or the kind of man who mistakes volume for strength. It means that when something needs doing and you know it and you're the one standing there, you get off the tailgate. You bring up the hard conversation instead of hoping it gets brought up. You don't require a feeling in order to start. **Stop Waiting** is where we look at this closely.

## He accepts responsibility

The second mark is weight. I helped a friend move a gun safe one August, four hundred pounds of steel down a narrow hallway in heat that made the air thick. There is a moment with a load like that when you're committed. You've lifted it, your friend has lifted it, and the only way through is through. You can't set it down in the hallway. You can't decide halfway along that this isn't really your safe. You carry it to where it's going, then you set it down.

That's what responsibility feels like on a man. A boy waits for responsibility to be assigned to him, then argues about whether the assignment was fair. A man looks at what's in front of him and says "that's mine," even when it isn't his fault and even when nobody would blame him for walking past. The bill nobody wants to open is his. The kid nobody's talking to is his. The apology nobody's making is his. Lamentations says it is good for a man to bear the yoke while he is young: good, not merely tolerable. The weight shapes him. **Pick Up the Weight** shows how to take it without being crushed by it.

## He leads courageously

Somewhere there's a cold stock tank and a group of boys on the bank. The water is colder than any of them expected, and everybody is waiting for somebody to go first. That is what leadership is. It isn't the title or the corner office. It's being the one who goes first into the cold water so that the others can follow.

Somebody has to go first, in every house and every crew and every church. Somebody has to be the first one to say "I was wrong" in a marriage that's been quiet for three days. Somebody has to be the first one to pray out loud at a table where nobody does, the first one to call the brother who's been drifting, the first one to say the true thing in the meeting where everybody's nodding. Courage is not the absence of fear. If you weren't afraid, it wouldn't take courage to move. Courage is what happens when a man is afraid and puts his boots on anyway and walks. When God told Joshua to be strong and courageous, He didn't tell him to stop being afraid. He told him what to do while he was afraid, and why he could do it. **Go First** is about that.

## He expects the greater reward

The last mark is the one that separates a good man from a godly one, and it's the one most men skip, so I've put it last on purpose.

Pecan trees don't hurry. If you plant one this fall, you'll water it for years before it gives you a single nut worth cracking. Every man who has ever planted a pecan orchard was planting for somebody else. You don't plant pecans because you intend to eat pecans. You plant them because you believe in a harvest you won't hold in your own hands, and you're willing to spend your strength on it anyway.

That's the fourth mark. A real man expects the greater reward, God's reward, and that expectation changes what he does with ordinary days more than anything else in this book. He can do the right thing when nobody's looking. He can stay married through a hard decade. He can give money that won't come back and time that won't be thanked, because he believes a Father sees what's done in secret. Hebrews says anyone who comes to God must believe that He exists and that He rewards those who earnestly seek Him. He rewards, and God isn't embarrassed by that word. The man who doesn't expect a reward from God will go looking for one somewhere else: applause, money, women, a bigger truck, a boy's approval, his own father's approval. **Live for a Reward That Lasts** is about that, and I've come to believe it's what makes the other three possible.

## Why four, and why these

Each of these four marks answers a specific way that men break. Passivity is how a man breaks by doing nothing. Blame and evasion are how he breaks by refusing weight. Cowardice, which almost always travels under a nicer name, is how he breaks by hanging back. Living for the wrong reward is how he breaks slowly, over forty years, while looking successful. I have never met a man in trouble whose trouble didn't trace back to one of those four.

Notice the shape. The first three are things a good man of any faith or none might do. The fourth is what makes the other three Christian. It's the reason a man can keep doing the first three when they cost him and nobody claps. Take the fourth out and you have a self-improvement list. Leave it in and you have a way of life that will outlast you.

Same four marks in every season. The garden changes. The marks don't. A young man gets serious now. A young father turns toward his exhausted wife inside the chaos. A man in midlife notices he's drifted toward the place that claps for him and comes home. An older man gets up with no badge to put on and finds the work anyway.

## Before you turn the page

You've got the sentence now. Don't try to fix all four this week. Find the mark that's faintest on you, and hold that one for a while. The men I've watched change didn't change all at once. They worked one mark on one ordinary day and then did it again. May the Father who made you in His image show you the mark He wants to press into you first, and may He be patient with you while it takes, the way He's been patient with me.
