CHAPTER 35



These Men Are My Brothers
   Part 5: The long road
   It's a Tuesday as I write this. There's nothing special about it: a gray sky, a little
wind out of the north, the kind of day nobody remembers. I've been thinking about
the men in this book and where they are today, on this particular unremarkable
Tuesday, and I want to tell you before I let you go.
   Not because they're impressive. None of them is famous, and none of them will
be. If you passed any of them at a gas station you wouldn't look twice. I want to tell
you because they're the argument of the whole book, and the argument is this: the
four marks are not for exceptional men. They're for men, ordinary ones, in every
season, on Tuesdays, and I've watched them hold.

Where they are

   Roy is seventy-two now. He got up at 5:30 this morning and made the coffee
and sat on the porch with his wife while she drank it, and she told him about a
dream she'd had, and he listened to the whole thing and asked a question at the end.
Then he read Proverbs 12 out loud. Then he drove to the church to look at a leak in
the fellowship hall roof that nobody had asked him to look at. On Thursday he'll sit
at a table with two men in their thirties and ask them what's heavy, and they'll tell
him. His grandson has a plumb bob in his pocket at school right now. His daughter
calls on Sundays. He turned in a badge five years ago and thought his work was
over, and he'll tell you, if you ask, that the last five years have been the most
important of his life, and that nobody has clapped once.
  Tomás's daughter is two. He walked her around the block at six this evening the
way he has since she was three months old, and she talked the whole way. His wife
got five hours of sleep in a row last night because he took the first shift, and she's a

let him hang up until he'd answered. Now the divorced men at his church talk to
Kenny, because he's the only one they'll let in. He didn't choose that. He's serving
God's purpose in his generation with the thing he thought disqualified him.
   Micah is married now, and he and his wife had a younger couple over for dinner
tonight, and he cooked. He still meets Dale every other Tuesday for breakfast, and
he's started meeting a twenty-four-year-old on Thursdays who reminds him of
himself at the back table. He wrote a date inside his Bible a few years ago, the day a
man told him he was a man and to stop waiting for permission. He looks at it
sometimes.
   Walt sold off part of the business. He was home for dinner tonight, as he is four
nights out of five, and he and his wife are planning a trip for the fall that she picked.
He has a brother now; they talk Thursdays at noon on the drive to lunch. He told
me the empty nest turned out to be the best stretch of his marriage, because for the
first time since 1994 it was just the two of them and he actually showed up for it.
He still thinks about the bank parking lot in Abilene. He calls it the day he found
out he had three hundred contacts and nobody.
    Anthony walked into his kitchen tonight. He didn't stand in the doorway. His
daughter looked up from her homework to see what his face would do when her
mother came in from the car, and it did the right thing, and the girl smiled and went
back to her fractions. He lost a fight he was winning last week. He's been working
on that. He says the word "Father" in the truck every morning, and it still feels a
little foolish, and he does it anyway.

   Mr. Pruitt's pecan trees are a little past knee-high. He drank coffee with Ed at the

about the price of cattle and Ed's grandson and nothing in particular, and it was the
most important twenty minutes of Mr. Pruitt's week. He's seventy-six, and he won't
eat one of those pecans. He's the most settled man in the county.
  Mr. Alvarez bathed his wife this morning, and she didn't know his name, and he
knew hers. Then he called a widower from church and asked him what was heavy.
He's seventy-eight, and he said "in sickness" in 1968 and he meant the whole thing.
  The man at the wastewater plant is on shift right now, as I write, running his
checks and praying over four thousand sleeping people who don't know he exists.
   And the young man with four jobs in three years stayed at the fifth. He runs a
crew now. Two of the men on it started coming to church because of how he treats
them. He still doesn't have a calling. He has a purpose, and it turned out to be
wherever he stopped running.

What they have in common

   Look at that list. A retired trooper, a pipeliner, an HVAC man, a help desk tech,
a business owner, a man who used to stand in doorways, a mechanic, a farmer, a
widower, a caregiver, a plant operator, a crew chief. Twenty-four to seventy-eight.
Single, married, divorced, widowed, newborn in the house, empty nest, retired, sick.
Every season in the book.
   Not one of them is remarkable, and that's the point. I didn't choose them



These Men Are My Brothers                                                           341
because they were the best men I know. I chose them because they're the men I
know, and because every one of them, in his own season, on his own Tuesdays, is
doing the four things.
   Every one of them got off the tailgate. Roy went and found the work. Tomás
turned toward his wife inside the chaos instead of waiting for it to calm down.
Kenny made the call before the anger cooled. Every one of them rejected passivity.
   Every one of them picked up weight that was theirs, and some that wasn't. Walt
sold trucks to be home. Cole reached toward a father who never reached back. Mr.
Alvarez picks up a washcloth every morning. Every one of them accepted
responsibility.
   Every one of them went first. Anthony into the apology. Micah into the sentence
at a young man's table. Hollis across a parking lot to a man by a truck. Roy into his
daughter's driveway. Every one of them led courageously.
   And every one of them is doing it for a Father who sees in secret. Nobody's
clapping for any of them. They know exactly who's watching. They expect the
greater reward, God's reward, and it's the only reason the other three have held.

  Psalm 112:7 (NIV)
  They will have no fear of bad news; their hearts are steadfast, trusting in the LORD.



   Steadfast is the word this book is named for, and that's what it looks like. Not a
man who never fails, but a man whose heart is fixed to something that doesn't
move, so that when the bad news comes, and it comes for every one of these men,
he doesn't come apart. He reads the gauge, he says the true thing to God and a
brother, and he puts his hands back on the wheel.

These men are my brothers

  I've called them brothers throughout, and I want to say what I mean by it at the
end, because it's the last thing I want to leave with you.
   I don't mean they're my friends, though they are. I mean that we're in something
together. We've said the true thing to each other at tables and in trucks and on
phones at nine at night over dishes. We've asked each other what's heavy and waited
for the answer. We've told each other "you are the man" when we needed to hear it

and "that's what a man does" when we needed to hear that. We've stood at fence

  Proverbs 17:17 (NIV)
  A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for a time of adversity.



   A brother is born for a time of adversity. He's the man who's there when it goes
bad. Not the hunting buddy, and not the three hundred contacts. The one who
picks up. Every man in this chapter has one now. Most of them didn't when I met
them, and the difference between the man they were and the man they are is mostly
that.
   And here is what I want you to understand about the company they're in: it isn't
closed, and it isn't a club for men who've got it together. Every one of them walked
in as a man who didn't: Walt from a parking lot, Kenny from a ghost's apartment,
Cole from thirty-three years of waiting, Roy from a recliner, Anthony from a
doorway. They walked in because somebody asked them to, or because they finally
asked somebody. The door was open, and it still is.

  Hebrews 10:24 (NIV)
  And let us consider how we may spur one another on toward love and good deeds,



   Spur one another on. That's a company of men. Not a crowd; a company. Two
or three, a Thursday table. Men who know each other's exits and ask about them.
Men who go first for each other. Men who are building something nobody claps for
and who know that the men beside them see it even when no one else does.
   These men are my brothers. And if you've read this far, if you've named the
things and done the things and told a brother and carried the lines, then whether we
ever meet or not, you're one of them too. That isn't a sentiment. It's what the four
marks make. A man who lives them isn't alone in it. He's in a company that
stretches back to a garden and forward to a table that hasn't been set yet, and every
man in it is ordinary, and every man in it is steadfast, and every man in it got there
on Tuesdays.

The last time

  I've said the sentence a great many times in this book. I'm going to say it once
more and then get out of your way.
  A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and
expects the greater reward, God's reward.
   Four marks, not four moods. Four things you can see on a man the way you
could see the fence stretcher on my grandfather's palm. They aren't a personality,
and they aren't a feeling you find in a deer blind. They're the portrait of a Man in a
garden on the worst night of His life, who got up off the ground and went out to
meet what was coming and covered His men and finished the work, for the joy set
before Him. Every time you live one of them on a Tuesday, you're wearing His face.
    You're going to fail at them. So did every man in this chapter, this week. That
isn't the question. The question is whether you're in the first man or the second,
and if you're in the second, whether you'll get up tomorrow and turn back toward
the Father who does not leave and go stand in your garden again. That's the whole
life: you get up, you turn, you stand, and you repeat it until He says "well done."

  1 Corinthians 15:58 (NIV)
  Therefore, my dear brothers and sisters, stand firm. Let nothing move you. Always give yourselves
  fully to the work of the Lord, because you know that your labor in the Lord is not in vain.



   Let nothing move you. Your labor is not in vain. Not the slab, not the coffee, not
the Tuesday call, not the pecans, none of it. He saw all of it, and He's keeping the
books, and the reward is with Him, and it lasts.

A quiet Tuesday

   This book started on a quiet Tuesday, and it ends on one, because that's where a
man's life is actually decided. Not at the altar, not at the fence line, not at the
retirement ceremony, not in the last chapter of a book. On a gray day with a little
wind out of the north, when nobody's watching and nothing's at stake except
everything.
  Tomorrow is a Tuesday somewhere. There's a smoke detector chirping in a

hallway near you. There's a woman who needs you to know her, a child who needs a
name and a hill, a father who needs a call, a brother who needs to be asked, a job

  Numbers 6:24-26 (NIV)
  The LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face shine on you and be gracious to you; the
  LORD turn his face toward you and give you peace.



   The LORD bless you and keep you. Keep: the second verb of Genesis 2:15, the
one you were told to do for the garden, said back over you by the One who does it
for you. So go work it, and go keep it. Reject passivity, accept responsibility, lead
courageously, and expect the greater reward.
   These men are my brothers, and there's a chair open at the table. Come be one.

Before you close the book

   I've told you where the men in this book are on this particular Tuesday, and I've
told you the four marks one last time, and I've said the blessing the old priests said.
There's one more thing I'd like to say to you, and I'd like to say it the way I'd say it
across a table.
   You're going to fail at this. So am I. The men in this chapter failed at it this week,
and next Tuesday they'll get up and turn back toward the Father and stand in their
gardens again, and that's the whole life. If you've read this far you have a page
written and a brother asked and a mark you're working on, and that's more than
most men do with most books, and the Father who sees in secret has counted every
line of it. May He keep you, the way He asked Adam to keep the garden, and may
He make His face shine on you on the ordinary days, and may you find one day, at a
table that hasn't been set yet, that these men were your brothers all along.
