   You're all in the room, and I'm going to write as if you are. When your season

   One more thing before we go on. The men you'll meet in these pages, Roy and
Tomás and Kenny and the others, are composites, which means the conversations
happened but the names and details are mixed so that no one can be picked out of a
lineup.

The marks at every age

   The four marks don't change with age, but the garden does, and I want you to
see them on four men before we take them one at a time.
   A young man of twenty-two rejects passivity when he stops waiting for a wife or
a career to make him serious and gets serious now, in an apartment with
roommates. He accepts responsibility when he carries his own life, the rent and the
doctor and the truth, without a father standing over him. He leads courageously
when he's the first of his friends to say a hard true thing, or to pray at a table, or to
ask a woman out plainly. And he expects the greater reward when he does all of it
without an audience, because nobody claps for a single man who lives right, and
he's learning that the Father does.
    A young father rejects passivity when he turns toward his exhausted wife inside
the chaos instead of waiting for things to calm down. He accepts responsibility
when he takes the two o'clock shift without being asked. He leads courageously
when he goes first into the apology, or the prayer over the crib, or the conversation
about money that nobody wants. And he expects the greater reward when he does it
all at three in the morning with nobody watching, because he believes God sees the
three o'clock.
   A man in his fifties rejects passivity when he notices that he's drifted toward the
place that claps for him and comes home. He accepts responsibility when he says
"that's mine" about the distance in his marriage instead of explaining it. He leads
courageously when he walks across a church parking lot to a younger man who

How to read the rest of this book

   Every chapter from here on ends the same way, with four short lines, and each
line will come with a plain sentence about why it matters for that chapter. Name it
asks you to say, in your own words and out loud, what the chapter touched. Do this
week gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday; not a program, one thing. Tell
a brother asks you to say it to another man, because a mark you keep secret never
hardens into anything. Carry this hands you one line to take with you.
   And if you reach a chapter having not done the last one's thing, which will
happen, don't skip it in shame; say so to your brother and do it this week. If you
don't have a brother yet, don't skip that third line. Chapter 13 is for you, and it's
going to ask you for twenty minutes a week. Until you get there, telling a brother
can mean writing it on the back of a receipt and telling God, who has heard worse
than anything you've got.
  Now we've got four marks and a lot of Tuesdays ahead of us. Let's look at each
one the way you'd look at a man's hands.
