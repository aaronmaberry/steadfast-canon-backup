CHAPTER 25



The Bed Is a Covenant
   Part 4: The house
  Two ranchers I knew traded a stretch of grazing land on a handshake in 1979.
No lawyer and no paper. One of them told me about it forty years later, and I asked
what would have happened if the other man had backed out.
   He looked at me as though I'd asked what would happen if the sun came up in
the west. "He gave me his word," he said. "That's not a thing you back out of. That's
a thing you are."
   That's the difference between a contract and a covenant, and it's a difference
most men never learn about marriage. A contract protects you if the other party
fails; it's built for the exit. A covenant binds you regardless; it's built for the staying.
When those two men shook hands they weren't agreeing to terms. They were
putting themselves into each other's keeping, and the handshake was the sign of it,
the visible thing that stood for the invisible thing.
   The bed is that handshake. This chapter is about what that means, because
almost nobody has ever told you, and the culture has been telling you the opposite
loudly and for your whole life.

What the culture says, what the church said, what God says

    The culture says sex is recreation, a thing two adults do for fun, like bowling,
where the only rules are consent and enthusiasm and anything else is repression. Or
it says sex is self-expression: it's about who you are, your identity, your authenticity,
your needs. Either way, it's about you, and the other person is the equipment.
   The church, for a long stretch, said the opposite and got it just as wrong. Sex was
dirty and dangerous, the thing good boys didn't think about and good husbands did

quickly and quietly and with a little shame. A lot of men in their fifties and sixties
were raised on that and are still carrying it into their bedrooms.
   God says something different from both. He says the bed is a covenant act. It's
the one-flesh sign of a promise made before Him. It's the physical enactment of "I
am yours and you are mine, all of me, permanently." That's why the very first thing
the Bible says about a husband and wife, before the fall, is this.

  Genesis 2:24-25 (NIV)
  That is why a man leaves his father and mother and is united to his wife, and they become one flesh.
  Adam and his wife were both naked, and they felt no shame.



   One flesh, and no shame. Sex in the blueprint isn't recreation and it isn't dirt. It's
two people becoming one thing, in the sight of God, with nothing hidden, and it's
called good. And it's held in honor rather than embarrassment.

  Hebrews 13:4 (NIV)
  Marriage should be honored by all, and the marriage bed kept pure, for God will judge the adulterer
  and all the sexually immoral.



  The marriage bed kept pure. Not kept quiet, and not kept minimal. Kept pure,
which means kept for what it's for, kept between the two people it belongs to, and
kept honored. God isn't embarrassed by the bed. He built it, and He's as fiercely
protective of it as that rancher was of a handshake.
  Once you see the bed as a covenant sign, every question about it gets clearer.
What a husband does in it. What he does with his eyes outside it. What a single man
does before it. What a man does when the bed goes quiet for a season or for good.
All of it comes back to a single question: am I keeping the covenant, or breaking it?

What the covenant means in the bed

  Let me speak to husbands first, because most of you have never had a man talk to
you about this anywhere but a locker room. The covenant is exclusive, all the way
down. Proverbs has a picture of this worth memorizing.

   Your own cistern and your own well. Not the neighbor's, not the screen's, not

where your body goes. Jesus took it all the way inside.

  Matthew 5:28 (NIV)
  But I tell you that anyone who looks at a woman lustfully has already committed adultery with her

  Already committed adultery, Jesus says. A husband who's faithful with his body
and unfaithful with his eyes is a man with a cracked door in his covenant, and his
wife can feel the draft even if she can't say where it's coming from. The cistern is
hers alone, and that includes the part of you nobody sees. The covenant is generous.
Paul wrote a sentence about the marriage bed that turned the ancient world upside
down and still turns most men's assumptions upside down.

  1 Corinthians 7:3-4 (NIV)
  The husband should fulfill his marital duty to his wife, and likewise the wife to her husband. The
  wife does not have authority over her own body but yields it to her husband. In the same way, the
  husband does not have authority over his own body but yields it to his wife.



    In the same way. The husband doesn't own his own body either; it's hers. This
isn't a verse about a wife's obligation to a husband, though men have twisted it that
way for centuries. It's a verse about mutual self-giving, and the husband's line is the
surprising one. Your body is for her. Which means the bed isn't about your release.
It's about her delight. A husband who understands the covenant goes into the bed
asking what she needs and how she's made and what would bless her, and the
strange, backward, biblical thing is that a man who does that gets more out of the
bed than a man who takes.
   The covenant is present. A man can be absent in bed the same way he's absent in
the recliner. He can be there in body and somewhere else in his head: on a screen he
looked at last week, on a fantasy, on the clock. A wife knows the difference. She
knows when her husband is with her and when he's using her to get somewhere else.

The covenant means you're there, with her, this woman, in this bed, and no one else
is in the room, including the ones who live in your memory.
   The covenant is patient and tender. She isn't a screen, and she isn't you. Her
body works differently and on a different clock, and a man who has studied his
wife, as chapter 22 asked, knows this and honors it. Hurry is a kind of theft.
Tenderness is a kind of worship. The men I know whose wives speak warmly about
their marriages after thirty years are without exception men who learned to go slow.
    The covenant is never a weapon. Not withheld as punishment, not demanded as
payment, not used as leverage in a fight or as a scoreboard in a marriage. The
moment the bed becomes a tool for getting your way, it stops being a covenant sign
and becomes a contract negotiation, and everything Adam and Eve had in Genesis 2
is gone. If the bed has gone quiet in your marriage, and that happens, the answer is
never to make her pay for it. The answer is to find out what's actually wrong, which
is almost never about the bed.
    The covenant is honest. Most couples never talk about it. They do it or don't do
it and never say a sentence about what they need, what hurts, what they're afraid of,
what they'd love. A covenant is a spoken thing; the ranchers said words before they
shook. Talk to your wife about the bed, gently, not in the bed and not after a
disappointment. Ask what she'd want more of and less of. Tell her the truth about
yourself. Two people who can talk about the bed can keep it for fifty years.
   And the covenant is protected. Beds go quiet from neglect long before they go
quiet from age. The pursuit from chapter 24, the presence, the tenderness that has
nothing to do with the bedroom: those are how a husband keeps the well from
running dry. A man who only touches his wife to initiate will find one day that she
flinches at his hand, and he'll wonder why, and the answer is that he taught her his
touch always wanted something.

The screen, in the covenant frame

   Chapter 9 talked about the itch as an exit and gave you tools: one brother with
the whole truth, filters with his password, the phone in the kitchen, the hour filled,
and you should use them. But I want to say it differently here, because the frame
changes the fight.
  You aren't fighting pornography because it's against a rule. You're fighting it
because you made a covenant with a real woman, and every image is a small

breaking of it. It isn't a victimless private habit. It has a victim, and she sleeps next to
you. It trains your appetite for novelty inside a covenant built on one well. It makes
your wife a means to an end instead of the end. It rewires what your body responds

   For the husband, the fight against the screen is covenant-keeping, in the same
category as not sleeping with the coworker. Fight it like that. Not with white
knuckles, but with a brother, a filter, a habit, and a clear picture in your head of the
woman you shook hands with, and of the Father who sees what's done in secret and
keeps every faithful Tuesday on the books.

In her seasons, and in yours

    The covenant holds through every season, and the seasons change what keeping
it looks like. This is where husbands do the most damage by not knowing, so let me
be plain.
   After a baby, her body has been through a war. The doctor will say six weeks; her
body and her heart may say longer, and she won't be able to explain why in a way
that sounds like a reason. Your claims are laid down. Not your love; your claims.
She's exhausted, she's touched out, she's self-conscious about a body that doesn't
look or feel like it did, she may be sore in ways she won't describe, and she may be
quietly terrified that you're disappointed in her. You aren't keeping count. You're
telling her she's beautiful and meaning it when nothing's going to happen. You're
touching her in the third way, the way that wants nothing. And when she's ready,
you go slow, and you ask, and you stop if she needs you to. Tomás told me the
sentence that mattered was his own: "I'm not in a hurry. I'm not going anywhere.
You tell me." He said the bed came back stronger than before, months later, because
she'd learned she could trust him with her body when it was weakest.
  In illness, hers or yours, the covenant doesn't pause when the body does; it
changes shape. A husband whose wife is going through chemotherapy keeps the
covenant by tenderness that has nothing to do with the bed, by being present in the
bathroom at three in the morning, by telling her she's still his and meaning it when

she's lost her hair. That's the bed as covenant sign with the sign changed. The
covenant is the same.
   In the middle years, something happens to a woman in her forties and fifties that
most husbands know nothing about and have never asked about, and it changes her
body and her desire and her comfort in ways she may be embarrassed to name. A
man who has studied his wife knows this is coming and doesn't take it as rejection.
He asks, and he's patient, and he goes to the doctor with her if there's a doctor to go
to. He makes the bed a safe place for a woman whose body is doing something new,
and she'll remember for the rest of her life that he did.
   After forty years, Roy told me, with a grin I didn't expect from a former state
trooper, that the bed at seventy was better than the bed at thirty. "Less often.
Slower. I actually know her now." That's the covenant paying out. Men who kept
the well their whole lives get to drink from it in old age, and it's sweet.
  And when it's no longer possible, for one or both, the covenant holds. Mr.
Alvarez, bathing his wife every morning while she doesn't know his name, is
keeping the marriage bed pure at seventy-eight. Nothing is happening in it. He's still
one flesh with her. He said "in sickness" in 1968 and he meant the whole thing.

If there is no wife yet

  I told you in chapter 21 that the covenant starts before the bed. Here's what I
mean.
   If you're single and you intend to marry, every choice you make right now about
your body is a choice about a covenant you haven't made yet with a woman you
may not have met. You're either building it or spending it. A man who has kept
himself for his wife walks into the bed on his wedding night with something to give
her that can't be given twice: all of him, unshared. A man who hasn't walks in with
a comparison in his head and a well that's been drunk from.

If you're single again

   Kenny, and every man in your seat: single again is single. I know that's harder to
hear than it is for the man who has never married, because you know what you're
missing in a way he doesn't. The itch is going to come at you like a freight train in
the first two years, and the culture is going to tell you that a grown man who has
been married has earned the right to do as he pleases, and he hasn't. The covenant
standard has no clause for men who used to have a bed.
   That means no sex outside a new covenant. It means not moving in with
someone because "we're basically married." Basically married is not married; there's
no handshake, and the ranchers would tell you a handshake is not a formality. It
means the brother with the whole truth and the phone in the kitchen, the same as
the twenty-six-year-old, with more at stake, because your children are watching how
a man handles a body when it's hard.
   And if you're a widower, the same is true, and I'm sorry, because you kept the
covenant for fifty years and now the bed is empty and nobody warned you about
the loneliness of it. God knows about it, and He doesn't ask you to pretend the
emptiness isn't there. He asks you to keep the bed pure in the empty years the way
you kept it in the full ones, and to let your brothers carry some of the weight, and to
trust that the Father who saw every faithful night sees these too.

What the bed preaches

   Paul says the strangest thing about marriage at the end of Ephesians 5. He quotes
the one-flesh verse from Genesis, and then he says it's a profound mystery, and that


The Bed Is a Covenant                                                               247
he's actually talking about Christ and the church.

  Ephesians 5:31 (NIV)
  'For this reason a man will leave his father and mother and be united to his wife, and the two will
  become one flesh.'



   Christ and the church. That's what the one-flesh covenant between a husband
and wife is finally about. It's a picture of the covenant between Jesus and His
people. Which means the bed preaches. When a husband is exclusive, generous,
present, patient, never weaponizing, honest, and protective in the bed, he's showing
his wife and the watching world a small, real picture of how Christ loves the church:
faithfully, self-givingly, with delight, forever. And when a man breaks the covenant,
with his body or his eyes, he's preaching a lie about God.
   That's how serious it is, and that's how good it is. The bed isn't recreation and it
isn't dirt. It's a handshake between two people who have put themselves in each
other's keeping before God, renewed as often as they're able, for as long as they both
live, and it stands for the largest thing that's true.
   The rancher was right. A covenant isn't a thing you back out of. It's a thing you
are.

Before you turn the page

   This is the chapter men skim, and if you didn't, I'm glad, because the bed is
where a great many good men have quietly broken faith and never told anyone. If
that's you, the drawer can be opened; David's was, and his standing was restored in a
breath. The covenant isn't a rule you've failed; it's a handshake you can keep starting
tomorrow, with a real woman or with the one you're praying for. May the Father
who honors the marriage bed give you a well of your own to drink from and the
faithfulness to drink from no other, and may your bed preach the truth about Him.
