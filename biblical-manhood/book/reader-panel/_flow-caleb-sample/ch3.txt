The Four Marks
   Part 1: The man you were built to be
   You made it through the last chapter and I hope you set the pile down. If you
did, you've got a harder problem now than the one you started with, because a man
who knows the picture he was handed is broken still has to answer the question
underneath it. If that isn't what a man is, then what is?
  I want to answer that in this chapter, in one sentence you can carry in your
pocket, and then I want to spend the next four chapters taking the sentence apart so
you can see how each piece looks on a man in the middle of an ordinary week. But
before I give you the sentence, I want to give you a picture, because the picture is
what keeps the sentence from becoming one more thing on the shelf.

What a mark is

   My grandfather's hands looked like a map of every job he ever worked. There was
a white ridge across his left palm from a fence stretcher that slipped in 1961, and his
right index finger never straightened all the way after something happened to it that
he never explained. The knuckles were wide and the skin over them was thick in the
way skin gets when a man works outside for fifty years. You didn't have to ask him
what he'd done with his life. It was written on him, and anyone who knew how to
read hands could read it.
    That is what a mark is, and I want you to hold onto the word, because it's going
to do a lot of work in this book. A mark isn't a feeling you have about yourself, and
it isn't a title somebody gives you. A mark is what gets left on you by what you've
done over and over again for a long time. My grandfather didn't get that callus from
thinking about a shovel, or from wanting to be the kind of man who used one, or
from going to a weekend event about shovels. He got it from the shovel, on ten

   I say all that first because, as I said last chapter, there's a whole shelf of books for
men that start inside the chest, and I want you to see from the first page how this
one is different. Those books will tell you about your heart and your desires and
your longing for something wild, and some of that is true, and some of those men
were good to me when I was younger. I'm not writing against them. But this book
makes a plainer claim. The marks I'm about to give you are not things you feel.
They are things you do, and they show. A man could sit in a deer blind at dawn and
feel every wild thing a book ever promised him, and drive home and let his wife
carry the entire house alone, and what was in his chest that morning would leave no
mark on anything. What he did when he got home would.
  So here is the sentence. I didn't invent it, and I want to say so plainly. Robert
Lewis put it in these words years ago, teaching men what a man is, and the sentence
has been passed from man to man ever since because it holds. I've held it up against
my own life for a long time now, and against the lives of men in every season I can
name, and I have never found a place where it lies.
  A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and
expects the greater reward, God's reward.

   Read it again, slower, and notice what kind of sentence it is. It names four marks,
not four moods and not four qualities you either have or don't, but four things a
man does, which over time leave four things you can see on him the way you could
see the fence stretcher on my grandfather's palm.

The first mark: he rejects passivity

   Every Texas town I've ever spent time in has a man on a tailgate. He is not a bad
man, and I want to be fair to him. He has opinions about the drought and the
school board and the way the city runs the water department, and he'll share all of
them with you while the work he came to do waits behind him. He's waiting for the
right time, or the right price, or the right feeling, or some sign that hasn't come, and
he has been waiting so long that the waiting has started to look like wisdom to him
and to the men who sit with him.
  Passivity is the default setting of a man. It isn't something most of us choose so
much as something we fall into, the way a truck tire falls into a rut in a caliche road
and stays there because staying there is easier than climbing out. And what makes it
dangerous is that it almost never looks like laziness. It looks like patience. It looks



The Four Marks                                                                        27
like keeping the peace. It sounds like "I didn't want to overstep," and the man saying
it usually believes he's being humble. The first man in the Bible was passive in
exactly this way, and the text goes out of its way to make sure we notice. When the
serpent came into the garden and started twisting the one command God had given,
the command the man alone had heard, the man was there.

     Genesis 3:6 (NIV)
     When the woman saw that the fruit of the tree was good for food and pleasing to the eye, and also
     desirable for gaining wisdom, she took some and ate it. She also gave some to her husband, who was
     with her, and he ate it.



   He was with her. He didn't grab the fruit and he didn't argue for it. He simply
stood there while the thing he'd been told to guard against walked in and did its
work, and his standing there was enough to break the world. Those three words,
"who was with her," have been indicting men ever since, and they indict the man on
the tailgate as surely as they indicted Adam.
   Rejecting passivity doesn't mean becoming loud, or pushy, or the kind of man
who mistakes volume for strength. It means that when something needs doing and
you know it and you're the one standing there, you get off the tailgate. It means you
bring up the hard conversation instead of hoping it gets brought up. It means you
don't require a feeling in order to start. The next chapter is about this, and I'll show
you how it looks in a house with a baby in it and in a house where the kids have
gone and in an apartment where a man lives alone.

The second mark: he accepts responsibility

    The second mark is weight. I helped a friend move a gun safe one August, four
hundred pounds of steel down a narrow hallway in a hundred and four degrees, and
I learned something about responsibility that I've never forgotten. There is a
moment with a load like that when you're committed. You've lifted it, your friend
has lifted it, and the only way through is through. You can't set it down in the
hallway because there's no room. You can't decide halfway along that this isn't really
your safe. You carry it to where it's going, and then you set it down, and that's the
only order the thing can be done in.
  That's what responsibility feels like on a man. It's a weight he picks up and
doesn't set down in the hallway. And I want you to notice that I said he picks it up.

A boy waits for responsibility to be assigned to him, and then argues about whether
the assignment was fair. A man looks at what's in front of him and says "that's
mine," and he says it even when it isn't his fault and even when nobody would
blame him for walking past. The bill nobody wants to open is his. The kid nobody's
talking to is his. The apology nobody's making is his. The neighbor's fence that
blew down on his side of the line is his. A man reaches for the weight, and over time
the reaching leaves a mark on him. There's a line in Lamentations that surprised me
the first time I read it slowly enough to hear it.

  Lamentations 3:27 (NIV)
  It is good for a man to bear the yoke while he is young.



   It is good, the text says, and not merely tolerable, and not a necessary evil that
builds character. The weight is part of what shapes a man into a man, and a man
who has been protected from it or who has walked past it all his life is, in a way
that's hard to name and easy to see, unshaped. Chapter 5 will show you how to pick
weight up without being crushed by it, and how to tell the difference between the
weight God gave you and the weight you grabbed in order to feel important.

The third mark: he leads courageously

    Every March there's a stock tank somewhere in this state with a group of boys
standing on the bank. The water is cold, colder than any of them expected, and
everybody is waiting for somebody to go first. That is what leadership is. It isn't the
title or the corner office or being the one in charge. It's being the one who goes first
into the cold water so that the others can follow.
   Somebody has to go first, in every house and every crew and every church.
Somebody has to be the first one to say "I was wrong" in a marriage that's been quiet
for three days. Somebody has to be the first one to pray out loud at a table where
nobody does, the first one to call the brother who's been drifting, the first one to say
the true thing in the meeting where everybody's nodding. That's what courage is
for, and I want to be clear about what courage is, because men get it wrong in a way
that keeps them on the bank. Courage is not the absence of fear. If you weren't
afraid, it wouldn't take courage to move. Courage is what happens when a man is
afraid and puts his boots on anyway and walks.

The fourth mark: he expects the greater reward

  The last mark is the one that separates a good man from a godly one, and it's the
one most men skip, so I've put it last on purpose and I'll give it the most room.
    Pecan trees don't hurry. If you plant one this fall, you'll water it for years before
it gives you a single nut worth cracking, and it won't hit its stride for a decade or
more. Which means that every man who has ever planted a pecan orchard was
planting for somebody else. For a son, or a grandson, or a family that might sell the
place before the trees mature. You don't plant pecans because you intend to eat
pecans. You plant them because you believe in a harvest you won't hold in your
own hands, and you're willing to spend your strength on it anyway.
   That's the fourth mark. A real man expects the greater reward, God's reward, and
that expectation changes what he does with his Tuesdays more than anything else in
this book. He can do the right thing when nobody's looking, because he isn't
working for the people who are looking. He can stay married through a hard
decade, because he isn't measuring the marriage by this year's return. He can give
money that won't come back and time that won't be thanked, because he believes
what Jesus said about a Father who sees what's done in secret.

     Hebrews 11:6 (NIV)
     And without faith it is impossible to please God, because anyone who comes to him must believe that
     he exists and that he rewards those who earnestly seek him.



   He rewards, the writer says, and God isn't embarrassed by that word, and you
shouldn't be either. The man who doesn't expect a reward from God will go
looking for one somewhere else, and there are only so many places to look: applause,

Why four, and why these

   You could argue for a different list, and men have. Let me tell you why I've
stopped arguing.
   Each of these four marks answers a specific way that men break. Passivity is how
a man breaks by doing nothing. Blame and evasion are how he breaks by refusing
weight. Cowardice, which almost always travels under a nicer name, is how he
breaks by hanging back. And living for the wrong reward is how he breaks slowly,
over forty years, while looking successful to everyone around him. I have never met
a man in trouble whose trouble didn't trace back to one of those four. Not in a
marriage, not in a business, not in a church, and not in a jail cell.
   And I want you to notice the shape of the four together. The first three are
things a good man of any faith or none might do, and plenty of men reject passivity
and carry weight and go first without ever thinking about God, and I'm grateful for
every one of them. The fourth is what makes the other three Christian. It's the
reason a man can keep doing the first three when they cost him and nobody claps.
Take the fourth mark out and you have a self-improvement list, and
self-improvement lists run out of fuel. Leave it in and you have a way of life that
will outlast you.

A word about seasons, said once

  I want to say this here so I don't have to keep saying it, and so you'll trust me
when I don't.
   You're reading this in a particular season, and I know it. You may be twenty-four
and single, and nobody has ever looked at you and called you a man out loud. You
may have a four-month-old and not have slept a full night since spring. You may be
divorced and seeing your kids every other weekend and not at all sure this book is
for you. You may be sixty-eight and have turned in a badge or a hard hat or a set of
keys last year, and be wondering what a man does with his mornings now. You may
have buried your wife.

The marks at every age

   The four marks don't change with age, but the garden does, and I want you to
see them on four men before we take them one at a time.
   A young man of twenty-two rejects passivity when he stops waiting for a wife or
a career to make him serious and gets serious now, in an apartment with
roommates. He accepts responsibility when he carries his own life, the rent and the
doctor and the truth, without a father standing over him. He leads courageously
when he's the first of his friends to say a hard true thing, or to pray at a table, or to
ask a woman out plainly. And he expects the greater reward when he does all of it
without an audience, because nobody claps for a single man who lives right, and
he's learning that the Father does.
    A young father rejects passivity when he turns toward his exhausted wife inside
the chaos instead of waiting for things to calm down. He accepts responsibility
when he takes the two o'clock shift without being asked. He leads courageously
when he goes first into the apology, or the prayer over the crib, or the conversation
about money that nobody wants. And he expects the greater reward when he does it
all at three in the morning with nobody watching, because he believes God sees the
three o'clock.
   A man in his fifties rejects passivity when he notices that he's drifted toward the
place that claps for him and comes home. He accepts responsibility when he says
"that's mine" about the distance in his marriage instead of explaining it. He leads
courageously when he walks across a church parking lot to a younger man who

   An older man rejects passivity when he gets up at 5:30 with no badge to put on

How to read the rest of this book

   Every chapter from here on ends the same way, with four short lines, and each
line will come with a plain sentence about why it matters for that chapter. Name it
asks you to say, in your own words and out loud, what the chapter touched. Do this
week gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday; not a program, one thing. Tell
a brother asks you to say it to another man, because a mark you keep secret never
hardens into anything. Carry this hands you one line to take with you.
   And if you reach a chapter having not done the last one's thing, which will
happen, don't skip it in shame; say so to your brother and do it this week. If you
don't have a brother yet, don't skip that third line. Chapter 13 is for you, and it's
going to ask you for twenty minutes a week. Until you get there, telling a brother
can mean writing it on the back of a receipt and telling God, who has heard worse
than anything you've got.
  Now we've got four marks and a lot of Tuesdays ahead of us. Let's look at each
one the way you'd look at a man's hands.
