INTRODUCTION




Introduction
   I have spent most of my adult life sitting across tables from men. Kitchen tables,
mostly, and the ones at the back of a church fellowship hall, and the tailgates and
truck cabs and breakfast counters that serve the same purpose in this part of the
country. Some of the men were young enough to be my sons and some were old
enough to be my father, and I have come to believe that underneath the different
jobs and different decades and different troubles, they were all asking me the same
thing. They wanted to know what a man is, and whether they were one, and if not,
whether it was too late.
  This book is my long answer to that question. It is not the only answer and I
won't pretend it is, but it is the one I have watched hold up in the lives of real men
over a lot of years, and I have tried to write it the way I would say it to you if you
were sitting across from me with your hands around a cup of coffee going cold.

Who I'm writing to

  I am writing to a man in every season of life, and I mean that literally, so let me
name you.
   You may be twenty-four and single and not sure anyone has ever looked at you
and called you a man out loud. You may be twenty-nine with a baby who does not
sleep and a wife you love and cannot seem to reach anymore through the
exhaustion. You may be forty-four with three kids and a career at full throttle and a
growing sense that you are providing everything for your family except yourself.
You may be divorced, seeing your children every other weekend, sitting in an
apartment you never expected to live in and wondering whether a book like this is
even for you. You may have just watched the last child leave and found yourself
across a quiet kitchen from a woman you're not sure you know anymore. You may
have turned in a badge or a hard hat or a set of keys last year and be wondering what
a man does with his mornings now. You may have buried your wife.
   You are all in this room, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise by writing only
to the one with the mortgage and the minivan. The counsel in this book changes as

What I'm claiming

    The center of this book is one sentence. I didn't invent it, and I'm not going to
pretend I did. Robert Lewis gave it that shape years ago, and men have been
handing it to one another ever since, and I've never found a better way to say it. But
I have held it up against my own life for a long time, and against the lives of
hundreds of men in every season I just named, and I have never found a place where
it lies.
  A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and
expects the greater reward, God's reward.
   Those are the four marks, and chapter 3 will explain them, and chapters 4
through 7 will take them one at a time. For now I only want you to notice what
kind of sentence it is, because it isn't a sentence about what a man feels. There is a
whole shelf of books for men that start inside the chest, with your heart and your
longings and your wild places, and some of those books were good to me when I
was young, and I'm not writing against them. But this book makes a plainer claim.
The four marks are things you do, on ordinary days, and they show. They leave a
mark on a man the way a life of work leaves a mark on his hands, and you can see
them or you can see their absence, and no amount of feeling makes up for either.

How the book is built

   The book has five parts and they move in a particular order, and it helps to know
the order before you start.
   Part 1 lays out the four marks and takes each one in turn. Part 2 is about what
you inherited, the chair at the end of your childhood table and what sat in it, and
about what is yours to do with that inheritance now, including the parts of your
trouble that have nothing to do with your father and everything to do with you.
Part 3 goes back to the beginning of the whole human story and shows you where
you sit inside it, because a man who only knows his own story will read it wrong.

Part 4 is about the house: the woman, the bed, the daughter, the son. Part 5 is about
the long road, the forty or fifty years of work and feeling and season and obscurity
that make up most of a man's life, and about how to finish it.
   Running through all five parts is one ordinary day. Chapter 1 is called "A Quiet
Tuesday," and the reason is that I have come to believe a man's life is decided almost
entirely on days like that, days with nothing special in them, when nobody's
watching and nothing seems to be at stake. It isn't decided at the altar, or at the
retreat, or at the ceremony, but on Tuesday, and every chapter in this book will land
at some point on a Tuesday-sized picture, and I make no apology for it.

The four lines at the end

   Every chapter ends the same way, with four short lines, and each line comes with
a plain sentence about why it matters for that particular chapter. I want to explain
them once here so I don't have to keep explaining them.
   Name it asks you to say out loud, in your own words, what the chapter touched
in you. Men are good at nodding along and terrible at saying "that's me," and
nothing changes in a man until he says it.
  Do this week gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday, not a program and
not a list, but one thing, and if you do only that one thing from each chapter, you'll
have done more than most men do with most books.
   Tell a brother asks you to say the thing out loud to another man. A mark you
keep to yourself never hardens into anything. If you don't have a brother yet,
chapter 13 is for you, and it is going to ask you for about twenty minutes a week,
which most men can find and most men have never been asked for. Until you get
there, telling a brother can mean writing it down where you'll see it and telling God,
who has heard worse.
   Carry this hands you one line to take with you into the week.
   One more thing about the four lines, because I know how a tired man reads a
book like this. You're going to get to a chapter having not done the last chapter's
thing. When that happens, don't skip it in shame and don't quit the book. Tell your
brother you didn't do it, which is itself the "tell a brother" line, and do it this week
instead. Every man in these pages has missed a week. The ones who changed are the
ones who said so and went again.

The men you'll meet

   You're going to meet a number of men in these pages by name: Roy, who turned
in a state trooper's badge; Tomás, who has a baby who doesn't sleep; Kenny, who's
divorced and calls his son every Tuesday; Micah, who sat at the single men's table at
weddings for six years; and others. They are composites, which means the
conversations happened, at real tables and with real men, but the names and the
details have been mixed together so that no one can be picked out of a lineup, and
so that you might recognize yourself in one of them without recognizing anyone
else.

One more thing before you start

   I'm not going to ask you to join a program, buy a workbook, or attend anything.
I'm going to ask you to read, to do one thing a week, and to find one other man to
do it with. That's the whole ask. It is small on purpose, because I've watched too
many men leave a conference on fire and be back in the recliner by the following
Tuesday, and I've come to believe that the small steady thing outlasts the big loud
one every time.
    So find your cup of coffee. Pull up a chair. Let me tell you about a Tuesday.
