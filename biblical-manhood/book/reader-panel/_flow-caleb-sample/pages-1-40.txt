INTRODUCTION




Introduction
   I have spent most of my adult life sitting across tables from men. Kitchen tables,
mostly, and the ones at the back of a church fellowship hall, and the tailgates and
truck cabs and breakfast counters that serve the same purpose in this part of the
country. Some of the men were young enough to be my sons and some were old
enough to be my father, and I have come to believe that underneath the different
jobs and different decades and different troubles, they were all asking me the same
thing. They wanted to know what a man is, and whether they were one, and if not,
whether it was too late.
  This book is my long answer to that question. It is not the only answer and I
won't pretend it is, but it is the one I have watched hold up in the lives of real men
over a lot of years, and I have tried to write it the way I would say it to you if you
were sitting across from me with your hands around a cup of coffee going cold.

Who I'm writing to

  I am writing to a man in every season of life, and I mean that literally, so let me
name you.
   You may be twenty-four and single and not sure anyone has ever looked at you
and called you a man out loud. You may be twenty-nine with a baby who does not
sleep and a wife you love and cannot seem to reach anymore through the
exhaustion. You may be forty-four with three kids and a career at full throttle and a
growing sense that you are providing everything for your family except yourself.
You may be divorced, seeing your children every other weekend, sitting in an
apartment you never expected to live in and wondering whether a book like this is
even for you. You may have just watched the last child leave and found yourself
across a quiet kitchen from a woman you're not sure you know anymore. You may
have turned in a badge or a hard hat or a set of keys last year and be wondering what
a man does with his mornings now. You may have buried your wife.
   You are all in this room, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise by writing only
to the one with the mortgage and the minivan. The counsel in this book changes as



Introduction                                                                             3
your season changes, and when it does, I'll turn toward you inside the paragraph
and say so. What I won't do is stop every chapter to run down the list by age,
because you'd start skipping and so would I. In chapter 32 we'll sit down and talk
about seasons on purpose, all of them, in one place. Until then, trust that I know
you're here.

What I'm claiming

    The center of this book is one sentence. I didn't invent it, and I'm not going to
pretend I did. Robert Lewis gave it that shape years ago, and men have been
handing it to one another ever since, and I've never found a better way to say it. But
I have held it up against my own life for a long time, and against the lives of
hundreds of men in every season I just named, and I have never found a place where
it lies.
  A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and
expects the greater reward, God's reward.
   Those are the four marks, and chapter 3 will explain them, and chapters 4
through 7 will take them one at a time. For now I only want you to notice what
kind of sentence it is, because it isn't a sentence about what a man feels. There is a
whole shelf of books for men that start inside the chest, with your heart and your
longings and your wild places, and some of those books were good to me when I
was young, and I'm not writing against them. But this book makes a plainer claim.
The four marks are things you do, on ordinary days, and they show. They leave a
mark on a man the way a life of work leaves a mark on his hands, and you can see
them or you can see their absence, and no amount of feeling makes up for either.

How the book is built

   The book has five parts and they move in a particular order, and it helps to know
the order before you start.
   Part 1 lays out the four marks and takes each one in turn. Part 2 is about what
you inherited, the chair at the end of your childhood table and what sat in it, and
about what is yours to do with that inheritance now, including the parts of your
trouble that have nothing to do with your father and everything to do with you.
Part 3 goes back to the beginning of the whole human story and shows you where
you sit inside it, because a man who only knows his own story will read it wrong.

Part 4 is about the house: the woman, the bed, the daughter, the son. Part 5 is about
the long road, the forty or fifty years of work and feeling and season and obscurity
that make up most of a man's life, and about how to finish it.
   Running through all five parts is one ordinary day. Chapter 1 is called "A Quiet
Tuesday," and the reason is that I have come to believe a man's life is decided almost
entirely on days like that, days with nothing special in them, when nobody's
watching and nothing seems to be at stake. It isn't decided at the altar, or at the
retreat, or at the ceremony, but on Tuesday, and every chapter in this book will land
at some point on a Tuesday-sized picture, and I make no apology for it.

The four lines at the end

   Every chapter ends the same way, with four short lines, and each line comes with
a plain sentence about why it matters for that particular chapter. I want to explain
them once here so I don't have to keep explaining them.
   Name it asks you to say out loud, in your own words, what the chapter touched
in you. Men are good at nodding along and terrible at saying "that's me," and
nothing changes in a man until he says it.
  Do this week gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday, not a program and
not a list, but one thing, and if you do only that one thing from each chapter, you'll
have done more than most men do with most books.
   Tell a brother asks you to say the thing out loud to another man. A mark you
keep to yourself never hardens into anything. If you don't have a brother yet,
chapter 13 is for you, and it is going to ask you for about twenty minutes a week,
which most men can find and most men have never been asked for. Until you get
there, telling a brother can mean writing it down where you'll see it and telling God,
who has heard worse.
   Carry this hands you one line to take with you into the week.
   One more thing about the four lines, because I know how a tired man reads a
book like this. You're going to get to a chapter having not done the last chapter's
thing. When that happens, don't skip it in shame and don't quit the book. Tell your
brother you didn't do it, which is itself the "tell a brother" line, and do it this week
instead. Every man in these pages has missed a week. The ones who changed are the
ones who said so and went again.

The men you'll meet

   You're going to meet a number of men in these pages by name: Roy, who turned
in a state trooper's badge; Tomás, who has a baby who doesn't sleep; Kenny, who's
divorced and calls his son every Tuesday; Micah, who sat at the single men's table at
weddings for six years; and others. They are composites, which means the
conversations happened, at real tables and with real men, but the names and the
details have been mixed together so that no one can be picked out of a lineup, and
so that you might recognize yourself in one of them without recognizing anyone
else.

One more thing before you start

   I'm not going to ask you to join a program, buy a workbook, or attend anything.
I'm going to ask you to read, to do one thing a week, and to find one other man to
do it with. That's the whole ask. It is small on purpose, because I've watched too
many men leave a conference on fire and be back in the recliner by the following
Tuesday, and I've come to believe that the small steady thing outlasts the big loud
one every time.
    So find your cup of coffee. Pull up a chair. Let me tell you about a Tuesday.

A Quiet Tuesday
   Part 1: The man you were built to be
   If you had asked me at twenty-five to name the days that would decide what kind
of man I became, I would have given you a list of big ones: the wedding, the day the
first child came, the funeral of a father, the job I took or the job I turned down, the
morning I stood in a river and let a pastor put me under. I would have told you
those were the hinges, and I would have believed it, and I would have been mostly
wrong.
   I've had all of those days now, and I can tell you that not one of them decided
much of anything. They marked things and they announced things, and they were
stones on a riverbank that I can point to and say, that's when. But the deciding
happened somewhere else, in a long string of days I couldn't tell apart if you paid
me, and the more men I sit with, the more I'm convinced it works that way for
every one of us.
   A man's life is decided on Tuesdays. I mean that almost literally. Not on the
Sunday when he stands up in church, and not on the Saturday when he takes his
son to the lease, and not even on the Friday when the paycheck lands or the
Monday when the week's trouble starts. On the day in the middle, with nothing
special in it. The day nobody remembers. The day he comes home tired and the
house is loud and there's a conversation he could have or not have, a call he could
make or not make, a battery he could change or walk under one more time. That's
where it happens. That's where the marks get made.

What a Tuesday looks like

   Let me show you one, because a claim like that ought to be able to survive a
specific day.


A Quiet Tuesday                                                                       9
   A man I'll call Anthony left for work at 6:10 on a Tuesday in October, having
slept badly, because his wife had said something the night before about money that
wasn't quite fair and he'd let it sit, and it was still sitting there in the truck with him
as he drove. He got to the shop, and a screw stripped on the first job of the
morning, and he stood there breathing hard for a minute while his apprentice
pretended not to see. Around ten a customer called to complain about something
that wasn't Anthony's fault, and Anthony was polite to him in a way that cost
something. At lunch he ate in the truck and looked at his phone for forty minutes
and couldn't have told you afterward one thing he'd read.
   In the afternoon his father called. His father calls maybe twice a year, and every
time the phone shows that name Anthony feels nine years old for a second, and this
time he let it ring through to voicemail and told himself he'd call back later. He
finished the day's work well. He is good at his job. He drove home listening to
nothing, pulled into the garage, and stood in the doorway between the garage and
the kitchen for a minute the way he does most nights, hearing his kids be loud and
his wife tell somebody to sit down, not sure why he wasn't walking in.
   Then he walked in. He kissed his wife on the side of her head without looking at
her, and he ate, and he got the kids to bed, more or less, and by 8:40 he was in the
recliner with the game on and his phone in his hand, and his wife was in the
kitchen, and neither of them said anything about the money, and around ten he
went to bed and lay there a while before he slept.
    That's a Tuesday, and there's nothing in it you'd put in a book, and I've just put
it in a book, because I want you to see how much of a man's life was actually
decided in those sixteen hours.
   The thing he let sit from the night before was a decision. Letting his father go to
voicemail was a decision. The doorway was a decision, and so was the recliner, and
so was the kiss on the side of her head without looking, and so was the phone at
lunch and the phone at 8:40. None of them felt like decisions. That's the whole
point. They felt like a day. And a man who has ten thousand days like that will
become a particular kind of man without ever once choosing to, because the
choosing was happening the whole time, in pieces too small to notice.
   Now, Anthony's a good man. I want to be clear about that, because the point of
this book isn't to make good men feel like failures. He didn't sin against anybody on
that Tuesday, not in any way you could name. He worked hard and provided and
came home and was gentle enough. But he is becoming something, one Tuesday at

a time, and if you asked his wife what he's becoming she could tell you, and it isn't
what he'd choose if he thought he was choosing.

Why the big days can't do it

   Here's why I've stopped trusting the big days to make men. A big day is a burst.
It gathers up everything you feel and believe and want to be, and it puts it all on the
table at once, in front of witnesses, with music playing, and that's a good thing,
because God builds days like that into a man's life on purpose, and later in this book
I'm going to tell you to make one for your son. But a burst is not a shape. It doesn't
leave a mark by itself, because a mark is what gets left on you by what you do over
and over, and a big day, by definition, is something you do once.
   Think about your hands. If you've worked with them, they have marks on them:
a ridge of callus across the palm, a finger that doesn't straighten all the way, a scar
from a fence stretcher that slipped. Nobody got those from one hard day. You got
them from a thousand ordinary ones, none of which you remember. The big days
of your working life, the first day and the last and the one when you got hurt, didn't
shape your hands. The ordinary Tuesdays did.
   The men who go to a conference and come home on fire and are back in the
recliner within two weeks aren't weak men. They've just misunderstood where a life
gets made. They put everything into the burst and nothing into the shape, and the
shape is where the marks come from.
  And there's another reason the big days can't do it, which is that on the big days
you're watched. At a wedding, at a baptism, at a ceremony, at a funeral, you know
what a man is supposed to do and everyone's looking, and most men do it. That
doesn't tell you much about the man. What tells you about the man is what he does
when the room is empty, when nobody would know if he made the call or didn't,
opened the drawer or didn't, said the true thing or let it sit. Tuesday is when
nobody's watching. That's exactly why it counts.

What God does with Tuesdays

   If you've been in church a while, you've probably absorbed the idea that the
spiritual life happens in the big moments too, at the retreat and the revival and the
altar, and God does meet men in those places; I'm not going to pretend He doesn't.
But look at how the Bible actually talks about the way a man is formed.


A Quiet Tuesday                                                                         11
     Luke 16:10 (NIV)
     Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with
     very little will also be dishonest with much.



    Very little is a Tuesday phrase if there ever was one. Jesus is saying that the man
who can be trusted in the small unwatched things is the man who can be trusted in
the large watched ones, and that you don't get the second man without the first. He
is saying that the recliner and the voicemail and the kiss without looking are not
neutral. They're the training ground. They are where the man who'll one day stand
in a hard room gets made, or doesn't. And when Jesus tells a story about a man who
built a house that held, notice what He says about how the man built it.

     Matthew 7:24 (NIV)
     Therefore everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man who
     built his house on the rock.



   Puts them into practice. Not hears them and is moved by them. Practice is a
Tuesday word too. It's what you do again and again until it's how you do things.
The wise man in that story didn't build his house in a burst on a big day. He built it
the way houses get built, one course at a time, on days nobody photographed, and
when the rain came the house held because of all those days and not because of any
one of them.
  Most of a man's walk with God is Tuesdays. Most of his marriage is Tuesdays.
Most of his fathering is Tuesdays. If you want to know what kind of man you're
becoming, don't look at how you did at the retreat. Look at last Tuesday.

The other Tuesday

   Let me give you Anthony's Tuesday again, a year later, because I want you to see
that I'm not describing a trap. I'm describing a place where a man has more power
than he thinks.
   He left at 6:10 again, and he'd slept badly again, and there was something from
the night before sitting in the truck with him again. But somewhere on the drive he
said, out loud, "Father, I don't want to carry this all day," and then he pulled over at
a gas station and sent his wife a text that said he was sorry he'd gone quiet, and he'd

like to talk about it after the kids were down. It took him ninety seconds. He was
still tired. But the thing wasn't sitting in the truck with him anymore.
   The screw still stripped. He still stood there breathing hard, but this time he
walked out to the truck, waited a minute and a half for his body to come down, and
came back and finished the job. His father called in the afternoon, and Anthony felt
nine years old for a second, and then he answered, and it was an awkward six
minutes about nothing, and it was the first time in two years he hadn't let it go to
voicemail.
   When he came home he stood in the doorway for a second out of habit and then
he noticed he was doing it and walked in. He put his phone on the counter face
down, which he'd started doing a few months before after another man told him to,
and he found his wife and looked at her when he kissed her. After the kids were
down they sat at the kitchen counter for ten minutes and talked about the money,
and it wasn't a good conversation exactly, but it was one, and he laid down
something he'd wanted to say and said something truer instead. Then he did the
dishes. Then he prayed, badly and briefly, with his hand on her back. Then he went
to bed and slept.
   Nothing about that day would make a book either. His daughter, who is seven
and who watches his face when her mother walks in, would not be able to tell you
what was different. But something was different, and it was different in about six
places, each of which took less than two minutes, and if you gave that man ten
thousand of those Tuesdays instead of the other kind, you would end up with a
different man, and a different marriage, and a different girl watching a different
face.
  That's the claim of this book. Not that you need a big day. That you need to
know what a man does on a Tuesday, and then do it, and then do it again.

The same Tuesday at every age

   A Tuesday doesn't look the same at nineteen as it does at seventy, but it decides
the same things, and I want you to see yourself in one of these four men before we
go on.
  A nineteen-year-old I'll call Eli works the counter at a parts store and lives with
two roommates. His Tuesday has a moment in it around ten at night when the
apartment goes quiet and the phone is in his hand, and what he does in that


A Quiet Tuesday                                                                         13
moment is deciding what kind of man he'll be at thirty far more than anything he'll
say at church on Sunday. He told me once that he thought his real life would start
when something big happened to him. His real life is the ten o'clock moment, and
it's happening now.
   Tomás, who you'll meet more later, is twenty-nine with a baby who doesn't
sleep. His Tuesday has a moment in it at two in the morning when he hears the
crying and hears his wife start to move and could simply lie there, and he's so tired
that lying there feels like the only reasonable thing a man could do. Whether he gets
up is deciding the marriage. Not the anniversary trip they can't afford; the two
o'clock.
   Walt is fifty-five and built a business from one truck to eleven, and his Tuesday
has a moment in it at 6:15 in the evening when he could leave the shop and be home
for dinner or could stay another hour because the shop is quieter than the house.
He stayed for twenty years, one Tuesday at a time, and never once decided to. The
staying decided itself, in pieces too small to notice, until his wife said a sentence in
the kitchen that I'll tell you about in chapter 5.
   And Roy is seventy-two, retired from thirty-one years as a state trooper, and his
Tuesday has a moment in it at 5:30 in the morning when the alarm goes off and

How to read the rest of this book

   Every chapter from here on ends the same way, with four short lines, and each
line will come with a plain sentence about why it matters for that chapter. Name it
asks you to say, in your own words and out loud, what the chapter touched. Do this
week gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday; not a program, one thing. Tell
a brother asks you to say it to another man, because a mark you keep secret never
hardens into anything. Carry this hands you one line to take with you.
   And if you reach a chapter having not done the last one's thing, which will
happen, don't skip it in shame; say so to your brother and do it this week. If you
don't have a brother yet, don't skip that third line. Chapter 13 is for you, and it's
going to ask you for twenty minutes a week. Until you get there, telling a brother
can mean writing it on the back of a receipt and telling God, who has heard worse
than anything you've got.

Stop Waiting
   Part 1: The man you were built to be
   There's a smoke detector in a hallway somewhere near you that's chirping right
now, one chirp every forty seconds or so, because the battery's low. The man of the
house has walked under it two hundred times in the last three weeks. He knows
exactly what it is and exactly where the nine-volt batteries are kept. He is a capable
man who has rebuilt a carburetor and negotiated a raise and coached a ten-year-old's
baseball team to a losing record with real grace, and he cannot bring himself to
spend the ninety seconds it would take to make that chirp stop.
  If you asked him why, he'd tell you he's going to get to it. That's the honest
answer, and it's also the whole problem.
   I don't care about the smoke detector. I care about what it's standing in for,
because that same man has a conversation with his wife that he's going to get to. He
has a call to his brother he's going to get to, a habit he's going to deal with, a debt
he's going to face, a son who's gone quiet that he's going to sit down with, a church
he's going to start showing up at again. He's going to get to all of it. He's been going
to get to it for years, and the years are the point.
   The first mark of a man is that he rejects passivity. This chapter is about what
that looks like on a Tuesday, because the word "passivity" makes most men picture
somebody lying on a couch, and that isn't the man I'm worried about. The man I'm
worried about is busy and tired, and he is working hard on nearly everything except
the handful of things that matter most, and he's waiting, and he doesn't know it.
