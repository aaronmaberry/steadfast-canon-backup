CHAPTER 13



A Man With No Brothers
   Part 2: What you inherited, and what is yours
  The year Walt almost lost the business, he sat in his truck in the parking lot of a
bank in Abilene with his phone in his hand and scrolled through every contact he
had.
   There were three hundred and some names: customers, suppliers, his accountant,
men he'd hunted with for twenty years, men from church whose kids had played
ball with his kids, a brother-in-law in Fort Worth, his pastor. He scrolled all the way
to the bottom and all the way back to the top, and then he set the phone face down
on the seat and sat there for forty minutes.
   There wasn't one name on that phone he could call and say, "I'm scared. I think
I'm about to lose everything and I don't know what to do." Not one. He'd built a
business and a family and a reputation over thirty years, and he had three hundred
contacts and no brothers.
   He told me this a long time afterward, and he told it the way men tell things like
this, flat, as if it were a fact about the weather. "I didn't have anybody to call." And
then he said the sentence I hear more than any other from men who are being
honest. "I didn't think that was a problem until it was."

Most men have no brothers

  I need to say this straight, because the men it applies to almost never think it
applies to them.
   Most men have no brothers. They have buddies, men they do things with: hunt,
fish, golf, watch the game, work on trucks. They have coworkers, some of whom
they like. They have men at church whose hands they shake and whose names they

know. And if they're married they have a wife who is carrying the entire weight of
knowing them, which is a weight she was never meant to carry alone.
   Ask a man who knows his home exit, the anger or the itch or the drift we named
in chapter 9. Ask him who could call him at two in the morning and who he could
call. Ask him who's allowed to say "you're wrong" to his face and be heard. Ask him
who knows what he's actually afraid of. And then watch how many men, good
men, men with full contact lists, go quiet.
   We were not built to do this alone. That isn't a self-help line; it's the second
chapter of Genesis. The first thing God said was wrong with the world was that the
man was by himself. He fixed it with a wife, and that was the first remedy, but it
wasn't the only one He had in mind. All through Scripture, men are set in
companies. David had Jonathan and then thirty mighty men. Jesus had twelve, and
three inside the twelve, and He took those three with Him into the garden on the
night He needed them most. Paul never traveled alone if he could help it, and he
wrote most of his letters with a brother sitting next to him.

  Ecclesiastes 4:9-10 (NIV)
  Two are better than one, because they have a good return for their labor: If either of them falls down,
  one can help the other up. But pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up.



   Pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up. That was Walt in the
truck. He'd fallen, and he was reaching around in the dark, and there was no hand.

Why we don't have them

  I've asked a lot of men why they have no brothers, and here's what I've heard, in
more or less the order I hear it.
   They say they're fine. This is pride wearing a work shirt. You're not fine;
nobody's fine. The man who says "I'm fine" for thirty years is the man who ends up
in a bank parking lot with three hundred names.
   They say they don't have time, and they do; they simply haven't been asked for
the right amount, and I'll get to that.
  They say they don't want to be a burden. Under this is usually the empty chair. A
boy who learned that needing things was dangerous grows into a man who'd rather

go under than reach for a hand, and I understand it, and it's still killing him.
   They say men don't do that. But men did do that for most of history, and men in
Scripture did it constantly. We stopped about two generations ago, and we've been
dying quietly ever since and calling it independence.
   They say they've been burned. Some of you told a man something once and it
got around, or he used it, or he drifted off, and I'm sorry, and it happens. It's also
not a reason to never try again, any more than one bad meal is a reason to stop
eating.
   And they say they've got their wife, which is good, and I'd tell them to keep her.
But your wife is not your brother, and if she's the only one who knows you, you've
set a weight on her that will eventually bend her. She needs you to have a man. Ask
her, and she'll tell you.

What a brother is

  Let me define the word so you know what you're looking for. A brother is a man
who knows your exit. He knows whether you get angry or reach for the screen or go
quiet, and he asks about it, and you tell him the truth.
  A brother is a man who can ask you anything. Money, marriage, the bedroom,
what you looked at this week, whether you've prayed. Nothing is off the table,
because the two of you agreed that nothing would be.
  A brother is a man who will come. At two in the morning, to the hospital, to the
parking lot of the bank. Not because he has to, but because he's your brother. A
brother is a man who tells you the truth. Not the nice version but the true one, said
with love and said once, and then he lets you decide what to do with it.

  Proverbs 27:17 (NIV)
  As iron sharpens iron, so one person sharpens another.



  Sparks fly when iron sharpens iron. It isn't a comfortable picture. A brother is
going to say things that sting, and you'll be sharper for them.
  And a brother is a man who helps you find God when you can't. There's a line in
Samuel that I think is the best one-sentence description of brotherhood in the Bible.
David is on the run, hunted by the king, exhausted and hiding in the woods, and his


A Man With No Brothers                                                                   119
friend comes to find him.

  1 Samuel 23:16 (NIV)
  And Saul's son Jonathan went to David at Horesh and helped him find strength in God.



   Helped him find strength in God. Not gave him strength, and not fixed his
situation; Jonathan couldn't fix anything, since his own father was the one doing
the hunting. He came anyway, into the woods, and he helped his brother find what
only God could give. That is the job. That's what you need and that's what you're
for.
   Notice what a brother isn't. He isn't a crowd; you don't need a men's group of
forty, you need one man, maybe two. He isn't a counselor, though he might tell you
to see one. He isn't your boss or someone who reports to you. And he isn't
necessarily the man you have the most fun with. Some of your best hunting buddies
would make terrible brothers, and that's all right. Keep hunting with them, and find
a brother somewhere else.

Twenty minutes

  Here's the part I promised, and it may be the most practical thing in this book.
   The reason most men don't have a brother isn't that they don't want one. It's
that every picture of brotherhood they've been handed is too big: a weekly men's
breakfast at six in the morning, a small group with homework, a retreat, a weekend
away. Those things are good, and I've done all of them, but if you're a man with a
newborn or a business going under or a custody schedule or a wife with cancer, you
look at them and you know you can't, and so you do nothing.
   So here's a smaller picture. It's the one that finally worked for me and for most of
the men in this book, and I've never seen a season of life it couldn't survive.
    It's twenty minutes, with one man, once a week, at the same time. On the phone
is fine, and in a truck is fine, and walking is fine. It doesn't have to be face to face
and it doesn't have to be at a table. It has to be the same twenty minutes every week,
because a time that floats is a time that dies. Tuesday at 7:30 in the evening after the
kids are down. Thursday at noon on the drive to lunch. Sunday afternoon on the
porch. Pick it, and treat it like a client meeting you'd never think of canceling.

   And here's what you do with the twenty minutes. Three questions and a prayer.
The first question is, what happened this week? Two or three minutes each. Not a
report; the two or three things that actually mattered. The fight, the win, the call
from the doctor, the thing your son said.
    The second question is, what's heavy? This is the one that matters. What are you
carrying? What's the fear underneath the week? Where's your exit showing up? You
each answer, and you each tell the truth, and the other man does not fix it. He
listens, and he might ask one more question, and he does not fix it.
   The third question is, what's next? You each name one thing, one move before
next week: the conversation you're going to have, the thing you're going to stop, the
call you're going to make. Say it out loud so that a man heard you say it, because
next week the first question is going to be "did you do it?"
   Then two minutes of prayer, out loud, for each other. It'll be awkward for about
three weeks, and then it'll be the best part.
   That's the whole thing. Some weeks it runs thirty. Some weeks one of you is a
wreck and you spend all twenty on his "what's heavy," and that's fine, because that's
what it's for. But the floor is twenty minutes, once a week, at the same time, and the
floor doesn't move.
   Kenny's older man, the one who called him every Tuesday night in the first year
after his divorce, was doing exactly this. Three questions and a prayer. Kenny told
me he didn't realize until much later that the man had a method. He only knew that
every Tuesday at 7:30 his phone rang and a man wanted to know what was heavy,
and that for the first time in his adult life, somebody did.

Why twenty

  I want to defend the number, because some of you are thinking it's too small to
matter.
  Twenty minutes is small enough to survive anything. A newborn, tax season, a
twelve-hour shift, a custody weekend, chemotherapy, retirement, grief. I have never
met a man who honestly couldn't find twenty minutes a week. I've met a great
many who couldn't find two hours, and so found nothing.
  Twenty minutes with a structure does more than two hours without one. Most
men's gatherings drift toward sports and trucks and politics within eleven minutes


A Man With No Brothers                                                             121
because nobody's steering, and three questions steer. You'll go deeper in twenty
minutes with the questions than most men go in a year of breakfasts without them.
   And twenty minutes grows. Once two men have done this for six months, they
start doing other things. They show up at each other's kids' games. They help with
the move. They call on the off days. The twenty isn't the ceiling; it's the seed. But if
you try to plant the whole tree at once, you'll plant nothing.

How to find him

    This is where most men stall, so let me be specific about it. Look for a man who's
living the four marks, mostly. Not perfectly; nobody is. But you want a man who's
off the tailgate, who carries weight, who goes first sometimes, and who seems to be
working for something bigger than applause. You'll know him when you see him.
He's probably already in your church or your crew or your neighborhood, and
you've never asked him anything real.
  He can be your age or he can be older. Older is often better for this, and that's the
next chapter. But another man in your own season who's serious about being a man
makes a fine brother, and sometimes he's the only one available.
   He shouldn't be your boss, your employee, or your brother-in-law if you can
help it. You need to be able to tell him the whole truth without a job or a
Thanksgiving hanging on it.
  Then ask him, in plain words. Here's the sentence: "I'm trying to be a better man,
and I've realized I don't have a brother. Would you be willing to talk with me for
twenty minutes a week for the next three months and see how it goes?"
  Give it a time box of three months, because that makes it easy for him to say yes,
and easy for either of you to let it go if it isn't working. Most of the time it works
and nobody remembers to stop.
   Expect it to be awkward. For the first three weeks you'll both feel like you're
doing something strange, and you are. You're doing something men used to do and
stopped doing, and your body doesn't have the muscle memory yet. Push through
the awkwardness. It passes, and what's on the other side is worth it.
  And expect that he's been waiting too. Almost every man I've ever asked has said
some version of "I've been wanting somebody to ask me that." Men are lonely.
They're not going to go first. You're going to have to, which is chapter 6.

Brothers in every season

   If you're single, don't assume your roommates or your buddies are brothers,
because proximity isn't brotherhood. You might already have a brother and only
need to add the twenty minutes and the three questions. Or you might have a house
full of men and not one who knows you. Find out this week.
   If you've got a baby in the house, twenty minutes is all you've got, and that's
exactly why the number is twenty. Do it on the phone at nine at night while you do
the dishes. Your wife will bless you for it, because a man with a brother is a better
husband, and she'll see the difference inside a month.
   If you're divorced, your friends probably vanished. Couple friends do that; they
were mostly her friends, or they didn't know what to do with you. You need one
man more than any other man reading this does, so find him. If the church men's
group feels too big and too married right now, skip it for the moment and get one
man on the phone on Tuesday nights.
   And if you're older, the working years gave you a built-in company of men, and
when the work stopped, so did they. Roy said the first year of retirement was the
loneliest year of his life, and he'd worked alone in a patrol car for thirty years. He
built the Thursday breakfast table because he needed it, not out of generosity, and it
turned out a dozen other men needed it too. If you're a widower who lost the
couple friends along with your wife, the same is true of you. You need one man.
Mr. Pruitt found his in a retired schoolteacher who also drank his coffee alone, and
the two of them have met every Wednesday morning at a gas station for four years
now.
  Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this
most often leaves out.
   A young man of twenty-two has a house full of roommates and buddies and may
have no brother at all, for the reason I gave above. Eli realized this when he broke his
hand at work and couldn't think of one man among the dozen he saw every week
who'd know what to do with the fact that he was scared about the medical bills.
The takeaway for him is to take one of the dozen and add the twenty minutes and
the three questions, and to find out whether a buddy can become a brother.
   A man of fifty has three hundred contacts and no one to call, which is Walt in
the bank parking lot, and the takeaway for him is that the loneliest years of a man's

life are the middle ones, that this isn't his imagination, and that the cure is one
sentence to one man he's known for twenty years and never asked anything real.

The garden

   The night before He died, Jesus took three men into a garden. He knew they'd
fall asleep. He knew one of them would deny Him before sunrise. He took them
anyway, and He asked them for something.

  Mark 14:34 (NIV)
  'My soul is overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death,' he said to them. 'Stay here and keep
  watch.'



  Stay here and keep watch. The Son of God, who needed nothing, asked three
men to stay with Him in the worst hour of His life. If He didn't do it alone, you
don't get to either.
   Walt has a brother now. It took him a year to ask, and he asked a man from
church he'd known for twenty years and never once had a real conversation with.
They talk every Thursday at noon for twenty minutes on the drive to lunch. Walt
told me that for the first month he thought it was the dumbest thing he'd ever
agreed to. In the sixth month, when the business hit another rough patch, he made
a call from the same parking lot in Abilene, and this time somebody picked up.
   So find a brother, and give him twenty minutes at the same time every week.
Start with the sentence, and go first.

Before you turn the page

   I know the sentence I've asked you to say to another man feels like the most
awkward sentence in the English language. Every man I've ever asked to say it has
said the same thing. I also know that nearly every man who's said it has been
answered with some version of "I've been wanting somebody to ask me that,"
because men are lonely and they aren't going to go first. The Son of God took three
men into a garden on the worst night of His life and asked them to stay. May the
Father give you the nerve to ask one man for twenty minutes, and may He give you,
on the other end of that phone, a brother born for a time of adversity.
