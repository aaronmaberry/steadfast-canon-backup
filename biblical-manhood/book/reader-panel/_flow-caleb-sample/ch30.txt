CHAPTER 30



You Were Made for a Work
   Part 5: The long road
   There's a man in a town near me who runs the wastewater plant on the night
shift. Ten at night to six in the morning, four nights a week, for nineteen years.
Nobody in that town thinks about him. Nobody knows his name. Every one of
them flushes a toilet and turns on a tap and never once wonders who's awake at
three in the morning making sure the water that comes back to them won't kill
them.
   He's a believer, and he asked me once, after church, standing by his truck,
whether his work counted. He meant for God. He'd been sitting through years of
sermons about calling and ministry and the mission field, and he'd started to feel
that his real life was the twelve hours a week he spent at church, and that the forty
he spent at the plant were a kind of necessary evil, something he did to fund the part
that mattered.
   "I'm just running a plant," he said. "It's not like I'm doing anything for the
kingdom."
   I told him a whole town drinks clean water because he stays awake, and that God
had noticed before I did. But I've thought about his question for years, because
most men are asking it in one form or another. Does my work count? Is it part of
the thing, or is it the thing I do so that I can get to the thing?
   This is the fifth part of the book, the long road, and it starts here because most of
a man's road is work. Forty or fifty years of it. If your work doesn't count, most of
your life doesn't count, and I don't believe that, and neither does Scripture.

Work came before the curse

  We've been to Genesis 2:15 several times in this book, and I'm going there again,
because men keep forgetting it.
  To work it and take care of it, before the fall, before the serpent, before the
thorns, before sweat was a word in a curse. God made a man and gave him a job,
and the job was part of the good rather than a punishment added later. The curse in
chapter 3 didn't invent work. It made work hard: thorns, sweat, ground that fights
back. But the work itself is in the blueprint, and a man who thinks of his job as a
necessary evil has read Genesis 3 and skipped Genesis 2.
   Go higher than that, because God Himself works. The first thing the Bible shows
God doing is six days of labor followed by a day of rest. Jesus said it plainly when
the religious men accused Him of working on the wrong day.

  John 5:17 (NIV)
  In his defense Jesus said to them, 'My Father is always at his work to this very day, and I too am
  working.'



   My Father is always at his work. The God you serve isn't a God who sits. He
builds and tends and keeps and makes things grow, and He made you in His image,
which means among other things that He made you to work. That's why a man
who can't work feels as though he's coming apart. That's why a man laid off or
retired or disabled goes sideways in ways that aren't only about money. The image
in him is straining against a life with no garden.
   And Jesus worked, and not only for the three years. Most of His life on earth,
from boyhood to thirty, He spent in a carpenter's shop with tools in His hands,
making things for people who paid Him for them. Nobody wrote it down, because
it wasn't remarkable. It was a man working. The Son of God spent six times longer
building furniture than preaching, and He didn't consider the furniture years a
waste.
    So here's the plain answer to the man at the plant: yes, it counts. It isn't the thing
you do so you can get to the thing. It is the thing, or most of it, and the way you do
it is most of what God's purpose in your generation looks like.

What work is for

  Let me be specific, because "your work matters" can mean anything. Here are the

four things work is for in Scripture, and the three things it isn't.
   Work is for provision. You work so that your house has what it needs. We've said
this; it's a real weight and a man carries it, and a man who won't has denied the
faith. Paul was blunter than I'd dare to be.

  2 Thessalonians 3:10 (NIV)
  For even when we were with you, we gave you this rule: 'The one who is unwilling to work shall not
  eat.'



   Unwilling is the word, and not unable, because there's grace for the man who
can't. There's a rule for the man who won't. Work is for service. Your work is for
your neighbor. The plant operator's work is clean water for four thousand people.
The electrician's work is a house that doesn't burn. The teacher's work is a child
who can read. The trucker's work is groceries on the shelf. This is the piece men
miss most: your job is how you love your neighbor forty hours a week, and the
quality of the work is the quality of the love. Paul told thieves to work, and look at
the reason he gave.

  Ephesians 4:28 (NIV)
  Anyone who has been stealing must steal no longer, but must work, doing something useful with their
  own hands, that they may have something to share with those in need.



   Something useful, so that they may have something to share. Work turns a man
from a taker into a giver. That isn't a side effect. It's what work is for.
    Work is for making the world more than it is, which is Genesis 1:28. Subdue and
rule, which we said means cultivate: take the raw thing and bring it to order and
fruit. Every honest job does this. The mechanic takes a broken machine and makes
it run. The farmer takes dirt and makes food. The accountant takes chaos and
makes it legible. The nurse takes a body that's failing and holds it steady. You're
doing in small what God did in six days, and that's why a finished job feels the way
it does. It's the image showing.
  And work is for worship, which is Colossians 3:23. Whatever you do, work at it
with all your heart, as working for the Lord. The man at the plant is working for the
Lord at three in the morning whether or not anybody in the town knows it, and the
Lord knows it, and the Lord is the audience that matters. When you do a job with

all your heart because God is watching, you've turned the plant into a sanctuary.
That isn't a metaphor. That's the fourth mark applied to a time card.
   Now the three things work isn't. Work is not your identity. It's what you do and
not who you are. The man who is his job dies when the job does, and we'll come to
that. Your name is son, brother, husband, father, keeper of a garden. Your job is one
of the gardens.
   Work is not an escape, which chapters 5 and 24 have already said. Work claps and
home mostly doesn't, and a man can hide in a job and call it provision. If you're
staying late because the plant is easier than the kitchen, you've turned a good thing
into an exit.
   And work is not your salvation. You don't earn your standing with God by
working hard. You already have it, in Christ, and now you work because you're His
rather than in order to become His. A man who's trying to justify himself by his
output never rests and never finishes, because there's always more.

When you hate the job

  Some of you are reading this at a job you can't stand, and "work is worship"
sounds like a slap. Let me talk to you straight.
    The thorns are real, and Genesis 3 wasn't joking about them. There are bosses
who are fools, customers who lie, systems that grind, tasks that mean nothing to
anybody. Some of what makes work miserable is the curse, and the curse won't lift
until Jesus comes back, and pretending otherwise is a lie I won't tell you. But here's
what Solomon, who had every job and every luxury, concluded after looking hard at
all of it.

  Ecclesiastes 2:24 (NIV)
  A person can do nothing better than to eat and drink and find satisfaction in their own toil. This too,
  I see, is from the hand of God,



   Find satisfaction in their own toil, from the hand of God. Satisfaction in work is
a gift, and it's available in a great many more jobs than men think, and it comes
from three places. It comes from excellence. A man who does even a job he hates
with skill has something the job can't take from him.

  Proverbs 22:29 (NIV)
  Do you see someone skilled in their work? They will serve before kings; they will not serve before
  officials of low rank.



    Skilled in their work, whatever the work. Be the best one at it in the building, not
for the boss, but because skill is a form of dignity and God gave you hands to be
skilled with. It comes from people. The job is a garden, and the garden has people in
it, and those people are your assignment as much as the tasks are. The apprentice
who's watching how you handle a bad day. The coworker whose marriage is coming
apart. The customer nobody else is patient with. You may hate the work and love
the people, and the people may be why God has you there.
   And it comes from the audience. If you're working for the boss, the job is as good
as the boss. If you're working for the Lord, the job is as good as the Lord, and He's
watching a man do a thing well that nobody else values, and He counts it.
   If you should leave, leave like a man. Not on a Tuesday because you had a feeling;
chapter 20 covered that. With a plan, with your wife in the room, with the next
thing lined up or the savings to bridge it, with your reasons written down and
checked by a brother. Men leave jobs badly in two directions: they quit in a huff, or
they stay in a coffin. The man in the middle plans his exit and provides through it.

When you lose the job

  This one is different, and it's coming for more of you than you think, and
nobody prepares men for it.
   You get called in on a Thursday, and by Friday you're a man with no garden.
And the first thing that happens isn't about money. It's about who you are. Because
you've been introducing yourself by your job for twenty years, and now the
sentence has nothing after "I'm."
   Here is what a man does. Tell your wife the same day. Not after you've figured
out a plan; the same day. Men hide layoffs from their wives for weeks, and it's the
worst thing they can do, because she will find out, and what she'll learn is that you
hid. Tell her, and tell her you're scared if you're scared, and tell her you're going to
handle it.
   Tell your brother that week. The twenty minutes was built for this. "What's

heavy?" has an answer now.
   Treat the search as your work. Get up at the same time, shave, dress, and sit down
at eight and work at finding work until five. A man who treats unemployment as a
vacation drifts into the recliner and doesn't come out. A man who treats it as a job
stays a man.
   Take the bridge job. Paul made tents so that he could preach. If the plant closes
and the only thing available is driving a delivery truck at two-thirds the pay, drive
the truck. It's provision, it's service, it's making the world more than it is, and it's
worship if you do it for the Lord. It isn't beneath you. Nothing that feeds your
house is beneath you.
   And kill the shame. Chapter 15: guilt says you did a wrong thing; shame says you
are a wrong thing. Losing a job is neither. It's a thorn, and it happened to you. Your
Father saw it coming, and He isn't surprised and He isn't disappointed, and He's
the one who told you not to worry about what you'd eat, because He knows what
you need.

When your work is the house

   Some of you have work that doesn't look like work to anybody, including you.
You're home with the children because of how the math worked out, and you feel
like less of a man for it, and you shouldn't. You're disabled and can't do what you
used to. You're a caregiver: Mr. Alvarez bathing his wife every morning is doing the
second verb of Genesis 2:15, keeping what he was given, and it's the hardest work in
this book. If your garden is a house and a body that needs you, that's a garden.
Work it and keep it. The Father who sees in secret is watching a man do the
unglamorous thing with all his heart, and He doesn't grade on visibility.

After the badge

   Now the longest section, because the men past sixty have been under-served on
this by a century of Christian books.
   Scripture has no retirement, and I've looked. There is no verse where a man stops
working God's purpose because he reached a number. What there is, though, is a
shift, and it's in a passage most men have never read.

  Numbers 8:25-26 (NIV)
  but at the age of fifty, they must retire from their regular service and work no longer. They may assist
  their fellow Levites in performing their duties at the tent of meeting, but they themselves must not do
  the work. This, then, is how you are to assign the responsibilities of the Levites.



   Retire from their regular service, and then: they may assist. The Levites who did
the heavy lifting in the tabernacle stopped doing the heavy lifting at fifty, but they
didn't stop. They moved from carrying to assisting, from doing the work to helping
the ones who did. That's the biblical shape of retirement. Not the recliner; a change
of garden.
   Roy's first Tuesday after thirty-one years of wearing a badge was, in his words,
the worst day of his life, because nobody needed him and there was no assignment.
He'd been a man with a work since he was nineteen, and then he was a man with a
pension and a television and a wife who had built a life in the spaces he wasn't in.
   Here's what pulled him out, and it's a rhythm any retired man can build. He kept
getting up at 5:30. Not because he had to, but because a man who sleeps until he
feels like getting up will soon feel like getting up around ten, and a man who gets up
at ten is halfway to the drift, so the alarm stayed.
   He made the coffee, which was chapter 5, and then he sat with his wife while she
drank it, and that became the first work of every day: knowing her, after forty-four
years of not.
   He read, Mark first and then Proverbs, a chapter a day, out loud on the porch,
which was chapter 17 and the plumb line. For thirty-one years he'd let the badge be
his north star, and it was gone, and he needed the one that doesn't move.
    He gave himself a job every day. Not a hobby; a job, something he'd committed
to that somebody else was counting on: the north gate, the widow's gutters, the
church van, the grandson's oil change. "If I didn't have a job by breakfast," he said,
"I found one by lunch. A man with no job by noon is a man in a chair by two."
   He built the Thursday table, which was chapters 13 and 14, for two men in their
thirties who needed a man ahead of them. That became his main work, and he'll tell
you it saved his life. Retirement from the regular service, and assisting the ones still
carrying.
   He fished with his grandson on Tuesdays, giving hills the way chapter 28

described. And he stayed in church, not as an attender but as a man with a job there.

   That's a post-badge rhythm. Seven things, none of them a career, all of them

   If your health took your work before your age did, the shape is smaller but it's the
same, and the word is still assist. Mr. Alvarez, when he can get an hour away from
his wife's care, calls every widower in the church, one a day, and asks them what's
heavy. He does it from a chair at seventy-eight, and it's a work, and men are being
kept alive by it.
   The danger for the older man is the drift wearing a fishing license, and its cousin,
the anger that comes through the television. Both are what happens to a man who
was made for a work and stopped, so don't stop; shift instead. Carry less and assist
more. Declare it to the next generation, as the psalm says, until you're done.

Work in the other seasons

  If you're single, work is your main garden, and you should work it like a man
who intends to hand it to a wife someday: with excellence, with money handled,
with a reputation you'd be proud for her to hear about. And watch that you don't
make the work your wife. Men who marry their jobs at twenty-six have a hard time
un-marrying them at thirty-two.
   If there's a baby in your house, you're working exhausted, and the temptation to
stay an extra hour at the plant because it's quieter than the house is going to be real,
and you should name it for what it is. The floor from chapter 5 holds this year more
than any other. Provision is holy; disappearing isn't; and a wife with a newborn
knows the difference by 6:40.
   If you're divorced, the money is tighter than it has ever been: two households,
support, lawyers. You may need to work more. Do it without vanishing from the
children. The Tuesday call from the truck at 7:30 costs nothing, and it's the most
important work you'll do that week. And let your kids see a father who's steady at
his job. In a world that shook, a dad who goes to work every day and comes home
every night is ground they can stand on.

  Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this

   A young man of nineteen is often working a job he considers beneath his real life,
which is Eli at the parts counter, and the takeaway for him is that the counter is the

regular's name, and within a year the owner had made him the man who trains the

   A man of fifty has often let work become his identity, and he finds out what that
costs when the work is threatened, which was Walt in the bank parking lot. The
takeaway for him is that his name is son and husband and father and keeper of a
garden, and that his job is one of the gardens rather than the whole of him.

He finished the work

   Jesus said it in a prayer the night before He died.

  John 17:4 (NIV)
  I have brought you glory on earth by finishing the work you gave me to do.



   Finishing the work you gave me to do. That's the sentence every man wants to be
able to say at the end. Not "I had a great career," and not "I made a lot of money."
The work You gave me, I did, and I finished it, and it brought You glory. The
carpenter years and the preaching years and the cross, all one work, all finished.
   You were made for a work. It might be a plant at three in the morning. It might
be a classroom or a crew or a spreadsheet or a body that needs bathing or a
Thursday table or a north gate, and it's all Genesis 2:15. It all counts, and the way
you do it is most of what your life will have been.
   The man at the plant is still there. Nineteen years, and he told me last spring that
he'd started praying over the town while he ran his checks at three in the morning.
Four thousand people asleep, drinking his water in the morning, none of them
knowing that a man they've never met asked God to keep them while he kept the
plant.
   That counts, and so does yours. Go do your work.

   If you've spent years thinking your work didn't count for God, I hope you'll
drive to it tomorrow with a different picture in your head: the Son of God at a
workbench for thirty years, making things for people who paid Him, and not one
hour of it wasted. Your plant, your classroom, your crew, your spreadsheet, your
kitchen, your chair are all Genesis 2:15. And if the work was taken from you, by a
layoff or a body that quit or a birthday, you weren't let go; you were reassigned.
May the Father who is always at His work give you a job by noon tomorrow that
somebody is counting on, and may you do it with all your heart, as for Him, and
find that it was the thing all along.

  DO THIS WEEK
  Because work becomes worship one ordinary task at a time: do one task at your job
  with all your heart, as for the Lord, and tell no one; if you're between jobs, treat the

  Because the man who's using work as an exit rarely knows it until someone asks: tell
