Introduction
   Most of the hours that have mattered in my adult life have been spent across a
table from a man. A kitchen table. A folding table at the back of a fellowship hall.
The cab of a truck with the engine off. A diner counter where the waitress already
has the coffee poured because she has seen you on this stool before. Some of those
men could have been my sons. Some could have been my father. Under the different
jobs and the different decades they were asking the same three things. What is a man.
Am I one. If I am not, is it too late.
    This book is the long answer I give when the coffee has gone cold. It is not the
only answer on the shelf. It is the one I have watched hold up in ordinary houses,
across a lot of years, and I have tried to write it the way I would say it if you were
sitting here.

Who I'm writing to
   Every season is already in the room. Find your seat.
   You may be single, still waiting for somebody to call you a man out loud. You
may be newly married, or so deep in the little-years that nobody in the house has
slept a full night since March. You may be providing everything except yourself.
Divorced, and still a father. Alone in a quiet house after the kids left. Retired,
wondering what a man does with a morning that has no clock-in. Standing in a
kitchen where her cup is still in the drainer. You are not a guest chapter. You are who
this book is for.
   I am not writing only to the man with the mortgage and the minivan. When the
counsel has to change with the season, I will turn toward you inside the paragraph
and say so. I will not stop every chapter to run the roll call again. Every Season Still
Counts, near the end, is where we sit down with the seasons on purpose. Until then,
trust that I know you are here. If the house is loud and the page count looks
impossible, start with A Quiet Tuesday, keep the closer each week, and come back
for the rest when you can. The book will keep.

What I'm claiming
   One sentence sits in the middle of this book. I did not invent it, and I will not
pretend I did. Robert Lewis, at Fellowship Bible Church in Little Rock, gave it this
shape years ago, in the teaching that became Raising a Modern-Day Knight. Men
have been handing it to one another ever since. I have held it up against my own life
for a long time, and against the lives of men in every season I just named, and I have
not found a place where it lies.
   A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects
the greater reward, God's reward.
    Those are the four marks. The Four Marks will open them, and the chapters after
it take them one at a time. For now, notice what kind of sentence it is. It is not a
sentence about what a man feels. There is a whole shelf of books that start inside the
chest, with longing and wild places, and some of those books were good to me when
I was young. I am not writing against them. This book makes a plainer claim. The
four marks are things you do, on ordinary days, and they show. They mark a man the
way thirty years of work marks his hands. You can see them. You can see their
absence. No amount of feeling covers either one.

How the book is built
   We start with an ordinary day, because that is where a man's life gets decided. A
Quiet Tuesday names the problem. Then we tell the truth about the room you are
standing in, and we put the four marks in your hands until you can say them
without looking.
   From there we go back to what you inherited and what is still yours. The chair at
the end of your childhood table. The anger, the itch, and the drift. The Father who
does not leave. The handoff you may never have received. Leaving home without
slamming the door. Brothers. The ask. The problem inside you, which would still be
there if your father had been perfect. Then further back, to the beginning of the
human story, so you quit reading your life as if you invented it. Then the house. A
wife, if there is one, and a life with a cause if there is not. The bed. A daughter. A
son. Then the long road. Work, feelings, seasons, the years nobody claps for, a page
you write, and the men who stay.
  Running through all of it is Tuesday. Not the altar. Not the retreat. The day with
nothing special on it, when nobody is watching. Every chapter lands, somewhere, on

A Quiet Tuesday
  If you had asked me at twenty-five which days would decide the man I became, I
would have given you the big ones. The wedding. The morning the first child came.
A funeral. The job I took and the job I turned down. The Saturday a pastor put me
under in a river and I came up sure.
  I have had those days now. They are stones on a bank I can point at and say, that
was the year. The deciding happened somewhere else, in a long run of days I could
not tell apart if you paid me.
   A man's life is decided on Tuesdays. Not on the Sunday when he knows how to
stand. Not on the Saturday when he takes his son somewhere and feels like a father
for an afternoon. On the day in the middle, with nothing special on it. The day
nobody writes down. He comes home tired. The house is already loud. There is a
sentence he could say or swallow, a call he could return or let die, a battery he could
change or step under one more week. That is where the marks get made.

What a Tuesday looks like
  So here is one.
   A man I will call Anthony left the house at 6:10 on a Tuesday in October. He had
slept badly. His wife had said something the night before about money that was not
quite fair, and he had let it sit. It rode in the truck with him.
  At the shop a customer's truck was promised by lunch. The parts ticket was still
on the bench where he had set it, meaning to call it in. He had not. The fuel pump
was not coming. He had to push the job while the shop lead listened from two bays
over and said nothing. At lunch he sat in the cab with his phone for forty minutes.
Afterward he could not have named one thing he had read.
   His father called in the afternoon. The man calls maybe twice a year. The name
on the screen still makes Anthony feel nine for a second. He let it go to voicemail and
told himself later.
  He finished the work. He drove home with the radio off, pulled into the garage,
and stood in the doorway the way he does most nights. Then he walked in. He kissed


PART ONE                                                                                  9
her on the side of the head without looking at her. He ate. He got the kids down. By
8:40 he was in the recliner with the game on and the phone in his hand. She was in
the kitchen. The money stayed where he had left it.
   The thing he let sit was a decision. The part he did not order was a decision. The
phone at 8:40, while his wife stood alone in the kitchen, was a decision. None of
them felt like one. They felt like a day. Give a man ten thousand days like that and he
becomes a particular man without ever standing up and choosing it.
  Anthony is a good man. He did not set out to wound anybody on that Tuesday.
He worked, he provided, he came home, he was gentle enough. He is also becoming
something, one ordinary day at a time. Ask his wife what, and she could tell you.

Why the big days can't do it
  I have stopped trusting the big days to make men. A big day is a burst. It gathers
what you feel and what you hope and sets it on a table in front of witnesses. God
builds days like that on purpose. Later in this book I will tell you to make one for
your son. A burst is still not a shape. A mark is what repeated work leaves behind, the
way a callus comes from a thousand mornings nobody photographed.
  Men come home from a conference on fire and are back in the recliner inside two
weeks. They spent the burst and skipped the shape.
   On the big days you are watched. A wedding, a baptism, a funeral. You know the
part, and most men play it. What tells you about a man is what he does when the
room is empty, when nobody would know if he made the call or left it, opened the
drawer or left it shut, said the true thing or let it sit. Tuesday is when nobody is
watching. That is why it counts.

What God does with Tuesdays
   If you have been in church a while, you may have filed the spiritual life under
retreats and altars. God does meet men there. I am not arguing with that. Look at
how the Bible actually talks about the way a man is formed.

     Luke 16:10 (NIV)
     "Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with
     very little will also be dishonest with much."

   Very little is a Tuesday phrase. The man who can be trusted in the small
unwatched things is the man who can be trusted when the room fills up. The
recliner, the voicemail, the kiss that does not look her in the face. Those are the shop
where the man who will one day stand in a hard room gets made, or does not.
   When Jesus tells about a house that held, notice what He says the man did.

  Matthew 7:24 (NIV)
  "Therefore everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man who
  built his house on the rock."



   Puts them into practice. Practice is what you do again until it is how you do
things. The wise man laid courses on days nobody drove by to admire. When the
rain came, the house held because of those days.
   Most of a man's walk with God is Tuesday. Most of his marriage is Tuesday. Most
of his fathering is Tuesday. If you want to know what you are becoming, leave the
retreat out of it. Look at last Tuesday.

The other Tuesday
  Here is Anthony's day again, a year later. I am not describing a trap. I am
describing a place where a man has more say than he thinks.
   He left at 6:10 again. He had slept badly again. Somewhere on the drive he said,
out loud, "Father, I don't want to haul this all day." He pulled into a gas station and
sent his wife a text. He was sorry he had gone quiet. He wanted ten minutes after the
kids were down. Ninety seconds. He was still tired. The thing was no longer riding
with him.
   The part for the lunch job was on the shelf, because he had called it in the day
before. When a different job went sideways anyway, he told the shop lead the truth
and called the customer before anybody had to overhear a scramble. His father
called. Anthony felt nine for a second. Then he answered. Six awkward minutes
about nothing. The first time in two years he had not let it die in the voicemail.
   At home he caught himself standing in the doorway and walked through it. He
put the phone on the counter, face down. He looked at his wife when he kissed her.
After the kids were down they sat at the counter for ten minutes and talked about
the money. He wanted to win a point and he laid the point down. He did the dishes.



A Quiet Tuesday                                                                                          11
He prayed, badly and briefly, with his hand on her back. Then he slept.
   His daughter is seven. She watches his face when her mother walks in. She could
not have told you what changed. Something had, in about six places, each of them
under two minutes. Give that man ten thousand of those Tuesdays and you get a
different girl learning what a husband's face does.
   That is the claim of this book. Know what a man does on a plain day, then do it
again.

The same Tuesday at every age
  A Tuesday does not look the same at nineteen as it does at seventy. It decides the
same kind of thing. Find yourself in one of these four before we go on.
  Eli is nineteen. He works the counter at a parts store and lives with two
roommates. Around ten the apartment goes quiet and the phone is in his hand.
What he does in that pocket of quiet is writing the man he will be at thirty. He told
me once he was waiting for his real life to start. His real life is the ten o'clock.
   Tomás is twenty-nine. You will meet him again. The baby does not sleep. At two
in the morning he hears the crying and hears his wife start to move, and lying still
feels like the only sane choice a tired man has. Whether he gets up is the marriage.
Not the trip they cannot afford. The two o'clock.
   Walt is fifty-five. He built a business from one truck to eleven. At 6:15 he can
leave the shop and make dinner, or stay another hour because the shop is quieter
than the house. He stayed for twenty years, an hour here and an hour there, and
never once held a meeting about it. The staying decided itself.
    Roy is seventy-two. He turned in a state trooper's badge after thirty-one years. At
5:30 the alarm goes off and there is nothing he has to pin on. Nobody would notice
if he slept until nine. What he does at 5:30 is deciding whether the last stretch of his
life is a long exit or the most useful years he has had. He gets up and makes the
coffee. You will see why that matters.
  Four men. Four hours that do not feel like decisions. That is why the book starts
here.

Before you turn the page
  I do not know which of those four you are, or whether you are somewhere

moment, probably several, where you will choose without feeling the choice. I am
not asking you to be a different man by Sunday. I am asking you to see the moment
once, and to do one small thing in it on purpose. The Father who made you sees that
moment. He is glad when you meet it. May He give you eyes for a plain day, and the
small strength it takes to walk through a doorway instead of standing in it.

CHAPTER 2



You Are Not Crazy
   For years I treated a pile of slogans as if they were a plan. Strong, then soft. In
charge, then out of the way. The drive in me was dangerous, until the day I failed to
use it, and then that failure was why the house had gone cold. I could quote the pile.
I could not steer a week by it. A man in the middle of his working years took the
booth across from me at a diner and described the same pile. Six manuals for
manhood. Each one wiped out the other five. He was supposed to live as though
they formed a single set of directions.
    What sat on him was weariness. The target kept moving, and he was still being
scored. Somebody had called manhood a costume he could hang up. Somebody else
had called it the central fact of him. A third voice told him to invent the meaning
himself. A fourth said the meaning had been fixed for ages and he was simply bad at
it. In church he had been handled as a problem the room needed to manage. In
locker rooms he had been handled as an appetite the room needed to feed. Stay in
that long enough and a man asks whether the fog rolled in from outside or whether
he carried it in his own coat.
  I told him the line I am telling you. The confusion lives out in the world. The gap
you feel is a real one.

The picture you were handed
   The picture of manhood on offer to men right now will not hold still in one
piece. We have spent years clamping the pieces together, then blaming our hands
when they spring apart. I want the picture where you can look at it. A map that was
never a map should come off your bill.
   Begin at the table where you grew up. The Empty Chair takes that room apart
later in the book. Here, the point is smaller. The person at the head of that table
taught you manhood before letters made any sense to you. Most of us received the
lesson in broken pieces. A father present and wordless. A father already absent. A
father who entered like a storm. Even a faithful father left gaps. You left home
holding a fragment, and you went looking for the rest.
   Sellers were happy to supply pieces, and none of the pieces agreed. The culture


A Quiet Tuesday                                                                       15
around you filed manhood under problems. Building, competing, protecting, taking
hold of a room, all of that got described as an old instinct the species should have
dropped. The same culture then ridiculed men who stayed boys, who would not
promise anything, who refused to grow. It never owned the order it had given.
   Screens offered a fool, a brute, or a hero who answered to nobody. The fool was
the sitcom father, lost inside his own kitchen. The brute was the villain, and some
nights the person a boy was coached to admire. The hero was the private movie every
boy ran. No worn-out wife appears in it. No father phones twice in a year. No son
goes silent. Church, brothers, and a sink full of Tuesday dishes are all missing. He
keeps a life with no address. Boys were schooled to covet that life from inside an
actual house.
   The marketplace said a man is his wage. The internet said a man is his watch
history. Certain churches handed out "spiritual leader" in a form so vague that
Wednesday still had no instructions. Other churches handed out warrior and king at
a volume that left no chair for a payment book, a mother in a hospital bed, or third
shift at the plant. Books aimed at men often start in the feelings and never come out.
Feelings have a place. They do not score a life. Action does.
   Put the fragment from home next to those manuals and you have the kit we have
been navigating with. One picture was never in the box. Six maps were, and no two
of them share a north. Carrying all six is easy. Walking by all six is impossible. Every
new heading erases the last. The man who keeps at it gets dizzy and signs his own

into the book, the naming done, the doing done, a brother told, you belong with
them whether our paths cross or not. A garden behind us. A table not set yet, ahead.
The four marks make that company. Ordinary. Steadfast. Same men.

The last time
   Once more, and then I am out of the way. A real man rejects passivity, accepts
responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.
   You can see the four on a man the way the fence stretcher showed on my

   Failure is coming. The question is the man you are standing in, the first or the

An ordinary day
   Ordinary days decide a life. Altars do not. Fence lines do not. Last pages do not. A
gray day, a little wind, nobody looking, and the stake seems to be nothing, which
means it is everything.
   Tomorrow is one. A smoke detector chirps in a hallway near you. A woman needs
to be known. A child needs a name and a hill. A father needs the phone to ring. A
brother needs the ask. A Father is on the road, watching for you. You already know
the move. What remains is getting up. The old priests left the people with words
God had given them. I will do the same.

Work it. Keep it. My brothers are these men. The table has an open chair. Come take

About the Author
   Wes Hart is a husband, a father of two, and a Texan. His working hours go to
leading teams in technology. His best hours go to teaching men what it means to be
steadfast.
   He did not set out to write a book or to run a men's training. He set out to stop
being the passive husband and distracted dad he had quietly become. A handful of
older men helped him by keeping a chair for him at their table every week. Years later
he started keeping chairs for other men. Steadfast Men, the 24-week training this
book grew out of, began at a kitchen table with a few skeptical guys. It has since been
taught in living rooms, garages, church classrooms, and online, to men in every
season.
   Wes is not a pastor and he is not a counselor, and he will tell you so before you
ask. He is a layman who believes ordinary men, walking together under the Word of
God, can become the husbands, fathers, and leaders their families and churches are
hungry for. He has watched it happen too many times to doubt it.
  When he is not working or teaching, he is usually outside with his kids, on a long
walk with his wife, or at a diner early on a weekday with a Bible, a cup of coffee, and
one other man.
   He and his family live in Texas.
