# 9. Anger, the Itch, and the Drift

Owen took all three exits in one week, and he'd have told you he has a temper and left it there.

The first night, his daughter loaded the dishwasher wrong and he slammed the door hard enough to knock a glass off the top rack, and the kitchen went quiet the way his kitchen has learned to go quiet. Two nights later he was in the bathroom with the door locked and his phone in his hand for forty minutes, and nobody in the house needed anything from him in there, which was the point. By the weekend he was on the couch with a game on, and when his wife asked what he thought about her sister taking the kids in July he said "whatever you think" and meant it, because he wasn't in the room.

Three nights, three exits. Owen would call them three bad moods. They weren't. Under all three sat one thing he hadn't told anyone: the contract he'd counted on for the fall had gone to somebody else, he'd run the numbers, and he was scared.

## Where pain goes

The chair at the end of the table never stays empty; something always sits down in it. Pain doesn't sit still in a man. It has to go somewhere, and it nearly always goes one of three directions.

It goes out, as anger. It goes down, as appetite. Or it goes away, as absence. Every man has a home exit, the one he takes when things get heavy, and most men think it's their personality. It isn't. It's the way a boy learned not to feel something, worn into a habit that answers to his name. Block the home exit and the pain finds the next one, which is how Owen took three in a week.

I won't soften any of them, and I won't shame you for any of them; shame is fuel for all three.

## Anger

Anger is the exit that looks like strength, which is exactly why men like it.

Get underneath a man's anger and you nearly always find fear, shame, or helplessness: the money won't work, he's ashamed of something, he's watching something he loves go wrong and can't stop it. Every one of those feels like weakness, and anger is the one feeling that feels like power, so he grabs it the way a cold man grabs the nearest coat. The dishwasher didn't make Owen slam that door. The numbers did.

Scripture doesn't tell you not to be angry; it tells you what to do with it.

:::margin
Ephesians 4:26-27 (NIV)
"'In your anger do not sin': Do not let the sun go down while you are still angry, and do not give the devil a foothold."
:::

In your anger, it says. Then a deadline, sundown, and a warning: this is where the enemy gets a foot inside your house. Through the anger you carried to bed and woke up with and carried to bed again, until it was the weather in your home.

If anger is your exit, here's what's yours. Learn your ninety seconds, because anger in the body crests and starts to fall inside two minutes if you don't feed it. Walk to the mailbox and back. Don't say the sentence you've got loaded. Then come inside and say what's under it: "I'm scared about the money." Your wife can work with a scared man. She can't work with a storm. And don't let the sun go down on it. A man who masters this doesn't go soft. He becomes the strongest man in the room.

## The itch

The second exit goes down, into appetite. Most of this is about the sexual kind, which has the most men by the throat, but it applies to the bottle, the cart, the sports book, and the endless scroll. It's all one itch: comfort now, at no cost, without having to be known.

Owen, locked in the bathroom with his phone, wasn't chasing pleasure, mostly. He was chasing relief, from the numbers and the quiet kitchen. The screen asked nothing of him. It didn't need him to be a man. That's the pull, and that's the lie, because being needed as a man is the very thing that would heal him.

:::margin
James 1:14-15 (NIV)
"but each person is tempted when they are dragged away by their own evil desire and enticed. Then, after desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, gives birth to death."
:::

Dragged away is the feeling. Nobody plans to be up at two in the morning. And the road ends in death, rarely a dramatic one: the slow death of a man's ability to be present, to want a real woman, to look his wife in the eye. She knows something is off even when she can't say what.

Flee, Paul tells the Corinthians. Not manage, not moderate. Flee, the way Joseph ran from a house with a woman in it who wanted him. If the itch is your exit, here's what's yours. Tell one man the whole truth: not "I struggle with purity" but what, how often, since when, and what it's costing. The itch lives on secrecy and starts to die the day a brother knows. Cut the supply: filters on every device, the password in that brother's hands, the phone charging in the kitchen overnight. A child's rule, yes, for a man acting like one at two in the morning. And deal with the loneliness, because that's what this is. There's a whole chapter on brothers later in this book, and its first line belongs here: the itch shrinks in men who are known.

## The drift

The third exit is the quiet one, and I worry about it most in men past their working years, because nobody notices it and nothing seems wrong.

The drift is a man leaving without moving. He's in the house, he goes to work, he hasn't blown up and he hasn't blown it. He's just not there. His wife quit asking him things because the answers were "fine" and "whatever you think," and his kids learned early that Dad's body was on the couch and Dad was elsewhere. The drift is how a man becomes the full chair with nobody in it, the one some of you grew up staring at.

Under it is usually a man who got hurt and decided, without saying so, that caring cost too much, or a man who got tired, honestly tired, and let rest turn into retreat.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 10:18 (NIV)
"Through laziness, the rafters sag; because of idle hands, the house leaks."
:::

Nobody kicked a hole in that roof. It leaked because the man who should have been on the ladder was on the couch, and by the time anybody names the drift the ceiling is stained.

If the drift is your exit, here's what's yours. Name it to your wife: "I've been in the house and I've been gone, and I'm sorry." She knows, and hearing you say it will change something. Take one thing back: one evening a week with the screens dark and your attention in the room, one question a day you actually wait for the answer to. And go find the man who decided caring wasn't worth it, with a brother or a counselor to help you look. That isn't weakness. That's a man on the ladder.

## The same root

The angry man is a boy who learned that being scared got him hurt, so he traded fear for something that felt like power. The itching man is a boy who learned nobody was going to see him, so he found comfort that didn't require being known. The drifting man is a boy who learned that caring cost too much, so he quit. Every exit is a way not to feel the chair, and every one leads out of the house.

Jesus took none of them. He got angry and never sinned in it; He was tempted in every way we are and didn't go down; and when everything in Him wanted another road He said "not as I will," and stayed. So when you take an exit, there's a Man who can find you in the ditch and walk you back without shame.

You've tried just stopping. Men don't get free by clenching harder; they take the pain somewhere it can be healed instead of numbed. That's where we go next. There is a Father who does not leave, and He isn't afraid of your anger, isn't disgusted by your itch, and hasn't drifted from you while you drifted from Him.

## The exits in every season

The exits wear different clothes in different seasons. If you're young and single, the itch is probably loudest, but watch for the drift too: a job you don't care about, a screen that eats your evenings. Numbness at that age is a warning light, not peace. If there's a baby in the house, anger will surprise you: three in the morning, the crying won't stop, and a voice comes out of you that you last heard from your father. Ninety seconds, then the true thing: "I'm so tired I'm scared of myself." The divorced seat may take all three exits at once in the first year or two: anger at her, the itch because you're alone and you weren't, the drift because caring is what got you here. What pulls a man back is usually one older man who calls every week and won't accept "fine." And if you're older, watch the drift and the anger together, because they travel as a pair and usually arrive by television. Be up before she is with the coffee made, sit with her while she drinks it, and ask one question you wait for.

## Owen's mailbox

Owen told his wife about the numbers on a night with nothing on. He said he'd been scared for three weeks and had taken it to the dishwasher and the bathroom and the couch instead of to her. She said she'd known something was wrong. She could handle scared. She couldn't handle gone.

The next time the kitchen started to go quiet, he walked to the mailbox and back, came inside, and said the true sentence. It didn't fix the fall. It fixed the kitchen.

## Before you turn the page

Your exit has a name now, and a thing with a name can be carried to the Father. May He meet you at the mailbox, the screen, and the couch, and give you one small refusal this week that turns into a habit.
