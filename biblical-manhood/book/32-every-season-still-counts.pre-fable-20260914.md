# 32. Every Season Still Counts

A cotton field in February looks dead. Bare dirt, stubble, a few tumbleweeds caught on the fence. A man who didn't know farming would drive past it and think nothing was happening.

Everything is happening, though. The ground is resting, and the stubble is going back into the soil. The moisture from the winter rains is working its way down to where the roots will need it in July. The farmer is fixing equipment, planning rows, watching the market, praying about rain. February isn't the off-season. It's a season with its own job, and a farmer who tried to skip it, who plowed and planted in February because he couldn't stand the waiting, would lose the whole year. Solomon said it plainer than I can.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 3:1 (NIV)
"There is a time for everything, and a season for every activity under the heavens:"
:::

A season for every activity. Not one season that counts and a great deal of waiting around it. Every season has an activity. Every season has an assignment.

I told you in chapter 3 that I wasn't going to stop every chapter and sort you by age, and I've tried to keep that promise, turning toward you inside the paragraphs when your season changed the counsel. This is the chapter where we do it on purpose: all the seasons, in one place, each with its job, because the culture has taught men to rank them, and the ranking is a lie that costs men decades.

## The lie of the peak

Here's the ranking most men carry without knowing they carry it. The twenties are for freedom. The thirties are for building. The forties are the peak. The fifties are for coasting. Sixty and beyond is done, a long slow exit with golf in it. And any season that doesn't fit the arc, divorce or illness or a wife dying, is a detour to get through so that you can get back on the arc.

Scripture doesn't know this arc. Scripture knows a man who serves God's purpose in his own generation, and his generation includes every season he lives through, and every season has a job, and none of them is a detour. Moses started his real work at eighty. David was anointed as a boy and did his best writing as an old man. Caleb asked for a hill country at eighty-five.

:::margin
Joshua 14:10-12 (NIV)
"So here I am today, eighty-five years old! I am still as strong today as the day Moses sent me out; I'm just as vigorous to go out to battle now as I was then. Now give me this hill country that the LORD promised me that day."
:::

Give me this hill country. That's an eighty-five-year-old man asking for the hardest assignment on the map, because his season still counted and he knew it. So let me walk the seasons with you. For each one I'll tell you what it's for, what it's tempted by, what its assignment is, and which man from this book is standing in it. You'll find yours, and you may find that you're standing in two at once, because seasons overlap, and that's all right. God's purpose in your generation is God's purpose in your season, whichever one it is.

## The young single man

This is the season of strength and open hours, and it's the most wasted season in most men's lives.

Micah stood in it for six years at the back table, and he told you in chapter 21 that he wasted the first three of them waiting. That's the temptation: to treat this season as the lobby, to wait for a wife or a career or a calling or a feeling before you start. And the other temptation, the one that fills the waiting, is the itch, because a young man with open hours and no one watching has more exposure to that exit than he'll ever have again.

The assignment is to build. Chapter 21 gave you the list: a house, a table, a company of brothers, a devotion, a fatherhood toward somebody else's fatherless boy, a purity. Paul told a young man in this season not to let anyone discount him for it.

:::margin
1 Timothy 4:12 (NIV)
"Don't let anyone look down on you because you are young, but set an example for the believers in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith and in purity."
:::

Set an example, Paul says, and he doesn't say to prepare to set one when you're older; he means now. And Solomon, looking back from old age, gave the young man one instruction above all the rest.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 12:1 (NIV)
"Remember your Creator in the days of your youth, before the days of trouble come and the years approach when you will say, 'I find no pleasure in them'"
:::

Remember your Creator in the days of your youth. The men who find the Father at twenty-five are spared thirty years of scanning the bleachers. This season counts, not as a warm-up, but because the man you build now is the man every later season gets.

## The first year of marriage, and the first year with a baby

Nobody warns you about this one. The wedding gets the attention, and then two people who were dating are suddenly one flesh in one apartment with one bank account, and the season that decides the whole marriage begins the day after the honeymoon, when everyone has stopped watching.

The temptation is to believe the wedding did the work, and it didn't. The wedding was the marker; the Tuesdays are the marriage, and the first year of Tuesdays sets the floor for the next forty. The assignment is chapters 22, 23, and 24, run hard: know her, because you don't yet, whatever you think; lay it down, every preference you've never had to lay down before; find out what she actually needs; and keep the twenty minutes with your brother going, because a newly married man who drops his brothers has handed his wife a weight she can't carry.

And if a baby comes in that first year or two, you're in a season inside a season, and it's the one that breaks more young marriages than any other, and it's the one the church talks about least.

Tomás's daughter was four months old when we first met him in these pages, and here is what he'd tell you. The marriage takes the hit when the baby comes. Not because either of you did anything wrong, but because a third person who needs everything has moved in, and the two of you are running on fumes, and every gauge on both your dashboards is pegged, and you've stopped talking about anything but logistics, and the bed has gone quiet, and you're both a little afraid this is what it's going to be now.

It isn't going to be this forever. But it's what it is this year, and the assignment in this season is to protect the marriage inside the chaos rather than after it. Ten minutes at the kitchen counter after the baby's down, phones face down, is the season's whole liturgy. Chapter 4 said it: that isn't waiting for a better season. It is the season. Lay down sleep and take a shift. Know her postpartum body and heart the way chapter 22 described, and watch for the darkness that has a name. Keep the floor from chapter 5 even when the job is hard and the house is harder. Don't evaluate your marriage at three in the morning; chapter 31. And keep your brother. Twenty minutes on the phone at nine while you do the dishes is what brotherhood looks like this year, and it's enough.

This season counts, and not as survival until the real marriage starts. The floor you pour in this year, exhausted and unthanked, is the floor the marriage will stand on when the child is sixteen. Tomás said, at the end of his first year, "We didn't have a great year. We had a year where I found out I could be a man when I was too tired to be anything, and she found out she could count on me. I think that's better than a great year."

## The middle years

Thirty-five to fifty-five, children at home, career at full throttle, the mortgage, the schedule. This is the season the culture calls the peak, and it's the season where most men lose the house while building the career.

Anthony is in it, standing in a doorway between a garage and a kitchen. Walt was in it when he built eleven trucks and disappeared. The temptations here are the strongest and the best disguised: disappearing into provision, the drift that looks like being tired, the scoreboard against every other man at church, and, for a good many men in their forties, the itch coming back with a middle-aged face, when the marriage has gone quiet and a woman at work laughs at your jokes.

The assignment is presence, and it's harder here than anywhere. It means the floor held, and the marriage tended: date her, pursue her, know her again, because the woman you married at twenty-six is gone and the one who's here needs studying. Brothers, because the middle years are the loneliest of a man's life; men between forty and fifty-five have fewer real friends than at any other age, and the men with none are the ones who end up in bank parking lots. Hills for your sons and daughters, and the day, chapters 28 and 29. And a hard look at what you're actually working for, because the fourth mark gets tested in this season more than in any other. If the reward you're chasing is the scoreboard, you'll win it and lose everything that mattered.

This season counts, but not the way the culture says. It isn't your peak. It's the season where you find out whether you're a corner post or a line post, and your children are watching to find out too.

## The divorced man

Nobody plans this season, and Kenny certainly didn't. And when it comes, most men treat it as a detour, a thing to get through so they can get back to the real story, and that's the first mistake, because it isn't a detour. It's a season with a job, and it lasts longer than anyone tells you.

Here's the shape I've watched. The first year or two is survival. Grief that comes in waves at the exchange and on Sunday nights. Anger at her, at God, at yourself. The itch, hard, because you're alone and you weren't. The drift, because caring is what got you here. Money you don't have. A custody schedule that runs your life. A church that doesn't know what to do with you. The temptation in this stretch is to make a turn: move, marry, quit, leave the church, anything to make the gauges stop screaming. Don't make the turn; chapter 31 told you why. Drive straight and slow.

The assignment in the survival years is short and hard. Grieve out loud, and own your part without attaching hers. Children first, before any new woman and before your own comfort. One brother, on the phone, every Tuesday. The Tuesday call to your kids, even when they say nothing. Stay in the house of God even when the house is awkward about you.

Then, somewhere in years three to five, something shifts, and the gauges settle. You've built a house, a real one, with your children's drawings on the refrigerator. You have a smoker you built across eleven weekends. You've become the man in your church that the other divorced men will talk to. And you begin to see what God was doing, which is what Kenny saw: a purpose he never would have chosen, served with the very thing he thought disqualified him. Peter, after the worst failure of his life, was told: when you have turned back, strengthen your brothers.

I'm not going to promise you remarriage. That's between you and God and your pastor and a woman who may or may not come. I'm going to promise you that this season counts. That the Father who does not leave didn't leave in the courtroom. That a man who walks through a divorce the second Man's way, covering his children, owning his part, refusing the exits, comes out of it a man other men will follow. And that your children, who are watching how a man handles the worst thing, are learning something they'll need.

## The empty nest

The last one leaves for college or the Army or an apartment across town, and the house goes quiet, and two people who've spent twenty years talking about children look at each other across a kitchen and aren't sure what's left.

The temptation is two strangers in a house. The drift, because the thing you organized your life around is gone. And the affair, because a fifty-two-year-old man who feels invisible at home and visible somewhere else is in real danger.

The assignment is to court her again, and I don't mean it as a figure of speech. Ask her out, and find out who she is now; chapter 22 at fifty-two. Make plans for the years that are left that are hers as much as yours. Walt did this after he sold off part of the business, and he said the empty nest turned out to be the best stretch of their marriage, "because for the first time since 1994 it was just us and I actually showed up for it." And the assignment is to turn outward, because the younger men need you now, chapter 14, and the grandchildren are coming, and there are hills to give.

This season counts because it's the one where you find out whether you built a marriage or a co-parenting arrangement, and if it was the second, this is the season God gives you to build the first.

## The retired man

Chapter 30 gave you Roy's whole rhythm, so I'll only say the shape here. Retirement in Scripture is a shift from carrying to assisting, not from working to sitting. The temptation is the drift with a fishing license and the anger that comes through the television, and both are what happens to a man who was made for a work and stopped.

The assignment is Numbers 8, which is to assist: the younger men, the grandsons, the church, the widow's gutters, your wife who has waited forty years for you to be home. And the assignment is Caleb: ask for a hill. At sixty-eight, at seventy-five, ask God for something hard, and expect Him to give it.

Roy's season counts more than his badge years did, by his own accounting. "I kept the peace for thirty-one years," he said. "I've been making men for five. I know which one God's going to ask me about."

## The widower

Mr. Pruitt buried his wife of fifty-one years and planted forty pecan trees the next spring. That's the assignment in one sentence, and it took him a year of grief to get there, and the grief was part of the assignment too.

The temptation is to decide that life ended when hers did. To let the couple friends fade and not replace them. To drift into the recliner and let the television keep you company. To let the loneliness gauge, which is telling the truth, convince you that you're done.

The assignment is to grieve as long as it takes and not let anyone rush you. To find one brother, because God said not alone and He didn't add an exception for men past seventy. To keep working whatever's left of the garden: trees, grandchildren, a Wednesday coffee at a gas station. To stay in the house of the Father. And to hold onto the promise God made specifically to old men.

:::margin
Isaiah 46:4 (NIV)
"Even to your old age and gray hairs I am he, I am he who will sustain you. I have made you, and I will carry you; I will sustain you and I will rescue you."
:::

I will carry you. It's the same word from Deuteronomy, a father carrying his son, and God says it to the gray-haired. This season counts, because somebody's grandson is going to eat those pecans.

## The man in weakness

Some of you are sick. Some of you are caring for someone who is. Mr. Alvarez has bathed his wife every morning for three years. Others of you have the illness yourselves, and your body won't do what it did, and you've concluded that a man who can't work is a man who's done. He isn't done. Paul had a thorn he begged God to remove, and God said no, and told him why.

:::margin
2 Corinthians 12:9 (NIV)
"But he said to me, 'My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.' Therefore I will boast all the more gladly about my weaknesses, so that Christ's power may rest on me."
:::

Made perfect in weakness. Not in spite of it; in it. The temptation in this season is uselessness, and it's a lie. The assignment is Numbers 8 again: assist. Pray, and pray specifically, for people by name; a man in a chair with a list is a power in a church. Call people, write to them, bless them. Mr. Alvarez calls a widower a day. A homebound man I know has prayed for every young man in his church by name every morning for eleven years, and those young men know it, and it has done something to them. That's a work, and it counts.

## The last season

There's one more, and men don't like to talk about it, and Scripture talks about it constantly.

:::margin
Psalm 90:12 (NIV)
"Teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom."
:::

Number our days, the psalm says, which means know that they're finite, and live like a man who'll give a completion report. The assignment in the last season is to finish well: to say what needs saying, to bless your children the way Jacob did from his bed, to hand off the way David did to Solomon, to reconcile what can be reconciled, to leave your plumb bob to someone with a story attached to it. Paul wrote his last letter from a cell, knowing.

:::margin
2 Timothy 4:7 (NIV)
"I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith."
:::

Finished the race is the sentence, and notice that it isn't won but finished, and that he kept the faith. Every man in this book is going to stand in this season, and the ones who have lived the four marks in every other one will find that the last season has a job too, and that they know how to do it, because they've been doing it all along.

## The marks don't change

Here's what I want you to see, having walked the seasons. The four marks look different at twenty-five and at seventy-five. Passivity in a young man is a tailgate; in an old one it's a recliner. The weight a new father picks up is a two o'clock feeding; the weight a widower picks up is a post-hole digger and forty bare-root trees. Going first at thirty is an apology in a kitchen; at seventy it's walking across a church parking lot to a young man who won't ask. And the greater reward is the same in every season, because it was never in the season. It's on the other side of all of them.

The marks don't change; the garden does. A man who has learned to reject passivity, accept responsibility, lead courageously, and expect God's reward in one season already knows how to do it in the next. He only has to find the new garden and work it.

There is no off-season, and February counts. The field that looks dead is doing the work that July depends on. Whatever season you're standing in, it has an assignment with your name on it, and the Father who set the seasons is watching you work it.

Find your season, and then work it like it counts, because it does.

## Before you turn the page

You've walked every season in this chapter, and I'd guess you found yourself in one of them and maybe saw your father or your son in another. I'd like you to close it without the ranking, because the ranking was a lie, and it has cost men decades of treating a real season as a waiting room. February counts. The field that looks dead is doing the work July depends on, and the Father who set the seasons is watching you work yours. May He give you the assignment of this season in one plain sentence with a verb in it, and may you find, whether you're nineteen or seventy-nine, that the garden you're standing in has your name on it.
