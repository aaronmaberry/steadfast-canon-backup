# 4. Stop Waiting

The third stair from the bottom talks. Put your weight on it and it groans loud enough to wake a baby, so everybody in the house learned to step over it before they learned to spell their own names. The man who owns that stair could quiet it before breakfast. Two long screws, a bead of wood glue, and the drill already hanging on a peg in the garage. Ask him about it and he'll tell you he's going to get to it. He means it, too. Meaning it is as far as the repair has gotten.

The wood doesn't worry me. The habit does. A man can know something is broken, know exactly how to fix it, and step over it until stepping over it feels like part of the floor. The stair is the small version. The big versions live in the same house. The money talk with his wife that he's parked under "when things calm down." The brother he hasn't called since the funeral. The bank statement he won't open. The son who went quiet about the time his voice changed. The church he keeps meaning to walk back into. He's going to get to all of it. He's been saying so for years.

The first mark says a man rejects passivity. Most men hear that word and picture a guy on a couch with the blinds closed. He's not the one I lose sleep over. The man I lose sleep over is up before the sun. He's useful and tired and good at almost everything, except the handful of things that would actually change his house. On those, he waits. He's waited so long that it doesn't feel like a decision anymore.

## What it wears

Passivity almost never shows up looking lazy. Laziness is easy to spot, and shame at least gets a lazy man moving. So passivity borrows nicer clothes.

Sometimes it dresses up as patience. I'm giving it time. Now and then that's wisdom. More often, the man is asking time to do a job that belongs to him, and time can't have the conversation for him.

Or it looks like keeping the peace. I don't want to start something. So nothing starts. The peace he's keeping is a kitchen where nobody says anything that costs them.

It can pass for humility. Who am I to bring it up? You're her husband. You're the boy's father. You're the one standing there. That's who.

It can look like getting ready. Another book, another podcast, another long talk about the conversation instead of the conversation itself. A man can become an expert on something he's never once done.

It even borrows faith. I'm waiting on the Lord. There's a real way to wait on God, and there's a fake one that signs His name to folded hands. The difference matters, and we'll get to it.

Busyness is the best disguise of all. A man who's on the road before dawn, home after dark, coaching on Saturday, and stacking chairs on Sunday doesn't look passive to anyone, least of all himself. His wife could tell you in one breath what he's avoiding. For a lot of men, busy is where they hide.

The first man hid too. After he ate the fruit, when God came walking in the garden in the cool of the day, Adam was in the trees.

:::margin
Genesis 3:9 (NIV)
"But the LORD God called to the man, 'Where are you?'"
:::

God wasn't lost. He knew exactly where Adam was standing. The question meant something else. Where is the man I put here to work this ground and take care of it? Where's the man who stood right there while it all went wrong and never said a word? God still asks that question, and He asks it by name. Tonight He'll ask it at the bottom of your stairs.

## Why we wait

No man has ever told me he was waiting because he's passive. He has better reasons, and every one of them sounds reasonable when he's sitting in his driveway with the engine off.

He didn't want to make things worse. That's fear of a fight, and it's the most common reason there is. He figures the quiet he has beats the noise he'd make. What he doesn't figure is that quiet has a cost too, and the cost keeps adding up while nobody looks at the bill.

He wasn't sure he was right. That's fear of being wrong, wearing a responsible face. Standing still is a choice with results of its own, and he'll answer for those results the same way he'd answer for a bad move.

Nobody asked him. This one usually starts in a boyhood where taking initiative got you in trouble, where the safe way to be in a room was to wait for orders. Some men carry that into the office, into church, and into their own marriage, still waiting to be picked. If you're young and this is you, hear it plainly. Nobody hands a grown man a permit to lead his own life. The ground under your boots is yours to work.

It'll sort itself out. It won't. I've never seen a field weed itself.

:::margin
Proverbs 24:30-31 (NIV)
"I went past the field of a sluggard, past the vineyard of someone who has no sense; thorns had come up everywhere, the ground was covered with weeds, and the stone wall was in ruins."
:::

Look at what the sluggard didn't do. He didn't burn the field or knock the wall over. He did nothing for long enough, and the thorns took care of the rest. Thorns don't ask permission. Neither does the distance in a marriage, or the interest on a debt, or the silence of a boy who's decided his father isn't coming.

Under all the reasons a man will say out loud, there's one he won't. He's comfortable. Comfort never argues with him. It just keeps the room warm.

## The real kind of waiting

Scripture does tell a man to wait on the Lord, and I won't take that from you. But look at the posture it asks for.

:::margin
Psalm 27:14 (NIV)
"Wait for the LORD; be strong and take heart and wait for the LORD."
:::

Be strong and take heart. Those words are for a man on his feet, doing every part that's his while he waits on the one part that isn't. Abraham waited most of twenty-five years for the son God promised him. In those years he moved his whole household to a country he'd never seen, built altars, armed his own men to go rescue his nephew, and stood eye to eye with kings. Waiting on God never meant sitting down on the work that already had his name on it.

Here's a simple test. When you say you're waiting on God, look at your hands. If they're doing the next right thing you already know to do, you're waiting on God. If they're folded until He makes it obvious, you're waiting on comfort and blaming the delay on Him.

:::margin
Luke 9:51 (NIV)
"As the time approached for him to be taken up to heaven, Jesus resolutely set out for Jerusalem."
:::

Jesus knew what was waiting for Him in Jerusalem. He didn't drift toward it. He set His face and went.

## The same stair in every house

In a single man's apartment, the stair is called "when I'm married," as if a wedding flips a switch. The man at the altar will be the man this year is building. In a house with a newborn, it's called "when things settle down," and things won't. Ten minutes at the counter after the baby is down, phones face down, is what this season has room for, and it's enough. For the man whose marriage ended, the stair is the call he'll make once the anger cools. The anger cools after a man does the right thing in spite of it. For the man who sold the business or turned in his keys, the stair is a phone that never rings because nobody's calling. The work is wherever he walks in and asks. A young man hides behind "once I," and the sentence never gets finished. A father in his middle years waits for a quiet son to come around on his own. Different houses, same stair.

## The first screw

Name the thing in words a calendar could hold. "My marriage" is too big. Try "the money talk I've owed her since spring." "My son" is too big. Try "half an hour in the truck with the radio off, and the question I'm afraid to ask him." You can't do a fog. You can do a named thing.

Then ask yourself what you're really waiting for. Be honest. It's almost never more information. It's a feeling. You're waiting to feel ready. That feeling doesn't come first. It shows up after you move.

Find the smallest action that still counts as action. Pulling up his number doesn't count. Calling him does. Sitting with the drill across your knees doesn't count. The first screw does.

Do it before noon. A man's resolve gets weaker as the day goes on, and by dark the couch has hired a lawyer.

Expect it to feel wrong. Your nerves have been tuned to waiting for years, so the first right thing will feel like a mistake for about a day. That's what it feels like to use a muscle you've let go stiff.

## What He sees

:::margin
James 4:17 (NIV)
"If anyone, then, knows the good they ought to do and doesn't do it, it is sin for them."
:::

That verse closes the back door. Doing nothing isn't neutral ground. God writes it down in the same book as the things we do. But turn the page. The Father who sees what's done in secret also sees a man come down the hall with a drill before the house is awake, because he's done stepping over that stair. He sees the number dialed and the question asked. There's a reward for that, His reward, and we'll sit with it when we talk about what lasts. Today, fix the step. Then make the call.

## Before you turn the page

Nobody sat you down and taught you to step over the stair. You picked it up the way a boy picks up his walk, from the men around him, without a lesson. You can put it down the same way, one deliberate move at a time. The first move is the hardest by a mile. May the God who went looking for Adam in the trees find you on your feet.
