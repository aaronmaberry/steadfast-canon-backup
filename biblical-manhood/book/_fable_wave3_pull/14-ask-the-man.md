# 14. Ask the Man

When I was young enough to think a long marriage was a trick somebody could explain, I asked an old man named Harlan how he'd done it. He'd been married longer than I'd been alive. We were standing by his truck after church, and I don't know what made me ask.

He looked at me a second, deciding something. Then he said, "Ride with me Saturday."

I spent Saturday riding shotgun in his feed truck while he ran his route, and he hardly mentioned marriage. But at noon he pulled into a gas station and called his wife from the pay phone to tell her where he was and when he'd be home, and I learned later he'd done that every working day since their wedding. I rode with him the next Saturday, and the one after, and by the end of the summer I'd learned more about marriage from the cab of a feed truck than from every sermon I'd heard on it.

There's a man about twenty years ahead of you on the road you're walking. He knows what's coming because he's been there, and he's near you right now, waiting for somebody to ask. Be the one who asks him.

## Two men, both waiting

The younger man doesn't ask. He figures the older man is busy, or that he ought to work this out on his own, or that his own father should have told him, and since he didn't, there's something shameful in asking anyone else. The older man doesn't offer. He figures the young man doesn't want an old guy's opinion, or that he made too many mistakes to have anything worth handing on. So both of them wait, and a generation of men grows up with nobody twenty years ahead telling them what's coming.

That isn't the design. Scripture's picture of manhood is a chain: Moses to Joshua, Elijah to Elisha, Paul to Timothy, and then Paul told Timothy to keep it going.

:::margin
2 Timothy 2:2 (NIV)
"And the things you have heard me say in the presence of many witnesses entrust to reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others."
:::

Four generations in one sentence: Paul, to Timothy, to reliable people, to the others they'll teach. That chain is broken in most churches and families in this country, and the break is on both ends. A brother knows what you're going through because he's in it too. The man ahead knows because he came out the other side, and he can tell you what's over there, which a brother can't. You need both.

## What to ask for

Don't ask him to mentor you. "Mentor" is a large, formal, frightening word; an older man hears a commitment he isn't sure he can keep and a role he isn't sure he's qualified for, and he'll say no, or say yes and dodge you. The word gets in the way of the thing.

Ask him something specific instead. "How did you stay married this long?" is a question a man can answer, or can say "ride with me" to. How did you decide when to leave that job? What do you wish you'd done differently with your kids? What did you do the first year after the divorce? Can I come watch you do that?

One question is the whole ask. If it goes well, ask another next week. If he says "ride with me," go, and bring your hands. If you want a shape, borrow the twenty minutes and a time box: "Could I call you for twenty minutes a week for a couple of months? I've got some things I'm trying to figure out, and I think you already have."

## How to pick him

You're looking for a man about twenty years ahead of you who's living the four marks: not the most impressive man at church, but one who has walked the road you're on and is still standing at the end of it.

Watch his wife's face when he talks. A woman who's been married to a real man for decades looks at him one way, and a woman married to a performer looks at him another. Be careful of the man who only has wins; he's either lying or he hasn't been paying attention, and either way he can't help you. What you need isn't his wins. It's his scars and his noon phone calls.

He has to be a man you'd want to be like at his age in the things that matter, and he has to be reachable. If you can't think of one, look harder. He's the deacon nobody asks anything of. He's the man in the back pew who's been married longer than you've been alive and hasn't had a real conversation with anybody half his age in years. He's the retired man at the hardware store. There are more of them than you think, and nearly all of them are waiting.

## Once you've asked

Come with a question every time. An older man will give you the rest of his life if you come with a real question, and drift off if you come to hang out. The question is the respect.

Do what he says, at least once, and come back and tell him what happened. That report is how you earn the next hour. Older men have wasted a great deal of advice on young men who nodded and did nothing, and they've learned to stop. Be the young man who does the thing and comes back, and he'll give you everything he has.

Don't make him your father. That's a weight he can't carry. He'll give you a lot, but he can't give you what your father didn't, and if you go looking for that you'll wear him out and end up disappointed.

And say thank you, out loud and specifically. "What you told me about handling my dad, I did it, and it changed something." Older men don't hear that enough.

## To the man who's ahead

Now the older man, because half of you are him.

You're the man, and it doesn't matter that you don't feel finished; nobody finishes, and Paul was writing about his own unfinished race the week he died. You've walked a stretch of road a younger man is just starting, and you know where the washouts are. He doesn't need you to have gotten it right. He needs the truth about where you got it wrong, and what you did on ordinary days that held.

:::margin
Titus 2:6-7 (NIV)
"Similarly, encourage the young men to be self-controlled. In everything set them an example by doing what is good."
:::

That's the plan, and it isn't a program: example and word, in the ordinary.

Go first, because he isn't going to ask; he thinks you're busy, and he's a little afraid of you. Give him something he can say yes to: "I'm hanging a gate Saturday and could use a hand, if you want to learn how." Then don't lecture. Ask questions. What's going on with work? How's it going with her? What's heavy? A young man will tell an older man things he'd never tell a peer, if the older man asks and then actually waits for the answer.

Tell the truth about your failures; that's what makes the whole thing work. When a young man hears a man he respects say, "I nearly lost my marriage, and here's what I did wrong," something in him unlocks, and he stops performing. Your scars are the curriculum.

Let him work beside you; men learn beside each other more than across from each other. Keep it small and steady, twenty minutes a week or a job every other Saturday, because the steadiness is the teaching. A young man who has never had a reliable man learns more from you showing up eleven weeks in a row than from anything you say on any one of them.

And expect to get more than you give. Every older man I know who's done this says the same thing: I thought I was helping him, and mostly he was saving my life. If you've turned in your badge or your hard hat and wonder what a man does with his mornings now, this is what. God sets the lonely in families, in both directions at once.

## The man ahead, in every season

The single man should ask a married man about marriage before he's in one; he'll learn more in a year of asking than in three years of doing. The father with a baby should find a man whose kids are grown and ask what he wishes he'd done when they were small, while there's time to act on it. The divorced man needs a man ahead more than most and is the most tempted to skip this out of shame; find a man who's been through it, or one who's been married a long time and won't judge you, and ask him to walk with you. The man in the middle of life stands in both places at once, with a man ahead he's never asked about the empty nest and a man behind who's watching his marriage and has never been spoken to; the chain needs him to look both ways. And the older man whose own father never did this for him isn't disqualified by what he didn't get. He's equipped by what he lived through.

## Ride with me

Harlan has been gone a while now. I rode with him a good many more Saturdays over the years, and asked him a few hundred more questions, and he answered most of them by putting the truck in gear. When I got married he sat in the back pew, and afterward he shook my hand and said, "Well. You paid attention."

I did, because I asked, and because he said ride with me, and because neither of us waited for the other one to go first.

Ask the man, and be the man. The chain has been broken long enough.

## Before you turn the page

The break in the chain isn't your fault, and nobody can fix it but the men reading this. If you're the younger man, ask one real question, feel foolish for about eight seconds, and go back next week. If you're the older man, be the one who says ride with me. May the Father who set men in companies put a Harlan in your life and make you a Harlan in someone else's, and may you both find that neither of you was as busy as you thought.
