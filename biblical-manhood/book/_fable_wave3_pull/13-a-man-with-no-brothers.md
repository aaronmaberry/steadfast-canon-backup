# 13. A Man With No Brothers

The clipboard asks for two names.

Dale learned that in an emergency room a little after two in the morning, with a pressure in his chest that turned out to be nothing and felt, for three hours, like everything. The nurse handed him the intake form and went to find a gown. He wrote his wife's name on the first line. Then the form asked for a second emergency contact, someone other than a spouse, and he sat with the pen over the line and couldn't fill it.

His phone held a few hundred names: customers, suppliers, hunting buddies, men from church, a brother-in-law two states over, his pastor. He could have called any of them to move a couch. There wasn't one he could call at two in the morning and say, "I'm scared."

He told me this long afterward, flat, the way men report the weather. "I didn't know I didn't have anybody until I needed somebody."

## Most men have no brothers

The men this applies to almost never think it does, so let me say it straight. Most men have no brothers. They have buddies, men they do things beside: hunt, fish, golf, watch the game. And if they're married they have a wife carrying the entire weight of knowing them, a weight she was never built to carry alone.

Ask a man who could call him at two in the morning, and who he could call. Ask him who's allowed to tell him he's wrong to his face. Ask him who knows what he's actually afraid of. Then watch how many good men, with full phones, go quiet.

We weren't made to do this alone. That isn't a self-help line; it's on the second page of your Bible, where the first thing God called not good was a man by himself. A wife was the first remedy, not the only one; all through Scripture men come in companies. David had Jonathan. Jesus had twelve, and three inside the twelve.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 4:9-10 (NIV)
"Two are better than one, because they have a good return for their labor: If either of them falls down, one can help the other up. But pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up."
:::

Pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up. That was Dale on the gurney with the pen in his hand.

## Why we don't have them

I've asked a lot of men why they have no brothers. They say they're fine, which is pride in a work shirt; nobody's fine. They say they don't have time, and they do; nobody has asked them for the right amount yet. They say they don't want to be a burden, and under that is usually a boy who learned early that needing things was dangerous, and grew into a man who'd rather go under than reach for a hand. They say men don't do that, but men did it for most of history, and we've been dying quietly since we stopped and calling it independence.

They say they've been burned, and some were, but one bad meal is no reason to stop eating. And they say they have their wife. Good. Keep her. But your wife is not your brother, and if she's the only one who knows you, you've set a weight on her that will eventually bend her. Ask her.

## What a brother is

A brother is a man who knows what you reach for when the day goes bad, the anger or the screen or the silence, and asks about it, and you tell him the truth. He can ask you anything: money, marriage, the bedroom, what you looked at this week, whether you've prayed. He will come, at two in the morning, to the hospital, because he's your brother. And he tells you the truth, not the nice version, said with love and said once.

:::margin
Proverbs 27:17 (NIV)
"As iron sharpens iron, so one person sharpens another."
:::

Sparks fly when iron sharpens iron. A brother will say things that sting, and you'll be sharper for them.

And a brother helps you find God when you can't. David is on the run, hunted by the king, worn down and hiding in the woods, and his friend comes to find him.

:::margin
1 Samuel 23:16 (NIV)
"And Saul's son Jonathan went to David at Horesh and helped him find strength in God."
:::

Helped him find strength in God. Not gave him strength, not fixed his situation; Jonathan couldn't fix anything, since his own father was doing the hunting. He went into the woods anyway and helped his brother find what only God could give. That's the job, and it's what you're for.

A brother isn't a crowd; you need one man, maybe two. He isn't a counselor, though he might tell you to see one. And he isn't necessarily the man you have the most fun with; some of your best hunting buddies would make terrible brothers.

## Twenty minutes

Most men don't lack the want. They lack a picture small enough to survive their life. Every picture they've been handed is too big: a weekly breakfast at six in the morning, a small group with homework, a weekend away. A man with a newborn or a business going under looks at those, knows he can't, and does nothing.

Here's a smaller one, the one that finally worked for me. Twenty minutes, with one man, once a week, at the same time. It has to be the same twenty minutes every week, because a time that floats is a time that dies.

What you do with it is three questions and a prayer. What happened this week? Not a report, just the two or three things that mattered. What's heavy? This is the one that counts: what are you carrying, and what's the fear under the week? You each tell the truth, and the other man does not fix it. He listens, and he does not fix it. What's next? You each name one move before next week and say it out loud so a man heard you, because next week the first question will be, "Did you do it?" Then two minutes of prayer, out loud, for each other.

Some weeks one of you is a wreck and you spend all twenty on his "what's heavy," and that's what it's for. But the floor is twenty minutes, once a week, at the same time, and the floor doesn't move.

Twenty minutes is small enough to survive anything: tax season, a twelve-hour shift, chemotherapy, grief. I've never met a man who couldn't find twenty minutes a week, and plenty who couldn't find two hours and so found nothing. Three questions also steer; without them most men's gatherings drift to sports and trucks inside eleven minutes. The twenty isn't the ceiling; it's the seed.

## How to find him

Look for a man who's living the four marks, mostly. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. He's probably already in your church or your crew, and you've never asked him anything real. He can be your age or older; older is often better, and more on that soon. He shouldn't be your boss, your employee, or your brother-in-law, because you need to tell him the whole truth without a job or a Thanksgiving hanging on it.

Then ask him, in plain words: "I'm trying to be a better man, and I've realized I don't have a brother. Would you be willing to talk with me for twenty minutes a week for the next three months and see how it goes?" The three months make it easy for him to say yes and easy to let go if it isn't working. Most of the time it works and nobody remembers to stop.

Expect it to be awkward. It passes. And expect that he's been waiting too, because men are lonely, and they aren't going to go first. You are.

## Brothers in every season

The season changes the shape of it, not the need. A single man with a house full of roommates may already have a brother and only need to add the twenty minutes, or may have a house full of men and not one who knows him. A father with a newborn has twenty minutes and not a minute more, which is why the number is twenty; do it on the phone with the dishwater running. A divorced man usually finds his friends vanished with the marriage, because they were mostly her friends or didn't know what to do with him, and he needs one man more than anyone else reading this; skip the big group for now and get one man on the phone. And a retired man or a widower finds the built-in company of men went out with the work or with his wife. You need one man.

## The garden

The night before He died, Jesus took three men into a garden. He knew they'd fall asleep. He knew one of them would deny Him before sunrise. He took them anyway, and He asked them for something.

:::margin
Mark 14:34 (NIV)
"'My soul is overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death,' he said to them. 'Stay here and keep watch.'"
:::

The Son of God, who needed nothing, asked three men to stay with Him in the worst hour of His life. If He didn't do it alone, you don't get to either.

Dale has a brother now. It took him most of a year to ask a man from church he'd known for two decades and never had one real conversation with. They talk every Thursday on the drive to lunch, three questions and a prayer. For the first month, Dale said, he thought it was the dumbest thing he'd ever agreed to. Then last spring he was back on a clipboard for a scheduled surgery, and when the form asked for the second name, the pen didn't stop.

So find a brother and give him twenty minutes at the same time every week. Start with the sentence. Go first.

## Before you turn the page

I know the sentence I've asked you to say feels like the most awkward sentence in the language. I also know that nearly every man who has said it was answered with "I've been wanting somebody to ask me that." May the Father give you the nerve to ask one man for twenty minutes, and may He give you, on the other end of that line, a brother born for a time of adversity.
