# 15. The Problem Inside

There was a bubble in the paint on the fender of my first truck, the size of a thumbprint. Every spring I sanded it to bare metal, primed it, and painted it. Every fall it was back, bigger, with two smaller ones beside it.

The third year I took it to a body man. He pressed the bubble with his thumb, and it gave, and a little brown water ran out. "Paint's not going to fix that," he said. "It's coming from the inside." The rust was on the back of the panel, where paint never reached, eating its way out the whole time I'd been dressing up the front.

This is where the book turns. Everything so far has been about what you inherited: the father you got or didn't, the blessing nobody spoke, the brothers and older men you didn't have. All of it shaped you, but if I stopped there I'd leave you in the driveway with a can of primer, because there's a problem inside you that understanding your father will never reach.

The problem is you, and me, and every man who has ever lived, and Scripture has a plain word for it. Until you say the word, you'll spend your life painting over it.

## Even with a perfect father

Suppose your father had been home every night, kind and present and strong, told you he loved you, and blessed you on your way into manhood, and you had brothers and a mentor besides.

Would you be a good man? Better, probably. But good? Would you never lie to your wife, never look at what you shouldn't, never quietly decide you're better than the man in the next pew?

You know the answer, because the first man had all of it. Adam had a perfect Father, a perfect place and work and wife, and no wound of any kind. He still reached for the fruit, or stood silent while it was reached for, then hid, then blamed. The problem inside a man didn't come from his father. It came through his father, from a father before that, all the way back to a garden, and it would be in you if you'd been raised by saints. Jeremiah called the heart deceitful above all things and beyond cure, and Jesus said as much to religious men busy managing their outsides.

:::margin
Mark 7:23 (NIV)
"All these evils come from inside and defile a person."
:::

From inside, He says, not from your father or your culture or your wound. Those opened doors, but what walked through them was already in you.

That doesn't sound like good news. It is. If your problem is entirely what was done to you, there's no way out; you can't un-have your father. A man who believes his whole problem is his inheritance has found a way to never repent, because you can't repent of what somebody else did; he'll spend his life on Adam's excuse: the father you gave me, he did it. But if the problem is yours, it can be dealt with, because God deals with what's yours. He doesn't offer to fix your father. He offers to fix you.

:::margin
Romans 5:8 (NIV)
"But God demonstrates his own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us."
:::

While we were still sinners. Not while we were victims, or under-fathered. He named the real problem and died for it, and if you'll name it too, you get the cure.

## The word

The word is sin, and men avoid it for two opposite reasons.

Some avoid it because it sounds too harsh. They'd rather say struggles, or issues, or baggage: soft words in a hard place. A struggle is something that happens to you. Sin is something you do. You manage the first and repent of the second.

Others avoid it because churches swung it like a club at them when they were boys. But the word doesn't belong to the men who misused it. It belongs to God, and in His mouth it's a diagnosis, not a beating. The body man wasn't attacking me. He was the first one to tell me the truth.

Sin is the bend in a man toward himself: he hides when he should stand up, blames when he should own, takes when he should give, goes quiet when he should speak. It's what Adam did in the garden and what you did last night. God described it to Cain before Cain ever raised a hand against his brother: sin is crouching at your door, it desires to have you, and you must rule over it. It's an animal, and you're told to rule it, which means you're responsible and not helpless. Cain didn't, and it had him, and his brother was dead in a field. Same animal, same door, every man.

## What to do with it

Paul said it for every man: what I want to do I do not do, but what I hate I do. You want to be patient with your kids and you're not; you want to be present with your wife and you reach for the phone. Then he asks the only question that goes anywhere: Who will rescue me? Not what. Who.

I won't hand you a program; most come down to trying harder, which is another coat of paint. Here's what I've watched work.

Say the word about yourself. Not "I've got some stuff," but "I sinned," out loud, to God, and specifically. "I lied to her about the money." David said silence wasted his bones; then he confessed, and God forgave him, and the whole distance between the two was one honest sentence.

Know the difference between guilt and shame. Guilt says, "I did a wrong thing." Shame says, "I am a wrong thing." Guilt is the bubble in the paint, ugly and honest about where the rust is, and once you've confessed it's done its work. Shame is a liar, and it drives men straight back to whatever they reach for, because it's the exact feeling anger and appetite and absence were built to numb.

Turn; don't just feel bad. Repentance isn't a feeling; it's a change of direction, and a man can feel terrible about something for decades and never once turn. Confess it, make it right where you can, put something in place so that door is harder to walk through tomorrow, and get back to work. Then turn again tomorrow, and that isn't failure. That's a Christian life from the inside: a man who keeps turning back toward the Father he keeps walking away from, and a Father who keeps receiving him.

Bring a brother into it. Sin lives in the dark, and it can't survive being said to another man who loves you.

:::margin
James 5:16 (NIV)
"Therefore confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed. The prayer of a righteous person is powerful and effective."
:::

The first time Wade said the word about himself to another man was in the twenty minutes, when his brother asked what was heavy. He started to say "work" and stopped, and after a long silence said, "I scared my daughter last night. I didn't touch her. I didn't have to. I raised my voice and she went small, the way I used to go small, and I've been calling it my temper for years, and it's a sin." The other man was quiet a while. Then he said, "Thank you for telling me," asked what Wade was going to do next, and prayed for him. Wade said he'd expected to feel exposed and instead felt, for the first time in years, clean. James says a man like that is healed, not only forgiven.

And ask for a steadfast spirit. David, after the worst sin of his life, didn't ask God to fix his circumstances. He asked for a new inside.

:::margin
Psalm 51:10 (NIV)
"Create in me a pure heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me."
:::

You can't paint over what's rusting from the inside; the metal has to be made new, and only God does that, and He keeps doing it. That's the prayer of a man who has quit dressing up the front.

## The same rust in every season

The single man who learns this now walks into marriage with a tool most husbands find too late. The father with a newborn will meet parts of himself this year he didn't know were there; exhaustion strips paint off a man, and what's under it isn't new, only visible. The divorced man has been dreading the part where I say the divorce was all his sin, and I won't, because only God and the two of them know; but there's a part that's his, and he can't heal until he's said "I did this," full stop, without her part attached. The man in the middle of life has built a competent life around managing the outside, and competence isn't righteousness. And the older man may have managed the outside so long he's forgotten there's an inside; confessing in unison with a congregation isn't the same as saying it about yourself, by name, in your truck. The metal can be made new at any age.

## The right thing

The body man cut the panel out, welded in new steel, and the fender never bubbled again. I stood in his shop feeling two things at once: foolish, because I'd spent three springs and good money on the wrong thing, and relieved, because somebody had finally told me the truth and the truth had a fix.

That's what it feels like to stop blaming your father and say the word about yourself. Foolish, for a minute. Then relieved all the way down, because the problem inside has a name, and the name has a cure, and the cure has already been paid for by a Man who knew exactly what was in you and came anyway.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. All four start here, with a man who quits painting and says the word.

## Before you turn the page

I've asked you to say the hardest word in the language about yourself, and it's good news, because God deals with what's inside you and has already paid for it. May the Father who forgave David the moment he spoke meet you the moment you do, and may you feel, on the far side of the foolishness, the relief of a man who's finally fixing the right thing.
