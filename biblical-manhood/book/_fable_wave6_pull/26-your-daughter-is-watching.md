# 26. Your Daughter Is Watching

I stand at the front at weddings, so I see the thing the guests can't. At the end of the aisle, before her father lets go of her arm, the bride looks at his face. Not the groom's. His. It's quick, and she's been doing it her whole life.

Her father thinks she's asking permission. She isn't. She's reading the face she learned to read before she could talk, the one that taught her what a man is, what a woman can expect from one, and what she's worth. The groom six feet away was chosen with that face in mind.

Your daughter is watching. That's the whole chapter. But she's learning three things from you that she'll carry to her grave, and she's learning all of them before you say a word.

## The three lessons

The first is what a man is, and she's learning it from you. You're the definition, and every man she meets for the rest of her life gets measured against it. If you're gentle, she'll expect gentleness and be startled by harshness. If you're a storm, she'll marry a storm or spend her life flinching at weather. So be the definition on purpose. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. Whatever she sees you do with those four is what the word will mean to her.

The second is what a woman deserves, and she's learning it from how you treat her mother. Whether you defend her, thank her, touch her gently, listen when she talks. Whether you roll your eyes or go quiet for three days. Your daughter is taking notes on what a woman can expect from a man, and she'll choose a husband with those notes in her hand. If you want to know what kind of man your daughter will marry, look at how you treated her mother this week.

The third is what she's worth, and she's learning it from how you look at her. Whether your attention has to be earned, by grades and goals and pretty, or is given because she's yours. A girl her father delights in grows up knowing her worth isn't up for negotiation. A girl he doesn't will go looking for a man to tell her what her father didn't, and the men who volunteer for that job are almost never the ones you'd choose.

Three lessons, taught before breakfast, whether or not you mean to teach them.

## The face in the bleachers

Wade learned the third one at a softball field. His daughter Nora struck out to end an inning, and before she walked back to the dugout she turned, found his face in the bleachers, and read it. He'd seen it a hundred times and never asked what she was looking for.

She was looking for what his face did when she failed. He was loud and proud on the hits. He also let his face fall on the strikeouts, not in anger, just the small drop of a man who wanted it for her, and she'd learned to check for the drop. Her worth, as far as she could tell, went up and down with the count.

On the drive home he said, "I don't come to watch you hit. I come to watch you." Then he went to work on his face, which is harder than it sounds; you can't fake gladness in front of a child. He decided before every pitch what his face would do after it, and made sure the face she found after a strikeout was the one she found after a double. It took a season. She doesn't look up after strikeouts anymore. She looks up after hits, to find somebody to share them with. Wade says that's the difference between a girl checking and a girl knowing.

## What she needs from you

Delight, first, and on your face. Approval is for what she did; delight is for who she is. God does this with His people: a Father who takes great delight in His child and rejoices over her with singing. That's a face that changes when the child walks in, and yours can. Look up and let her see you're glad she exists. It's on your face or it isn't, and she's reading it.

:::margin
Zephaniah 3:17 (NIV)
"The LORD your God is with you, the Mighty Warrior who saves. He will take great delight in you; in his love he will no longer rebuke you, but will rejoice over you with singing."
:::

Her mother honored. The most important thing you'll ever do for your daughter is love her mother where your daughter can see it. Thank her at the table, take her side, apologize to her in front of the children when you were wrong. If you're divorced, she's watching how you treat her mother across two houses, and when she tries to get you to take her side against her mother, the sentence is, "Your mom loves you and she's doing her best, and I won't talk about her that way." She'll relax when she hears it, because she'd been afraid she had to choose.

Protection. The second verb God gave the first man was to keep, to guard, and your daughter is the most precious thing in your keeping. Protection isn't control; a father who locks his daughter in a tower teaches her that she's fragile and men are jailers. Protection is a fence with a gate, and a father standing at it. You know what's on her phone, and you've told her why. The boy who wants to take her somewhere meets you first. You've told her, before she needs it, what happens when a boy puts his hands where he shouldn't: she says no, she leaves, she calls you, and you come. And you protect her from you: your temper, your sarcasm, your bad day landing on her. Paul told fathers not to exasperate their children, and a daughter worn out by her father's moods learns that men are exhausting.

:::margin
Ephesians 6:4 (NIV)
"Fathers, do not exasperate your children; instead, bring them up in the training and instruction of the Lord."
:::

Your words. What you call her, she'll grow toward. Call her beautiful and mean her whole self. Call her strong, and call her yours. Say "I love you" out loud every day, because there's a boy coming who'll say it to get something, and it works on a girl who's been starving for it. Job gave his daughters an inheritance alongside their brothers in a world that didn't do that. He put his name and his weight behind what they were worth.

Your time, because delight without time is only a sentiment: a standing breakfast, a drive with the radio off, a walk after supper that never stops. Your steadiness when she pushes you away, because most daughters go through a stretch of wanting nothing to do with their fathers, and hurt fathers withdraw. She isn't pushing you away because she doesn't need you. She's testing whether you'll stay. Keep the breakfast when she sits there in silence. Say "I love you" to a closed door. When she comes out the other side, she'll remember who stayed.

Your faith where she can see it, and your eyes at home. Pray for her by name and tell her you do. A daughter who has watched her father kneel knows what a man on his knees looks like, and she'll know to look for one. And she notices where your eyes go: the pool, the restaurant, the screen. A father whose eyes wander teaches her that women are to be looked at. A father whose eyes stay home teaches her that she can expect a faithful man.

## In every season

If she's grown, she's still watching: whether her father has changed, whether he'll show up for her children the way he didn't for her. A grown daughter still needs her father's blessing, and the window didn't close when she left home. If you weren't the father she needed, say so in those words, without a "but." If you've never told her you're proud of her, the call is overdue, and it will mend something she didn't know was still broken.

If you're in the divorced seat, you can't be at the breakfast table every morning. Never speak against her mother, not when your daughter baits you, not when it's true, not when you're tired; she's half her mother, and every word against the mother lands on the girl. Be there for everything you're allowed to be, and fight for some of the rest. Call on a schedule, the same night every week, through the months of "fine" and "nothing," because the night she finally talks for an hour is paid for by every night she didn't. Make your house a home she lives in, not a place she visits: her own room, her own toothbrush in the cup. And be steady when the world isn't. Your divorce shook her ground. A father who's present, honorable, and growing is where she stands while it shakes.

If you have no daughter, there are girls in your church who don't have any of this. Be a man they can watch. Honor the women in your church where the girls can see it. Coach, teach, be the uncle who shows up, and do it in the light: never alone with a girl who isn't yours, always visible, always above reproach. That's part of what you're teaching her, that a good man is safe.

## The face she checks

Jesus was tender with daughters. When a synagogue leader's little girl died, He went into the room, took her by the hand, and said, "My child, get up." Then He gave her back to her father. That's the Man you're following, gentle with a girl who wasn't His.

:::margin
Luke 8:54 (NIV)
"But he took her by the hand and said, 'My child, get up!'"
:::

Your daughter has been reading your face since before she had words for what she found there, and someday she may read it one last time at the end of an aisle. What she finds then is what she's found all along. Give her something worth seeing.

## Before you turn the page

If your face has been teaching your daughter the wrong thing, don't leave this in shame, because shame is what a father's face shows when he's ashamed, and she'll read that too. Wade's gladness came back the way a muscle does, from using it on days it didn't feel like much. May the Father whose face changes when His children walk in change yours this week, and may your daughter catch you being glad she exists.
