# 5. Pick Up the Weight

If you've ever built a fence that had to hold, you know that the whole thing depends on the corners.

Line posts you can set shallow and quick, because all they do is hold the wire up off the ground. But a corner post takes the strain of the entire run. Every foot of wire you stretch pulls against it, and it pulls for years, through wet winters and dry summers and cattle leaning on it to get at the grass on the other side. So you dig it deeper than seems reasonable, and you set it in concrete if you've got any, and you brace it with an H and a diagonal and tamp it until your shoulders burn. And then you never think about it again, because a corner post that does its job is invisible. Nobody drives past a fence and admires the corners. They only notice when one starts to lean.

That is responsibility, and that's the second mark. A real man accepts responsibility, and what that means on a Tuesday is that he sets himself at the corner and takes the strain, and mostly nobody notices, and he has made his peace with that.

## The oldest excuse

When God asked Adam what he'd done, Adam gave the first excuse in human history, and men have been giving versions of it ever since. He said the woman gave him the fruit. And then, as if that weren't enough, he added that the woman God had put there with him gave it to him. In one sentence he blamed his wife and his Creator and left himself out of the story entirely.

I would like to tell you I've never done that. I've done it within the last month.

Here's what blame is, underneath the words. Blame is a man setting down weight that belongs to him and pointing at whoever happens to be standing closest. It's fast, and it works in the moment, and it also leaves the weight sitting on the ground where it stays and grows. Nothing in a man's life has ever gotten better because he correctly identified whose fault it was. I've watched men win that argument for thirty years and lose everything the argument was about. The next generation did it too. When God asked Cain where his brother was, Cain answered with a question that has become something like a motto for men who don't want to carry anything.

:::margin
Genesis 4:9 (NIV)
"Then the LORD said to Cain, 'Where is your brother Abel?' 'I don't know,' he replied. 'Am I my brother's keeper?'"
:::

The whole rest of the Bible is going to spend its pages answering that question, and the answer is yes. You are your brother's keeper, and your wife's, and your children's, and your neighbor's, and not because you caused their trouble but because you're the man standing there. That is the piece most men miss about responsibility, and I want to slow down on it. Men tend to think accepting responsibility means owning their fault, and it does include that. But a man who only takes responsibility for what he caused is still measuring by fairness, and fairness is how a boy thinks. A man takes responsibility for what's in front of him. The bill nobody opened, the kid nobody's talking to, the widow down the road whose gutter came off in the storm, the mess in the church parking lot after an event he didn't even attend. Nobody would blame him for walking past any of it. He picks it up anyway, because that's what a corner post is for.

## What the weight is

I want to be specific, because "responsibility" is one of those words that can mean everything and therefore mean nothing, and a man can nod at it for years without ever picking anything up.

The first weight is your own life. Your body, your money, your time, what you put in front of your eyes, whether you're in church or in bed on Sunday morning, whether you've told the truth this week. Before a man can carry anything for anyone else he has to carry himself, and a surprising number of grown men are still waiting for somebody to do that for them. If you're single, this is where the weight starts, and it's not a small thing. A man who pays his own rent on the day it's due, gets himself to the doctor, keeps his word about small things, and shows up when he said he would is already carrying more than a good many husbands I know.

The second weight is your house. If you're married, the state of your marriage is your responsibility, and I don't mean half of it. I'm not saying your wife has no part; she has a large one. I'm saying you don't get to wait for her part before you carry yours, and a man who keeps a ledger in a marriage has already started losing it. If you have children, their formation is yours too. Not the school's, not the youth pastor's, and not their mother's alone. That doesn't mean you do everything. It means that if it isn't happening, you don't get to say "I don't know" the way Cain did.

The third weight is your work, whatever it is. The job isn't only how you get paid. It's a place where somebody is trusting you with something, and how a man handles a trust is how he handles a trust, whether it's a fleet of trucks or a spreadsheet or a classroom or a hospital floor at three in the morning.

The fourth weight is your people. Your church, your brothers, your street. The older man at the end of the pew who stopped coming three weeks ago. The younger man who keeps hovering at the edge of the group and has never been asked in.

And the fifth weight, which is the heaviest and the quickest to set down, is what you've done wrong. When you've sinned against somebody, the weight is naming it without a "but" attached, and making it right where you can. Paul puts two sentences a few lines apart in Galatians that sound like they contradict each other, and they don't.

:::margin
Galatians 6:2 (NIV)
"Carry each other's burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ."
:::

:::margin
Galatians 6:5 (NIV)
"for each one should carry their own load."
:::

Those are two different words in the original. A burden is something crushing that no one should have to carry alone. A load is a man's own pack, the daily weight that's his and nobody else's. A real man carries his own load without complaint and reaches for his brother's burden without being asked. A boy does the reverse; he wants help with his own pack and looks the other way when his brother goes down under something heavy.

## The wrong weight

Now I have to warn you about two ways this mark goes bad, because I've watched both of them wreck good men who thought they were being responsible.

The first is the man who grabs weight that isn't his, the man who has to fix everyone: his grown daughter's marriage, his brother's drinking, every problem at church that comes across his path. He can't sit in a room where something's wrong without taking it onto himself, and he calls it responsibility, but it's often something else wearing responsibility's clothes. Sometimes it's control, and sometimes it's a way to feel needed, and sometimes it's a boy who learned early that if he didn't hold the family together nobody would, and he's still doing it at fifty with people who never asked him to. There is a weight God gives a man and a weight a man grabs in order to feel important, and only the first one shapes him. The second one just breaks his back. Moses hit this wall, trying to carry an entire nation by himself, and he finally said so to God.

:::margin
Numbers 11:14 (NIV)
"I cannot carry all these people by myself; the burden is too heavy for me."
:::

God's answer wasn't "yes you can." It was seventy elders. Sometimes the most responsible thing a man can do is admit that the weight is too big for one man and go find the others.

The second way this mark goes bad is quieter, and it's the one I want to spend the rest of the chapter on, because I've watched it take down more good men than the first.

## Providing without disappearing

Walt is fifty-five, and by every outside measure he has done it right. He built a small business from one truck to eleven. His kids went to college without loans. His wife has never once, in thirty years, had to worry about a bill. He'd tell you, and he did tell me, that he has carried the weight of that house every single day of his adult life, and he's right.

He's also barely in it. He leaves before anyone's awake and gets home in time to eat whatever's left in the pan, standing at the counter. He knows the names of his daughter's boyfriends because his wife tells him. He hasn't been to a church service in a year that didn't fall on a holiday. And when his wife finally said to him one night in the kitchen, "I don't need more. I need you," he honestly didn't understand the sentence. Wasn't he there, he wondered, and wasn't every hour of it for them? I want to be careful here, because Scripture is not soft on a man who won't provide.

:::margin
1 Timothy 5:8 (NIV)
"Anyone who does not provide for their relatives, and especially for their own household, has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever."
:::

Provision is a weight, and a man carries it, and I'm not going to make a hardworking man feel guilty for working hard. But look at the word Paul uses, which is household and not paycheck. A man is responsible for a household, and a household is people, and people need more than a roof over their heads. They need a man in the room. Walt was providing everything for his house except himself, and somewhere along the way he'd started to believe that the check was the man.

Here's the plainest way I know to say it. Provision that costs your presence isn't provision. It's a very expensive way to disappear.

And I understand how it happens, because I've felt the pull. Work rewards you; it has a scoreboard, and it tells you clearly when you've done well, and home mostly doesn't. Home is a toddler screaming at a plate of food you paid for, or a teenager who grunts at you, or a wife who's tired in a way you can't fix with a purchase. So a man drifts toward the place that claps for him and tells himself he's carrying the weight, and he is, but not the one that matters most.

So how does a man provide without disappearing? He decides that presence is part of provision and not a luxury he earns after the provision's done, and he writes it into his week the way he'd write in a client meeting. Tomás, who you'll come to know well in this book, has a four-month-old and works ten-hour shifts on a pipeline crew. He can't shorten the shifts. What he did was claim the twenty minutes between his boots coming off and dinner going on the table. His phone goes on the charger by the door, not in his pocket. He takes the baby so his wife can sit down with both hands free for the first time since morning. That's twenty minutes out of a day, and it's also the entire difference between a man who's providing and a man who's disappearing, and his wife would tell you so.

There's a version of this for the man whose money is tight and whose shift just got longer. The overtime is real, and the budget is thinner than it was last year, and the temptation is to treat presence like a luxury you earn after the bills are paid. It isn't. On a Tuesday when you come home late and tired and the numbers are ugly, claim the same twenty minutes anyway: boots off, phone on the charger, baby on your shoulder, or ask your wife how her day was with your eyes on her face. Provision that never walks in the door isn't provision. The Father who feeds the birds also saw His Son leave the work and sit with people, and a man under a thin budget still has that move available to him.

He learns to tell his wife the truth about his capacity. Not "I'll be home by six" when he knows perfectly well it's eight. Not silence about the busy season until she finds out by living through it. A man who says "the next six weeks are going to be brutal, and here's what I'm going to protect, and here's what I'm going to need from you" is carrying weight in a way she can lean on. A man who vanishes into the busy season and comes out the other side expecting credit has asked her to carry the not knowing, which is a weight nobody should carry alone.

And he keeps a floor. A floor is the minimum that doesn't move no matter what the job does: Sunday morning, one dinner a week with no screens on the table, the bedtime prayer with the kids in person or by phone from a hotel room in Midland. The floor is what tells your family that they're the corner post and the work is the wire, and not the other way around.

If you're the older man reading this, past the paycheck years, don't imagine you've aged out of this one. Roy told me that after he retired he realized he'd spent thirty years providing for a house he'd barely lived in, and that his wife had built an entire life in the spaces he wasn't in. "I had to ask to be let back in," he said. "Not because she was cold. Because I'd been gone." He started by making the coffee, every morning, and sitting with her while she drank it. It sounds small until you've been gone thirty years.

## How to pick it up

The habit of this mark is simpler than it sounds, and I'll give it to you the way I'd give it to a son.

Say "that's mine," out loud if you can, the moment you see the thing that's in front of you and before your mind starts working out whose fault it is. You'll be surprised how much a sentence changes what your hands do next.

When you're owning something you did wrong, stop your sentence at the period. It ends at "I did it." Not "I did it, but you." Not "I did it because." A man who can say "I was wrong, I'm sorry, and I'm going to fix it" and then stop talking is rarer than you'd think and more powerful than you'd guess.

Own the outcome and not just the effort. A boy says "I tried." A man says "it isn't done yet, and here's what I'm doing next." Effort matters, but responsibility means staying with a thing until it holds, the way a corner post stays.

Carry your own load before you reach for a burden. If your own life is a mess, don't go fix your brother's marriage. Get your house in order and then reach; you'll reach with cleaner hands.

And when it's too heavy, ask for the seventy. Say so, to God and to men. That isn't setting the weight down. It's being wise about how a weight gets carried.

## The weight at every age

The weight a man carries changes shape as he goes, and I want you to see four men at the corner post before you decide where yours is.

A young man of twenty-three is learning that his own life is a weight. He's paying rent for the first time, and the rent is due on a day and not a season, and he's discovering that nobody is going to remind him to change his oil or go to the doctor or tell the truth about the ticket he got. Some men his age are still handing that load to their mothers, and some have picked it up. The takeaway for him is that a man who can carry himself is already carrying more than most husbands, and that this is where a corner post gets set.

A young father is learning that responsibility is not the same as the paycheck, which is Tomás's whole first year. He works ten hours and comes home to a wife who needs him to carry the baby for twenty minutes so she can use both hands, and the temptation is to feel that he's already carried enough. The takeaway for him is that the household is the weight, and the household is people, and the paycheck is only part of what people need.

A man in his fifties is discovering that he grabbed weight that wasn't his and set down weight that was. He has fixed everyone at church for twenty years and let his own marriage go quiet, and he's exhausted in a way that feels righteous and isn't. The takeaway for him is Moses and the seventy: some of what he's carrying was never his, and the thing that is his has been sitting in the hallway.

And an older man is learning that the weight doesn't retire. Roy thought his responsibility ended with his badge, and found that it had only changed shape into a grandson and two younger men and a wife he'd never studied. The takeaway for him is that the corner post doesn't get pulled when the fence gets old. It gets braced.

## The weight He carried

Every one of these marks ends at Jesus, and this one has to. The Man we follow picked up a weight that wasn't His. He hadn't sinned, and He wasn't at fault for a single thing that had gone wrong in the world since the garden. And He walked toward the whole of it, on purpose, and took it onto Himself.

:::margin
Isaiah 53:4 (NIV)
"Surely he took up our pain and bore our suffering, yet we considered him punished by God, stricken by him, and afflicted."
:::

Took up and bore are carrying words. Nobody handed Him that weight and made Him take it. He reached for it because it was in front of Him and He was the Man who could.

So when you set yourself at the corner, when you carry a thing nobody's going to thank you for, you are doing more than being responsible. You're looking like Him, and He notices, which is the fourth mark showing up inside the second. The Father who watched His Son carry the weight of the world sees a man in Lubbock get up at five in the morning to carry the weight of his house, and He does not forget it.

So pick it up. Dig the post deeper than seems reasonable, and set yourself at the corner, and take the strain.

## Before you turn the page

If you've been pointing at someone for a long time, I don't want you to feel crushed by this chapter. Blame is the oldest habit men have, and you learned it from a man who learned it from a man, all the way back to a garden. The Man we follow picked up a weight that wasn't His so that you could pick up the ones that are, and He isn't standing over you with a clipboard while you do it. He's under the other end of the safe. May the Father give you the plain courage to say "that's mine" this week, and may you find, as I have, that the weight gets lighter the moment you stop arguing about whose it is.
