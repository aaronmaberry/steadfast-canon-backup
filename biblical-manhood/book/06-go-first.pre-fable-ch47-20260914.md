# 6. Go First

A pickup rolled on Highway 287 north of Wichita Falls a few years back, in the kind of rain that comes at you sideways, and I was maybe six cars behind it when it happened. What I remember most is the slowing. Every car slowed down and every driver looked, and the brake lights ran all the way back as far as I could see, and you could feel forty people thinking the same thought at the same time. Somebody ought to stop.

One man did. He was already out of his truck and running before I'd finished having the thought, a big man in a feed store cap, and the second his door opened, three more doors opened behind him, and then six more, and inside of a minute there were a dozen men in that ditch, and the one in the cap wasn't telling anybody what to do. He didn't have to. He'd gone first, and going first turned out to be the entire job.

That's leadership, as far as I've been able to tell after a lot of years of watching it and reading about it. I've sat through the seminars with the pyramids and the principles, and some of it was useful, but I'll tell you what it comes down to for a man on a Tuesday. Somebody has to go first. Into the ditch, into the apology, into the prayer, into the truth. A real man leads courageously, and leading courageously is mostly the willingness to be the first door open on a highway full of people who are waiting to see what everybody else does.

## Not in charge, but first

I need to clear something up before we go further, because a lot of men hear the word "lead" and picture a man at the head of a table telling other people what to do. Some men want that picture badly and some are repelled by it, and both of them have missed the point.

Leadership in Scripture is almost never about the head of the table. When Jesus wanted to teach His men what leading looked like, He got up from the head of the table, took off His outer clothes, and washed their feet. Then He told them plainly what He'd done.

:::margin
John 13:14-15 (NIV)
"Now that I, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also should wash one another's feet. I have set you an example that you should do as I have done for you."
:::

I have set you an example. That's the leadership sentence in the New Testament. Not "I have given you orders," but "I have gone first into the lowest job in the room, and now you know what it looks like." The going first was the leading. Gideon, before his three hundred men went down against an army that outnumbered them past counting, didn't give a speech about courage. He said something a good deal shorter.

:::margin
Judges 7:17 (NIV)
"'Watch me,' he told them. 'Follow my lead. When I get to the edge of the camp, do exactly as I do.'"
:::

Watch me, follow my lead, do exactly as I do. If you can't say those words to your sons about how you treat their mother, or to the younger men at church about how you handle your money, or to your wife about how you handle your temper, then you're not leading them, whatever your title says. You may be managing them, which is a different thing and a smaller one.

So when I say a man leads, I don't mean he's in charge of everybody. I mean that in every room he's in, he's willing to be the first one to do the right thing. And the reason that takes courage is that first is exposed. The first man in the ditch might be wrong about what he'll find there. The first man to apologize might not get one back. The first man to pray out loud at a table full of relatives who don't will feel every eye in the room on him. First is where a man can look foolish, and looking foolish is what most of us fear more than nearly anything else.

## The places a man has to go first

Let me be concrete about where this shows up, because "lead courageously" can float above a man's actual week if you let it.

Every marriage has a standoff in it somewhere. Three days of politeness, everybody knowing what happened, nobody willing to say it. Somebody has to go first, and it has to be you, not because it was all your fault but because you're the man and going first is what a man is for. "I was wrong about how I spoke to you Tuesday. I'm sorry." Then you stop. You'll want to add "and I know you also," and you must not add it, because the moment you do, you've turned an apology into an opening statement. A man who apologizes first and cleanly changes the weather in a house.

I've known men who've been Christians for thirty years and have never once prayed out loud in front of their own wives. They'll pray at church if they're called on, and they'll pray with the men's group, but at their own table, in their own bedroom, with the one person who knows them best, they go quiet, and the reason is always the same: it feels awkward. Of course it does, because it's supposed to feel awkward the first time you do anything that matters, and you go first anyway. "Lord, thank You for this food and for this woman, and help us tonight. Amen." That's a whole prayer, and it will take you eleven seconds, and it will do more for your leadership in your home than a year of good intentions.

Every family and every crew and every church has a thing nobody's saying: the money isn't working, or Grandpa's drinking again, or the youth pastor hasn't shown up in three weeks. Somebody has to say it first, in the room, kindly and clearly. Nehemiah came to a city that had been sitting in rubble for decades, with everybody stepping around the broken walls as if they were furniture, and the first thing he did was name it out loud.

:::margin
Nehemiah 2:17 (NIV)
"Then I said to them, 'You see the trouble we are in: Jerusalem lies in ruins, and its gates have been burned with fire. Come, let us rebuild the wall of Jerusalem, and we will no longer be in disgrace.'"
:::

They did see the trouble. They'd been seeing it for years. What they needed was one man to say it and then to say "come." That is going first, and it's what your family needs from you about the thing nobody's saying.

In a marriage, somebody has to turn first. Toward her, when you've both been facing your phones for a month. Toward the hard conversation about the kids. Toward the bedroom, when it's been a long time and neither of you knows how to bring it up. A wife should not have to ask for her husband's attention, and if she's asking, you've already made her go first, and that's a weight she shouldn't have to carry.

And a house needs to know where it's going. Not a five-year plan with a logo on it, but a man who can say "this year we're going to be a family that eats together four nights a week, and here's how," or "we're going to get out of debt, and here's the first step," or "we're going to start going to church again, and I'm driving." Direction is a man saying "come" and then walking. If you're single, you still set direction, for your own life and for the people who orbit it: your roommates, your brothers, the younger guys at work who watch how you live whether you know it or not. A single man who knows where he's going and says so is a leader whether anybody ever hands him a title.

## What we're actually afraid of

I said first is exposed. Let me name what the exposure feels like, because a fear you can name is a fear you can walk through, and a fear you can't name will keep you on the bank forever.

We're afraid of looking foolish. The prayer will sound clumsy, the apology will come out wrong, the true thing will land badly and everybody will look at us.

We're afraid of rejection, that she won't apologize back, that the younger man won't want a mentor, that the family will go quiet and change the subject and we'll sit there having said the thing.

We're afraid of being wrong. What if the thing nobody's saying is actually fine and we're the one who's off?

And we're afraid of what it will cost, because if we go first on the truth about the money, we're the one who has to do something about it.

Every one of those fears is reasonable. Every one of them has come true for me at some point. And not one of them is an excuse, because Paul told a young man who was afraid of exactly these things what the Spirit inside him was actually like.

:::margin
2 Timothy 1:7 (NIV)
"For the Spirit God gave us does not make us timid, but gives us power, love and self-discipline."
:::

Not timid, Paul says, and notice that he doesn't say "not afraid," which I think is deliberate. God doesn't promise a man that the fear goes away. He says the Spirit He gave you isn't the timid kind. The fear is in you, and so is something stronger, and courage is what happens when you let the stronger thing move your feet. And here's the part that makes going first possible at all. You are never actually first.

:::margin
Deuteronomy 31:8 (NIV)
"The LORD himself goes before you and will be with you; he will never leave you nor forsake you. Do not be afraid; do not be discouraged."
:::

The LORD himself goes before you. Every time you're the first door open, you're actually the second, because He's already in the ditch. A man who goes first isn't being brave on his own account. He's following Somebody who went first into everything, including death, and came back out. Which means the worst thing that can happen to you for going first has already happened to Him, and He's fine.

## Going first in different houses

Going first looks different depending on where you're standing, and I want to turn toward a few of you so you'll recognize it in your own rooms.

If you're single and there's a woman you're interested in, go first. Ask her plainly, for a specific day at a specific place, with your intention where she can see it. Not a hangout and not a "we should get coffee sometime." And if it's been three months and you know what this is, be the first to say so. Men who make women guess aren't being careful. They're being cowards with good manners, and she deserves a man who'll go first into the awkward conversation about what the two of you are.

If you're a new father, go first at two in the morning. Don't wait to be asked. Don't lie there hoping she'll get up because she's the one who can feed him. Get up, change him, bring him to her, take him back when she's done. Your wife is learning in these months whether she has a partner or a second child, and she's learning it in the dark, and nobody's watching but God.

If you're divorced, going first is going to cost you more than it costs most men, because there's history in every direction you could move, and you go first anyway. Be the first to keep the tone civil at the exchange. Be the first to tell your kids the truth in a way that doesn't make them carry your anger. Be the first to show up at the school thing even though she'll be there. Kenny told me the hardest sentence he ever said was to his ex-wife on the sideline of a soccer field: "You've done a good job with them." It wasn't strategy, and he didn't say it to get anything. It was going first. He told me it changed the next two years.

And if you're an older man, going first means you initiate with younger men and not the other way around. They're not going to ask. They think you're busy, or they think you'd say no, or they've simply never seen it done. Roy started sitting at a different table at his Thursday breakfast, one with two men in their thirties who looked like they'd wandered in by accident. He asked them about their work. Then he asked about their wives. Then he asked if they'd like to keep doing this. That's the whole method, and it's Gideon's method: watch me, and follow my lead.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A young man of nineteen goes first when he's the one in the truck full of friends who says, "Let's not," about the thing everybody knows is stupid, and takes the ribbing for it. He goes first when he asks the girl plainly instead of hovering for a year, and when he prays out loud at a table of guys who've never done it. The takeaway for him is that courage at nineteen is mostly the willingness to look foolish for eight seconds, and that the other doors open after his.

A man in his forties goes first when he calls the brother who's been drifting, and when he says "I was wrong" in a marriage that's been polite for three days, and when he's the one at the meeting who says the money isn't working. Anthony's first sentence to his wife after nine years of winning fights was the hardest thing he'd ever done. The takeaway for him is that going first in his forties is mostly going first into the apology.

## How to go first

Prepare the first sentence and not the whole conversation. Just the sentence that opens the door: "I need to tell you something and I'm nervous about it." "I was wrong about Tuesday." "Can I pray for us before we eat?" A man who has the first sentence ready will say it. A man who's waiting for the whole speech to form in his head never will.

Go first in small rooms before you go first in big ones. Pray at your own table before you pray at the church picnic. Tell the truth to your wife before you tell it to the elders. Apologize to your kid before you apologize to your boss. Courage grows in small rooms, and a man who's practiced it there will have it when the big room comes.

Lead with a question when you can. "What do you think we should do about this?" is often stronger than an answer, because it goes first into the conversation without pretending you have everything figured out. Men think leadership means having the answer. Mostly it means being willing to start.

Expect that nobody will follow right away. The man in the feed store cap was alone in the rain for about eight seconds, and eight seconds is a long time when you're the only one out of your truck, and you go anyway, because the doors open after the first one, not before it.

And do it on a Tuesday. Not at the retreat and not at the New Year, but at a kitchen table or in a truck cab or on a soccer sideline with no music playing. The rooms where going first feels least dramatic are the rooms where it counts the most.

## The Man who went first

Hebrews gives Jesus a title I didn't notice for years.

:::margin
Hebrews 12:2 (NIV)
"fixing our eyes on Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith. For the joy set before him he endured the cross, scorning its shame, and sat down at the right hand of the throne of God."
:::

A pioneer is the one who goes first into a country nobody's been to and makes a way for the rest. He went first into the garden of Gethsemane and the men He brought with Him fell asleep. He went first into the trial and the ones who'd sworn to die with Him ran. He went first into death, alone, and that was the whole point of it.

And look at what carried Him through. For the joy set before Him. That's the fourth mark again, holding up the third. He could go first into the worst thing a man has ever faced because He was living for a reward on the other side of it, and so can you. The awkward prayer, the clean apology, the truth said out loud in the room, the two o'clock feeding, the sentence on the sideline: none of it is wasted on the Father who watched His Son go first and gave Him a throne for it.

Somebody has to be the first door open. Let it be you.

## Before you turn the page

You've probably got a room in mind by now, one where everybody's slowing down and looking and nobody's stopping. I'd like you to hear one more time that you won't be alone in it. The LORD Himself goes before you, and the men who followed Gideon into the dark didn't have to be brave on their own account; they only had to watch him and do what he did. You have a Pioneer who went first into everything, including the worst thing, and came out. May the Father give you the first sentence, and the eight seconds it takes to say it, and the grace to stand there while the other doors open.
