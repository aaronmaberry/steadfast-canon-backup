# 20. Destiny Is Not a Vibe

A young man told me over coffee that he'd left his fourth job in three years because it didn't feel like his calling. He was twenty-seven. He was sharp, and hardworking when he worked, and a believer, and completely stuck, because somewhere along the way he'd been taught that God had one specific thing for him to do with his life and that he'd know it when he felt it. He'd been waiting for the feeling since he was nineteen, and it hadn't come, so he kept leaving, and every time he left he was a little more certain the next thing would be the one, and a little less able to stay anywhere long enough to find out.

Across town that same week I sat with Hollis, the deacon who told Cole he was a good man. Hollis is seventy-four. He's worked the same land his father worked, taught the same Sunday school class for forty-one years, buried a wife and a son, and never once in his life, he told me, had anything he would call a calling: no voice, no lightning, no moment where he simply knew.

"I just did the next thing God put in front of me," he said. "For about fifty-five years."

I asked him whether he thought he'd found his destiny. He looked at me the way you'd look at a man who had asked whether you'd found your hands.

Here's what I want to say in this chapter, and I'm going to say it straight because a whole generation of men has been sold the opposite. Destiny is not a vibe. It isn't a feeling of significance. It isn't a sense that you're meant for something large that hasn't shown up yet. It's an assignment, given by God, carried out in a place, in a generation, mostly on Tuesdays, and finished. Hollis has one, and the young man across town has one too, and he has been walking past it four jobs in a row.

## The verse that ends the argument

There's a sentence in Acts that I think is the best one-line definition of a man's destiny in the Bible. Paul is preaching in a synagogue, walking through the history of Israel, and when he gets to David, the greatest king they ever had, he says this.

:::margin
Acts 13:36 (NIV)
"Now when David had served God's purpose in his own generation, he fell asleep; he was buried with his ancestors and his body decayed."
:::

He served God's purpose in his own generation. That's the whole thing. Not "discovered his passion" or "found what he was born to do" or "lived his best life." He served God's purpose, in his own generation, meaning among the people and in the place and time God had put him rather than some other. And then he died and his body decayed, which Paul includes so you'll understand that even David's destiny wasn't about David lasting. It was about the purpose lasting.

Notice the word served. Destiny is a service word. It's a man handed a job by Someone above him, and doing it. It's the corner post, not the parade. And notice that it's God's purpose and not David's. David had plenty of purposes of his own, and the ones that came from him nearly wrecked everything. The one that came from God is the one Paul is still talking about a thousand years later.

That is your destiny too. To serve God's purpose in your own generation. Whether or not it feels like anything is beside the point.

## Three lies

Let me name what most men have absorbed about destiny, because you can't put down a lie you haven't looked at.

The first lie is that you'll know it when you feel it. This is the one that had the young man changing jobs. The idea is that somewhere out there is a life that will finally feel right, and that the not-feeling-right is proof you haven't found it yet. But feelings are the most moving star in the sky; we said so in chapter 17. Moses didn't feel called; he argued with God for an entire chapter. Jeremiah felt too young. Gideon asked for signs three times. Jonah felt so strongly about his calling that he got on a boat going the opposite direction. If feelings were the test, none of them would have gone. The feeling, when it comes, comes after the going, and sometimes it never comes at all, and the work counts anyway.

The second lie is that it's big. The culture has taught you that a real destiny has a platform, a visible impact, a story people tell. So a man who has spent thirty years welding, raising three kids, teaching a Sunday school class, and showing up for his neighbors looks at his life and concludes he missed it, because nobody is telling his story. Meanwhile Jesus told a story about a master who came back and praised a servant for being faithful with a few things, only a few, and the reward for faithfulness in small things was more things to be faithful with. There's no version of Scripture where God's purpose for most men is a stage. His purpose for most men is a garden, and gardens are worked in obscurity.

:::margin
Zechariah 4:10 (NIV)
"Who dares despise the day of small things, since the seven eyes of the LORD that range throughout the earth will rejoice when they see the chosen capstone in the hand of Zerubbabel?"
:::

Who dares despise the day of small things. God's eyes range over the whole earth, and they rejoice at a man laying a stone. The small day isn't the waiting room for destiny. It's where destiny happens.

The third lie is that it's somewhere else: the next job, the next town, after I get married, after the kids are gone, after I retire. Men live their whole lives with destiny one move away, and the move keeps moving. But remember chapter 18: God placed the man. He put him in a specific garden and told him to work that one. Your generation is this one, and your place is where your boots are. Paul told the Athenians, who thought a great deal about destiny, that God marks out men's times and places on purpose.

:::margin
Acts 17:26 (NIV)
"From one man he made all the nations, that they should inhabit the whole earth; and he marked out their appointed times in history and the boundaries of their lands."
:::

Appointed times and boundaries, Paul says, which means you aren't in your town and your century by accident. The purpose is here, and it won't be more here somewhere else.

## Three truths

Now the other side. The first truth is that destiny is assigned, not discovered. You don't find it the way you'd find a good fishing spot. It was prepared before you arrived, and it's handed to you.

:::margin
Ephesians 2:10 (NIV)
"For we are God's handiwork, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance for us to do."
:::

Prepared in advance, the text says, which means the works are already laid out, and your job isn't to invent them. It's to do the next one. And here's what that frees you from: the terror of missing it. A man who believes he has to discover his one true purpose lives in fear that he'll choose wrong and waste his life. A man who knows the works were prepared in advance simply gets up and does what's in front of him, trusting that the One who prepared them will keep putting them there. That's Hollis, fifty-five years of the next thing, and he never once worried he'd missed his destiny, because he wasn't looking for it. He was doing it.

The second truth is that it's local. Your purpose has an address. It's your wife's name and your children's names and your street and your crew and your church and the men at the Thursday table. God's purpose for David was Israel and not the whole world; He handled the whole world through a great many Davids in a great many places. Esther's cousin said it to her when she was deciding whether to risk her neck for her people.

:::margin
Esther 4:14 (NIV)
"For if you remain silent at this time, relief and deliverance for the Jews will arise from another place, but you and your family will perish. And who knows but that you have come to your royal position for such a time as this?"
:::

For such a time as this. Not for some other time, and not for a bigger position. This one, now, here. And look at the warning that comes with it. If you stay silent, God will do it another way, and you'll have missed your part. That's the cost of treating your generation as a waiting room. God's purpose gets served regardless. It just gets served by somebody else, while you spent your one life waiting for a feeling.

The third truth is that destiny is finished by faithfulness, not by feeling. It's a work you complete, not a state you achieve. Jesus described His own purpose in exactly those terms.

:::margin
John 4:34 (NIV)
"'My food,' said Jesus, 'is to do the will of him who sent me and to finish his work.'"
:::

To finish his work. Not to feel fulfilled by it, but to finish it, and He did; "it is finished" were the last words. Paul at the end of his life didn't say "I found my passion." He said he'd fought the fight, finished the race, kept the faith. Those are completion words, said by a man in a cell who had spent most of his ministry in places he didn't choose, doing work that looked like failure to anyone watching.

That's the shape of a man's destiny: assigned, local, and finished by showing up. Every day of it looks like a Tuesday, and at the end of it there's a "well done" from the only One whose opinion of your life will last.

## How you actually find it

I've told you that you don't find it, and now I'm going to tell you how to find it, and both are true.

You don't find it by searching for a feeling. You find it by looking at what God has already put in your hands and doing that, faithfully, until He puts something else there. Here's where to look.

Look at the blueprint from chapter 18. You were made to work a garden and keep it, to carry a word, to speak, to not be alone, to love a bride if you have one, and to rest. Whatever else your destiny is, it's inside that shape. A man who ignores the blueprint while he searches for his calling is a man looking for his truck keys while he's sitting in the truck.

Look at your place. Where has God put you? What town, what job, what street, what church? That's your garden, and you work it. If God moves you, He'll move you; He isn't shy about it. Until He does, the purpose is here.

Look at your people. Who has God given you? A wife, children, a crew, brothers, a widow down the road, two young men at a breakfast table. Those names are your destiny, more than any job title ever will be. Most of what God's purpose in your generation looks like is what you do for the people whose names you already know. Look at your hands and ask what you can do, because Peter tells you what to do with that.

:::margin
1 Peter 4:10 (NIV)
"Each of you should use whatever gift you have received to serve others, as faithful stewards of God's grace in its various forms."
:::

Whatever gift you have received. Not the one you wish you had; the one you've got. If you can fix things, fix them for people who can't. If you can teach, teach. If you can listen, God knows there's a shortage. If you can make money, make it and give it. Your gift is a clue to your assignment, and using it to serve is how the assignment gets done.

And look at your season. Destiny changes shape as you go. A twenty-six-year-old single man's assignment and a seventy-one-year-old widower's assignment are not the same, and both are real. Ask what God's purpose looks like in this stretch, not the one you've left or the one you're waiting for.

Do those five things, and one day, probably not a dramatic one, you'll look up and realize you've been serving God's purpose in your generation for years without a single lightning bolt, the way Hollis did, with nothing but your hands.

## The story that names you

This whole part of the book is called "the story that names you," and I want to close the part by saying what that means.

Everybody has a name that his story gave him. The guy whose dad left. The guy who blew up his marriage. The guy who never got the promotion. The guy who's just a welder. The old guy nobody asks anything. Those names are real, in the sense that the events happened. But they aren't your name. They're the first man's names, the ones that come out of the first garden: naked, afraid, hiding, blaming. The master story gives you a different name: made in the image, placed, given a work, son of the Father who does not leave, in Christ, servant of God's purpose in your generation. And there's one more that I've saved, because it finally answers the question underneath all the questions.

:::margin
Revelation 2:17 (NIV)
"Whoever has ears, let them hear what the Spirit says to the churches. To the one who is victorious, I will give some of the hidden manna. I will also give that person a white stone with a new name written on it, known only to the one who receives it."
:::

A new name, known only to the one who receives it. God has a name for you that nobody else knows yet: not your father, not your ex-wife, not the men who've written you off, not even you. It's the name of the man He made you to be and is making you into, and one day He'll hand it to you on a stone, and everything your story called you will be gone.

That's what it means to be named by the story. Not by what happened to you, but by what God said about you before it happened, and by what He'll say when it's done.

## Destiny in every season

If you're single and waiting for your calling to arrive along with a wife or a career, hear the young man with four jobs. Your destiny isn't on the far side of a wedding or a promotion. It's the next assigned thing. It might be the young men at church who need a single man with time. It might be the job you've been thinking of leaving because it doesn't feel like enough. Stay there and work it, and serve God's purpose in this stretch of your generation, and let Him handle the next one.

If there's a baby in your house, your destiny currently weighs about twelve pounds and doesn't sleep, and I mean that with total seriousness. There's no larger assignment God will ever give you than the one crying in the next room, and the fact that it doesn't feel like a calling is exactly the point of this chapter.

If you're divorced, you may think the story is over, or at least the good part of it, and it isn't. Joseph's destiny ran straight through a prison. Peter's ran straight through the worst failure of his life, and Jesus told him ahead of time what would come after.

:::margin
Luke 22:32 (NIV)
"But I have prayed for you, Simon, that your faith may not fail. And when you have turned back, strengthen your brothers."
:::

When you have turned back, strengthen your brothers. Peter's failure didn't end his purpose. It became part of the equipment for it. Kenny told me that the men who listen to him now at his church are the divorced ones, the ones who won't talk to anybody else, and he said, "I never would have chosen it. But I'm the only one they'll let in." That's a man serving God's purpose in his generation with the very thing he thought had disqualified him.

And if you're older, "his own generation" is still yours. Not the one you led in your fifties; this one. The purpose isn't behind you.

:::margin
Psalm 92:14 (NIV)
"They will still bear fruit in old age, they will stay fresh and green,"
:::

They will still bear fruit, the psalm says, and I've watched it. Roy's destiny at seventy-two is a Thursday breakfast table and two men in their thirties. Mr. Pruitt's is forty pecan trees and a Wednesday coffee at a gas station. Hollis's is a Sunday school class and a deacon's word to a young man by a truck. None of those is a vibe. All of them are God's purpose, being served, in a generation, by men who stopped waiting for a feeling and did the next thing.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A young man of twenty-two is most tempted by the first lie, that he'll know it when he feels it, and he changes jobs and churches and girlfriends waiting for the feeling. Eli's assignment turned out to be a parts counter he stopped leaving and a nine-year-old he started coaching, neither of which felt like a calling and both of which were God's purpose for him in that stretch. The takeaway for him is that the assignment is found in what's already in his hands.

A man of fifty is most tempted by the second and third lies together, that destiny is big and somewhere else, and he's tempted to blow up a good life looking for a bigger one. Walt nearly sold the whole business to "do something that mattered" before an older man asked him whether being home for dinner might be the thing that mattered. The takeaway for him is that the purpose is here, and that it won't be more here somewhere else.

## Where it gets done

I'll tell you what happened with the young man. He stayed at the fifth job. Not because it felt like his calling, because it didn't. He stayed because an older man told him to stay for three years no matter what and see what God did with a man who stopped leaving. He's been there four years now. He runs a crew. Two of the men on it have started coming to church because of how he treats them, and he's told me, more than once, that he still doesn't have a "calling." He simply has a purpose, and it turned out to be wherever he stopped running.

Most of your destiny is going to be carried out inside a house. That's where the next part of this book goes: the wife, the kids, the bed, the table, the ceremony. If you're looking for where God's purpose in your generation gets served, it's mostly going to be in rooms with your family's names on them, on days with nothing special about them.

Destiny is not a vibe. It's a man doing the next thing God put in his hands, in the place God put him, for the people God gave him, until it's finished. Go do the next thing.

## Before you turn the page

If you've been waiting for a feeling, I hope this chapter gave you permission to stop. Not permission to stop caring, but permission to stop searching for a lightning bolt and to look instead at your place and your people and your hands and your season, because that's where the assignment has been the whole time. God has a name for you that nobody knows yet, written on a stone, and He's going to hand it to you when the work is finished. Until then, there's a next thing in front of you. May the Father who prepared your good works in advance show you the next one this week, and may you do it, without a vibe, and find that it counted.
