# 8. The Empty Chair

Curtis's son came home with his learner's permit on a Saturday, and by that afternoon the two of them were circling an empty school parking lot, the boy behind the wheel and Curtis in the passenger seat, trying to figure out what to do with his hands.

The boy did fine. He eased off the brake like he'd rehearsed it in his head for a year, which he had, and took two slow laps around the light poles while his father sat beside him with his right hand clamped on the door handle and his left foot mashed against a brake pedal that wasn't there, saying "easy" every time the boy touched the gas, in a voice Curtis didn't recognize. On the third lap his son put the car in park and said, "Dad. You're doing the thing."

Curtis didn't know what the thing was until late that night, at the kitchen sink after everybody else had gone to bed. He had never sat in that seat. When he was the one learning, the passenger seat held his mother, both hands braced on the dash, the two of them scared together, because his father had been gone for years by then and there was nobody else to do it. He had never once watched a man sit in that seat and be calm. He had no picture of it. He had a handle to hold and one word to say, and the word came out wrong.

## Reading the chair

Every man learned what a man is from a chair. Not from a book or a sermon; from whoever sat at the end of the table, or didn't. Before you could read a word you were reading him: how he came through the door at night, what his quiet meant, whether his hands were safe, whether he looked at your mother when she talked or over her head, whether he showed up to the thing you'd told him about twice. You finished the most important course on manhood you'll ever take before you were old enough to stay home alone.

This stretch of the book sorts what you inherited from what is yours.

Some of you had an empty chair, through divorce or death or prison or plain absence, and the shape at the table did the teaching: men leave, your mother's tiredness is yours to fix, figure it out because there's nobody to ask. Some of you had a full chair and the man in it was gone anyway. He worked, or drank, or gave the television an attention he never gave you, and you couldn't name one thing he thought about you. That man gets a pass he didn't earn. His hole runs as deep as the one left by a man who packed a bag and is harder to point at, because Dad was there, so what's your problem?

Some of you had a full chair and it was dangerous, and you read his mood from the way he set his keys down and got small, or got loud so the storm would land on you instead of your mother. And some of you had a good man in that chair. I'm glad. Even the best father had edges, and you inherited the edges too. Every man in the room inherited something. You'll just have to look harder for yours.

## The chair never stays empty

An empty chair doesn't stay empty. A boy can't live with a hole that size in the middle of his house, so something always sits down in it, and whatever sits down in it raises him.

Often it's the boy himself. He climbs into the chair and starts fathering himself, making his own rules, talking himself down when he's scared. Some of that becomes real strength. Some of it becomes a man who can't be fathered by anyone, including God, because he's done the job alone so long that letting somebody else do it feels like drowning.

For a lot of boys a lesson takes the seat: needing gets you hurt. So he quit needing and grew into a man who doesn't ask for anything, whose wife can never tell what he wants, who says he doesn't want anything and believes it. For others the room takes the seat, and the boy becomes an expert on everybody's mood but his own. Or the audience takes it, and he earns scarce approval with grades or jokes or a fastball, and he's still auditioning with gray in his beard and can't say for whom.

And sometimes what fills the chair is a picture of what he'll never be. Curtis swore before he could drive that he would never leave his kids, and he hasn't, and he's right to be proud. It's also the only picture of fatherhood he owns: a man he refuses to be. That's why his hand was on the handle. He knew every turn he wasn't going to make. Nobody had shown him how to sit still in the seat.

## Inherited, and still yours

What sat in that chair is what you inherited. Nobody asked a boy in grade school whether he wanted his father to leave. It happened to you, it shaped you, and God knows every line of it and isn't shrugging.

What you do with it now is yours. Curtis's short fuse with his kids is shaped like his father's absence, and it's still his. His wife doesn't live with his father's absence. She lives with his fuse. A wound can explain a man forever and never once change him; plenty of men have worked out precisely why they are the way they are and stayed that way, because understanding felt like progress and it wasn't.

Scripture is honest about men with empty chairs. Joseph's father loved him and couldn't keep him out of the pit. David's father left him with the sheep when the prophet came to look over his sons. Even Jesus was raised by a man who wasn't His father and who drops out of the story before He's grown. The chair shaped every one of them and excused none of them. That isn't harsh. It's dignity. God treats you like a man who can do something with what happened to him, because you are one.

## Where the chair goes

What does a man do with an empty chair?

He admits it's there. I've watched big men go quiet and say "I don't really think about it" when I ask about their fathers, and that sentence means the opposite. Say it out loud once. My father left. My father was there and I never knew him. My father scared me. My father was good and there's still a place he didn't reach.

Then he grieves it, which is the step men skip. Grief is feeling the actual size of what you didn't get: the seat nobody sat in, the hand that never landed on your shoulder, the words you needed as a teenager and are still waiting on. It's a loss as real as a death, and it deserves mourning, not managing. The man his father left in the field wrote the line I come back to most.

:::margin
Psalm 27:10 (NIV)
"Though my father and mother forsake me, the LORD will receive me."
:::

David doesn't pretend the forsaking didn't happen. He names it and says where he took it.

The third thing gets its own chapter later in this book, so I'll only point at it now. There is a Father who does not leave. The empty chair isn't the last word about fatherhood. It's the first word, and it's a lie, and the rest of this part of the book is about letting the truth sit down where the lie has been sitting.

:::margin
Psalm 68:5-6 (NIV)
"A father to the fatherless, a defender of widows, is God in his holy dwelling. God sets the lonely in families, he leads out the prisoners with singing; but the rebellious live in a sun-scorched land."
:::

A father to the fatherless. That isn't a figure of speech. It's who He is.

## The chair follows you

It doesn't stay in one room or one season. If your father is alive and still absent, quit waiting on him to become a man before you become one; build on the Father who's here. If you've just become a father, the chair comes back hardest now, in the fear that catches your breath when you hold your son: what if I do to him what was done to me? That fear means you're looking, which the men who repeat the chair never did. The divorced seat gets the hardest sentence, and I won't dress it up: a divorced father is somebody's empty chair now, and only a man honest enough to say so can do anything about it. Explaining won't fill it. Showing up will start to, every exchange, every game, every call, through the months when the answer to every question is "nothing." And if your father died before the conversation ever happened, I'm sorry. You can still say what you needed to say, to God, who knew him better than you did, and to a brother. Just not to him.

## The next Saturday

Curtis went back to the parking lot the next weekend, and before his son put the car in gear he told him the truth. Nobody ever sat in this seat for me, he said. I'm learning it while you learn that. Then he took his hand off the handle, set it on his own knee, and left it there, and when the boy swung the first turn wide, Curtis said, "You've got it," the first fatherly thing he'd ever said on purpose.

His son won't remember the driving. He'll remember the man who stayed in the seat.

That's what's yours. You didn't choose what sat at the end of your table. But you get to sit down in the chairs that are yours now and stay in them, and let your kids read something different from what you read. You get to stop fathering yourself long enough to be fathered. And you get to find out that the seat beside you was never as empty as it looked.

## Before you turn the page

God isn't asking you to pretend the chair was full. He's asking you to bring the empty one to Him. May the Father who sets the lonely in families receive you the way David said He would, and may you sit down at your own table tonight and stay.
