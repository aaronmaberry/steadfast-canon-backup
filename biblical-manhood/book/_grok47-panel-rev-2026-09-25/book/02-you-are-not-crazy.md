# 2. You Are Not Crazy

For years I treated a pile of slogans as if they were a plan. Strong, then soft. In charge, then out of the way. The drive in me was dangerous, until the day I failed to use it, and then that failure was why the house had gone cold. I could quote the pile. I could not steer a week by it. A man in the middle of his working years took the booth across from me at a diner and described the same pile. Six manuals for manhood. Each one wiped out the other five. He was supposed to live as though they formed a single set of directions.

What sat on him was weariness. The target kept moving, and he was still being scored. Somebody had called manhood a costume he could hang up. Somebody else had called it the central fact of him. A third voice told him to invent the meaning himself. A fourth said the meaning had been fixed for ages and he was simply bad at it. In church he had been handled as a problem the room needed to manage. In locker rooms he had been handled as an appetite the room needed to feed. Stay in that long enough and a man asks whether the fog rolled in from outside or whether he carried it in his own coat.

I told him the line I am telling you. The confusion lives out in the world. The gap you feel is a real one.

## The picture you were handed

The picture of manhood on offer to men right now will not hold still in one piece. We have spent years clamping the pieces together, then blaming our hands when they spring apart. I want the picture where you can look at it. A map that was never a map should come off your bill.

Begin at the table where you grew up. **The Empty Chair** takes that room apart later in the book. Here, the point is smaller. The person at the head of that table taught you manhood before letters made any sense to you. Most of us received the lesson in broken pieces. A father present and wordless. A father already absent. A father who entered like a storm. Even a faithful father left gaps. You left home holding a fragment, and you went looking for the rest.

Sellers were happy to supply pieces, and none of the pieces agreed. The culture around you filed manhood under problems. Building, competing, protecting, taking hold of a room, all of that got described as an old instinct the species should have dropped. The same culture then ridiculed men who stayed boys, who would not promise anything, who refused to grow. It never owned the order it had given.

Screens offered a fool, a brute, or a hero who answered to nobody. The fool was the sitcom father, lost inside his own kitchen. The brute was the villain, and some nights the person a boy was coached to admire. The hero was the private movie every boy ran. No worn-out wife appears in it. No father phones twice in a year. No son goes silent. Church, brothers, and a sink full of Tuesday dishes are all missing. He keeps a life with no address. Boys were schooled to covet that life from inside an actual house.

The marketplace said a man is his wage. The internet said a man is his watch history. Certain churches handed out "spiritual leader" in a form so vague that Wednesday still had no instructions. Other churches handed out warrior and king at a volume that left no chair for a payment book, a mother in a hospital bed, or third shift at the plant. Books aimed at men often start in the feelings and never come out. Feelings have a place. They do not score a life. Action does.

Put the fragment from home next to those manuals and you have the kit we have been navigating with. One picture was never in the box. Six maps were, and no two of them share a north. Carrying all six is easy. Walking by all six is impossible. Every new heading erases the last. The man who keeps at it gets dizzy and signs his own name to the dizziness.

## What the crossed maps do to a man

Three injuries follow those maps. I have the scars from each. You may recognize the set.

The maps freeze a man. If every path has been condemned by someone, the safe-looking choice is to stay put. Passivity, in a lot of men, is that freeze. I have sat on a tailgate with the tools still bagged and told myself that a muddled man should keep his hands off the wheel. It sounded like care. The tools stayed in the bag.

The maps turn a man into a set of performances. I learned the rotations early. One face for church. Another for the job. Another for the men after work. Another for my wife. The one who wakes up keeps getting replaced. A week of costume changes will empty you. Under the changes is a man who never got a picture stable enough to keep, so he borrows from the nearest spectator.

Shame is the third injury, and it trains a man to stay quiet. I carried that shame into the diner before I ever heard his version. Give a man orders he cannot meet, orders at war with each other, and he convicts himself. A hum starts under the day. He stays quiet about it, sure that speaking would seal the verdict. The man in the booth had the hum. Most men who sit with me have it. It is the noise a good man makes while he tries to satisfy six maps before Friday.

## What's actually true

Let me answer the hum at the speed it deserves.

Your sense that manhood is a real thing is a true sense. God authored it. Male and female, He formed them as image-bearers, and He judged the work good while their hands were still empty.

:::margin
Genesis 1:27 (NIV)
"So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them."
:::

Manhood is one way that image gets carried. The plan was His from the start.

Your other sense, that the instructions will not lock together, is sound as well. You were navigating on maps that cross. The lostness belongs on the maps' account.

And the hunger to hear, in ordinary words, what a man does is a right hunger. Other men were supposed to hand you a picture. Many of us got none. Guesswork filled the years after we left that table.

The answer ahead of you is plainer than a bookshelf and costlier than one. One sentence can hold it. Ordinary days have to live it, including the days when you feel nothing useful. A rush in the chest will not carry it. Neither will the biggest Sunday on your calendar.

## One pass through the room

Where you stand in life changes the place the contradiction bruises. The room is full of men with the same bruise.

School tells a young man his restlessness is a disorder. His phone tells him manhood is a crowd's attention. Youth group tells him to be a servant leader. Older boys tell him the count of girls is the measure. He attempts the entire menu and writes the crash on his own record. The crash belongs on the maps' account.

Inside one week a young father is ordered to show up and to earn, to direct and to yield, to be a rock for a newborn and to track his own emotions. He misses most of the orders because they cannot share a calendar. The hum beneath his fatigue comes from a demand that will not fit.

A man past the early years quits in silence. Manhood, he decides, is the job plus the tired ride back, and he has unplugged from the voices because they cancel one another. Peace is what it resembles. Under it, he folded the maps and used the stack as a seat.

An older man spent a life on a picture received decades back. Grandchildren hold screens that mock the picture, and he wonders if the foundation was an error. Part of what he received was true. Part was not. Truth did not expire. He needs a companion to sort the pile and to leave the true pieces upright.

Same maps. Every age in the room. You are not the sole carrier.

## Before you turn the page

Hauling a contradiction will tire a man out. That is the tiredness. Your strength has been spent on cargo that was mislabeled. If the hum has had a long run under your life, let it thin out tonight. The maps are visible now. Put them on the floor. A line that can bear weight is close, and you can set the false pictures down before the rest of life is solved. May God take off you what no one ever assigned, and brace you for what did.
