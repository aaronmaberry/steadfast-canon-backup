# 29. Give Your Son a Day He Will Not Forget

Ankle-deep, and cold enough to steal the breath. First light. Five of us went into the creek and each came back holding a stone.

He watched from the gravel, old enough that his voice was changing and young enough that men were the subject he studied. Something had been promised. Dressed by five. No reason given. We came off the road in the dark to the bar under the bridge, the place of his baptism two summers back. Alton, his grandfather, entered first. His father next. An uncle. The man who sits with his father twenty minutes a week. Then I did. Silence on the way in. The stones went down at his feet, a pile the bridge could see.

Joshua was what Alton read. Twelve men, twelve stones, out of the Jordan, so children would have a question. The men spoke. The uncle gave the boy the worst act of his life and the move a man makes after it. A hand on the shoulder, and the father said the three names he had worn out over the years, then a fourth. "You're becoming a man. That's what today is. Here's what that means." The four marks followed, every word, in his father's voice. A card carried the words in the father's own writing.

Alton's coat produced a brass plumb bob, heavy for the size of it, the boy's name and that date cut in the metal. It went into the boy's hand with an explanation.

He carries the bob daily. A parking lot is enough reason, his father says, for the boy to take it out and hold it. The stones have stayed on the bank. Some days he walks down.

A handoff never came for most of us. We waited on a sentence and the waiting became the life. Spare your son the wait. Give him a day. Men around him, a word, a weight, a welcome in. One morning he can point at and say, that is when.

## Why a day

God likes a marker His people can trip over. Israel crossed, and Joshua had twelve stones lifted from the riverbed and piled where the bank could hold them. He said what they were for.

:::margin
Joshua 4:6-7 (NIV)
"to serve as a sign among you. In the future, when your children ask you, 'What do these stones mean?' tell them that the flow of the Jordan was cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD. When it crossed the Jordan, the waters of the Jordan were cut off. These stones are to be a memorial to the people of Israel forever."
:::

Rocks, so a boy not yet born would ask, and a father would have a story ready. Passover ran the same way. A child asked why the night was different, and a father answered. Jesus heard His Father's voice in a river, out loud, before the work began. Things a man can point at.

Christian houses are thin on this for sons. A birthday. A graduation. No morning when the men say, plainly, you are a man, here is the meaning, and you belong with us. He supplies his own marker from the scraps. A girl. A drunk. A fight. A license. Stand-ins, grabbed because the real object was never put in his hand. Put a stone there.

## The design

One shape has held up.

Time it to the crossing. Voice, shoulders, the new habit of watching men. Those will tell you. Missed it? Hold the morning anyway.

Company stays small. Father, plus two to four men he already respects, men who will remain after the coffee is gone. A crowd makes actors of everybody. No men yet means you have a year to find them. You know that road.

Outdoors. Early. A place that weighs something. His hill. His baptism water. Arrival itself should cost him a little, a cold walk in the dark if nothing else.

He knows a morning is coming. He does not know its contents. You meet the men once. Each prepares a true thing. What a man is, from his own years. One failure and the afterward. One charge aimed at this boy. Five minutes, no more.

Get there by going, and go quiet, so the weight can sit on him before language does. Somebody reads. The line I keep hearing on these mornings is short.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 16:13 (NIV)
"Be on your guard; stand firm in the faith; be courageous; be strong."
:::

Four commands, one breath. Slow. Then the men, in turn, while he stands and receives what he will be able to quote. The father handles the four marks. The sentence, a line apiece, the weakness that is his own, the card in his handwriting. The name comes with a hand on the shoulder. Three, then the fourth. Tears are likely if the words are new in your mouth. Leave them visible. A son who sees his father weep in the act of naming him learns past anything a program can arrange. The token follows. Choose weight over price. Knife, a Bible dated inside, a great-grandfather's watch, a tool. Prayer with every hand on him, the father's last. Then the plain sentence. You are one of us. Breakfast after that, and they speak to him as they speak to a man. He will notice the change in the grammar.

## The plumb bob

Alton's explanation was a working man's.

You hang the bob beside the thing you are building. He had done it on every wall of the house this boy's father grew up inside. Set it there. Wait out the swing. Settled, it aims straight down. Your private idea of straight never edits it. Gravity refuses opinions.

Crooked work will leave your hands, and you will miss it, because crooked is also what stands around it. Hang the bob. Wait. Scripture is the line that does not wander. Hold a life next to it. Let the swing die. Look at the gap.

Weight is deliberate. Light brass swings and keeps swinging. Heavy brass decides. A man is built for the second kind, steady, loaded, hard to shove off true. The cut name is there for the hour you cannot remember who you are supposed to be.

His father says the bob has already sat beside a choice. Going along at school. Telling the truth about a grade. Brass, and also this book, sized for a pocket.

Your token can be another object. Say the meaning when it changes hands.

## The day after

Morning alone reforms nothing. I have seen the whole effort spent by Sunday, and Monday back to boy-treatment. The son files the day under a nice speech. Reality is the different treatment that follows. Larger hills. An opinion requested and then used. Work that belongs to a man. Correction that belongs to a man. Failure met with a man's bar and a man's mercy. The day is the marker. The Tuesdays are the handoff. A morning with no Tuesdays behind it is a stone somebody forgot to give a story.

County work filled Alton's years. The retirement dinner, he says, was the finest night his career ever handed him, and the next morning was the worst of his life. Well done at the table. Nothing when he woke. Stones mark a bank. Tuesday is the ground. He knew, so the following week the boy got a call to help pour a footing. Man, the day had said. The week began the making.

## If you never had a day

A stone in the throat is a fair response if nobody ever did this for you. Doubt that you can pass on what you missed is fair too, and it is wrong. The giving heals a place receiving would have healed. Fathers never named have stood at water, named their sons, broken in the middle, finished, and driven away changed. Want it complete? After your boy's morning, ask an older man for a private word, his hand on you. You are a man. The boy was done right by. Your hour did not close.

## The day in every season

Shape follows the seat. Divorce can still host this, and should. Tell his mother the aim. The boy is the subject. The two of you are not. A good stepfather can be invited. Resistance from her means you use your time, your men, and you keep the morning clean of the old war. Single, you may be a man in somebody else's circle, or you may be the start of a circle for a boy whose mother would thank God for four men and a creek. Ask her before you build it. Grandfathers should offer. Fathers want the day and cannot see the first step. You have standing, a token already in a drawer, and years enough to make the afterward true. Daughters need a marked day in another shape. Mother and grandmother present. Supper ahead of a dawn. Who she is, what she is worth, what a man owes her. Mark the son and leave her to guess, and you only did half.

## What do these stones mean

A grandson will ask. The bob, or the pile on the gravel. The answer is five men, cold water, first light, a card in a father's hand, a grandfather's bob on every wall he ever framed. Then the teller, if the men before him did the work, builds a morning for his own boy.

Give him the day. Then give him the week that follows it.

## Before you turn the page

No day of your own still leaves a word I would want spoken. The hour is open. What you give him comes back toward you. May the Father who piled stones in a river give you men, a morning, and nerve for the fourth name. May the boy carry a heavy thing in a pocket, and may it keep telling him who he is for.
