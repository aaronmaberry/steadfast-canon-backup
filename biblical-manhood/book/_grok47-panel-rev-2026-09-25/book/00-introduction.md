# Introduction

Most of the hours that have mattered in my adult life have been spent across a table from a man. A kitchen table. A folding table at the back of a fellowship hall. The cab of a truck with the engine off. A diner counter where the waitress already has the coffee poured because she has seen you on this stool before. Some of those men could have been my sons. Some could have been my father. Under the different jobs and the different decades they were asking the same three things. What is a man. Am I one. If I am not, is it too late.

This book is the long answer I give when the coffee has gone cold. It is not the only answer on the shelf. It is the one I have watched hold up in ordinary houses, across a lot of years, and I have tried to write it the way I would say it if you were sitting here.

## Who I'm writing to

Every season is already in the room. Find your seat.

You may be single, still waiting for somebody to call you a man out loud. You may be newly married, or so deep in the little-years that nobody in the house has slept a full night since March. You may be providing everything except yourself. Divorced, and still a father. Alone in a quiet house after the kids left. Retired, wondering what a man does with a morning that has no clock-in. Standing in a kitchen where her cup is still in the drainer. You are not a guest chapter. You are who this book is for.

I am not writing only to the man with the mortgage and the minivan. When the counsel has to change with the season, I will turn toward you inside the paragraph and say so. I will not stop every chapter to run the roll call again. **Every Season Still Counts**, near the end, is where we sit down with the seasons on purpose. Until then, trust that I know you are here. If the house is loud and the page count looks impossible, start with **A Quiet Tuesday** and keep the closer each week. Then read **Every Season Still Counts**, one chapter from the house that fits your seat, and one on your kids if you have them. Come back for the rest when you can. The book will keep.

## What I'm claiming

One sentence sits in the middle of this book. I did not invent it, and I will not pretend I did. Robert Lewis, at Fellowship Bible Church in Little Rock, gave it this shape years ago, in the teaching that became *Raising a Modern-Day Knight*. Men have been handing it to one another ever since. I have held it up against my own life for a long time, and against the lives of men in every season I just named, and I have not found a place where it lies.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Those are the four marks. **The Four Marks** will open them, and the chapters after it take them one at a time. For now, notice what kind of sentence it is. It is not a sentence about what a man feels. There is a whole shelf of books that start inside the chest, with longing and wild places, and some of those books were good to me when I was young. I am not writing against them. This book makes a plainer claim. The four marks are things you do, on ordinary days, and they show. They mark a man the way thirty years of work marks his hands. You can see them. You can see their absence. No amount of feeling covers either one.

## How the book is built

We start with an ordinary day, because that is where a man's life gets decided. **A Quiet Tuesday** names the problem. Then we tell the truth about the room you are standing in, and we put the four marks in your hands until you can say them without looking.

From there we go back to what you inherited and what is still yours. The chair at the end of your childhood table. The anger, the itch, and the drift. The Father who does not leave. The handoff you may never have received. Leaving home without slamming the door. Brothers. The ask. The problem inside you, which would still be there if your father had been perfect. Then further back, to the beginning of the human story, so you quit reading your life as if you invented it. Then the house. A wife, if there is one, and a life with a cause if there is not. The bed. A daughter. A son. Then the long road. Work, feelings, seasons, the years nobody claps for, a page you write, and the men who stay.

Running through all of it is Tuesday. Not the altar. Not the retreat. The day with nothing special on it, when nobody is watching. Every chapter lands, somewhere, on a Tuesday-sized picture. That is on purpose.

## The four lines at the end

Every chapter ends the same way, with four short lines. I will explain them once here so I do not have to keep doing it.

The first line, **Name it**, asks you to say, out loud and in your own words, what the chapter touched. Men are good at nodding and bad at saying "that is me." Nothing in a man moves until he says it.

**Do this week** gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday. One thing. If you do only that from each chapter, you will have done more with this book than most men do with any book.

**Tell a brother** asks you to say it to another man. A mark you keep to yourself does not harden into anything. If you do not have a brother yet, **A Man With No Brothers** is for you. Until you get there, telling a brother can mean writing it where you will see it, and telling God, who has heard worse.

**Carry this** hands you one line for the week. The kind of line a man might send to a friend without feeling preached at.

One more thing, because I know how a tired man reads a book like this. You will reach a chapter having not done the last chapter's thing. When that happens, do not skip it in shame and do not quit the book. Tell your brother you did not do it. That is itself the tell-a-brother line. Then do it this week. Every man in these pages has missed a week. The ones who changed are the ones who said so and went again.

## The men you'll meet

You will meet men in these pages by name. Roy, who turned in a state trooper's badge. Tomás, whose baby does not sleep. Kenny, divorced, who calls his son every Tuesday. Micah, who sat at the single men's table at weddings for six years. Anthony in a doorway. Walt with a shop that got bigger than his house. Others. They are composites. The conversations happened, at real tables, with real men. The names and the details have been mixed so nobody can be picked out, and so you might recognize yourself without recognizing anyone else.

## One more thing before you start

This book stands on its own. Read it. Do one thing a week. Find one other man to do it with. That is enough for a man to change.

If there is no other man yet, do not wait on him to start. Write it down. Tell God. Let the brother catch up when he shows.

We also built a twenty-four-week training on the same spine. Videos, printable guides, and a class a church or a small group or a man alone can run. In the back of this book you will find a short map of those weeks. If you want the full class, the guide is at walksteadfast.com. The book is the book. The training is the training. Use either. Use both. Do not binge four chapters on a Saturday and call it finished.

Find your coffee. Pull up a chair. Let me tell you about a Tuesday.
