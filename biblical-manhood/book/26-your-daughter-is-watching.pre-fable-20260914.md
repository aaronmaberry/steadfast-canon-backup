# 26. Your Daughter Is Watching

Anthony's daughter is seven, and she watches his face when her mother walks into a room.

He didn't know it until his wife told him. She'd been noticing it for months: the girl at the kitchen table, or on the floor with her crayons, glancing up at her father the moment her mother came through the door, not at her mother but at him, watching what his face did.

"She's learning what a man's face does when a woman walks in," his wife said. "She's learning it from you."

Anthony told me he didn't sleep much that night, because he knew what his face had been doing for a couple of years, which wasn't cruelty but nothing at all, a glance up and a glance back down at the phone. And his daughter, who isn't going to remember a single sermon she hears before she's twelve, was memorizing that.

Your daughter is watching. That's the whole chapter, and I could stop there, but I want to tell you what she's watching for, because she's learning three things from you that she'll carry to her grave, and she's learning all of them before you say a word.

## The three lessons

The first thing she's learning is what a man is. Not from books or movies or her friends' fathers; from you. You are the definition. Whatever you are, that's what "man" means to her, and every man she meets for the rest of her life will be measured against it, for better or for worse. If you're gentle, she'll expect gentleness and be startled by harshness. If you're absent, she'll expect absence and be suspicious of a man who shows up. If you're a storm, she'll either marry a storm or spend her life flinching at weather.

The second thing she's learning is what a woman deserves, and she's learning it from how you treat her mother, every single day. How you speak to her. How you look at her. Whether you defend her, thank her, touch her gently, listen when she talks. Whether you roll your eyes. Whether you help without being asked. Whether you go quiet for three days. Your daughter is taking notes on what a woman can expect from a man, and she's going to choose a husband with those notes in her hand. If you want to know what kind of man your daughter will marry, look at how you treated her mother this week. That's the man.

The third thing she's learning is what she's worth, and she's learning it from how you look at her. Whether you're delighted in her or merely tolerating her. Whether you see her or see past her. Whether your attention has to be earned by performance, by grades and goals and pretty, or whether it's given simply because she's yours. A girl who is delighted in by her father grows up knowing her worth isn't up for negotiation. A girl who isn't will spend her teens and twenties looking for a man to tell her what her father didn't, and the men who volunteer for that job are almost never the ones you'd choose.

Three lessons, all taught before breakfast, every day, whether or not you mean to teach them.

## What she needs from you

Let me get practical, because "be a good father to your daughter" is a sentence everybody agrees with and nobody knows how to do on a Tuesday.

She needs delight, and she needs to see it on your face when she walks in, and delight is different from approval. Approval is for what she did; delight is for who she is. God does this with His people, and it's the picture I'd give every father of a daughter.

:::margin
Zephaniah 3:17 (NIV)
"The LORD your God is with you, the Mighty Warrior who saves. He will take great delight in you; in his love he will no longer rebuke you, but will rejoice over you with singing."
:::

He will take great delight in you. He will rejoice over you with singing. That's a Father whose face changes when His child walks in, and yours can change too. Look up, and put the phone down, and let her see that you're glad she exists. You don't have to say anything; it's on your face or it isn't, and she's reading it.

She needs to see her mother honored. I've said it, and it's the biggest one, so I'll say it plainer. The most important thing you will ever do for your daughter is love her mother where your daughter can see it. Speak to your wife the way you want a man to speak to your girl in twenty years. Thank her at the table, take her side, touch her shoulder in the kitchen. Apologize to her in front of the children when you were wrong. Every one of those is a lesson your daughter is writing down.

If you're divorced, this lesson doesn't stop. It gets harder and more important at the same time. Your daughter is watching how you treat her mother across two houses, at the exchange, on the phone, when her mother is late. She's learning whether a man honors a woman even when it's over, even when it hurts, even when he has reasons not to. Kenny said the hardest thing he ever did for his daughter was speak well of her mother in his own truck when the girl was trying to get him to take her side. "I said, 'Your mom loves you and she's doing her best and I'm not going to talk about her that way.' My daughter looked at me like I'd grown a second head. Then she relaxed. I think she'd been afraid she had to choose."

She needs protection, and here you should remember the second verb in Genesis 2:15, which is to keep and guard the garden. A father is a keeper, and his daughter is the most precious thing in the garden.

Protection is not control. A father who locks his daughter in a tower to keep her safe teaches her that she's fragile and that men are jailers. Protection is a fence with a gate, and a father standing at the gate who knows who's coming through. It means you know her friends' names and their parents. It means you know what's on her phone and who's texting her, and you've told her why. It means the boy who wants to take her somewhere meets you first, looks you in the eye, and understands without your saying it that she is not unaccompanied in this world. It means you've told her, plainly, before she needs it, what a man does when a boy puts his hands where he shouldn't: she says no, she leaves, she calls you, and you come. And protection means protecting her from you: from your temper, from your sarcasm, from your absence, from your disappointment landing on her because you had a bad day. Paul told fathers exactly what not to do.

:::margin
Ephesians 6:4 (NIV)
"Fathers, do not exasperate your children; instead, bring them up in the training and instruction of the Lord."
:::

Do not exasperate, Paul says, which means don't wear them out with your unpredictability, your harshness, your rules that change with your mood. A daughter exasperated by her father learns that men are exhausting, and she'll either avoid them or resign herself to one. She needs your words. What you call her, she'll become. Chapter 18 said God gave men the power to name, and nowhere does that power land harder than on a daughter. Call her beautiful, and mean her whole self and not only her face. Call her strong, and call her kind when she's been kind, and call her yours. Say "I'm proud of you" when she did nothing to earn it, simply because you are. Say "I love you" out loud every day. There are fathers who have never said those three words to their daughters and think it's understood, and it isn't; it's needed, and if she doesn't hear it from you, there's a boy at fifteen who'll say it to get something, and it will work, because she's been starving for it. Job did something at the end of his life that was unheard of in his world.

:::margin
Job 42:15 (NIV)
"Nowhere in all the land were there found women as beautiful as Job's daughters, and their father granted them an inheritance along with their brothers."
:::

He granted them an inheritance along with their brothers, in a culture that didn't. He looked at his girls and said: you count, fully, the same as the boys, and I'm putting my name and my wealth behind that. That's a father using his words and his weight to tell his daughters what they're worth.

She needs your time. Delight without time is only a sentiment. She needs you, regularly, on the calendar, with nothing else in the room. A breakfast on Saturday, or a drive to school with the radio off, or a walk. Tomás started taking his baby daughter for a walk every evening at six, just the two of them, when she was three months old, and his wife thought it was sweet and a little pointless. He's still doing it; she's two now. "She talks the whole time," he said. "She's been talking to me since before she could talk." That's a girl who's going to grow up knowing her father wants her company.

She needs your steadiness when she pushes you away. Somewhere between twelve and sixteen, most daughters go through a stretch when they don't want much to do with their fathers. She'll roll her eyes and prefer her room and be embarrassed by you. And a lot of fathers, hurt, withdraw, which is the worst thing they could do. She isn't pushing you away because she doesn't need you. She's testing whether you'll stay, so stay. Keep showing up at the door. Keep the Saturday breakfast even when she sits there in silence. Keep saying "I love you" to a closed door. When she comes out the other side, she'll remember who stayed.

She needs your faith, lived where she can see it. Pray for her by name and tell her you do. Pray with her at bedtime, even when she's fifteen and pretends to hate it. Let her see you read the Bible at the kitchen table. Let her see you go to church when you don't feel like it. Let her see you apologize to God out loud. A daughter who has watched her father kneel knows what a man on his knees looks like, and she'll know to look for one.

And she needs your eyes to stay home. She notices where they go: at the pool, at the restaurant, on the screen. A father whose eyes wander teaches his daughter two things, that women are to be looked at and that she'll be compared. A father whose eyes stay home teaches her that a man can be faithful and that she can expect it.

## If she's grown

Roy's daughter was forty-one when he said the sentence in her driveway, the one from chapter 12: "I wasn't the father you needed. I'm sorry." She's a grown woman with children of her own, and it still mattered.

A daughter never stops watching. She's watching at forty to see whether her father has changed. She's watching how he treats her mother in retirement. She's watching whether he'll show up for her children the way he didn't for her. And a grown daughter still needs her father's blessing, his words, his delight. Most men think that window closed at eighteen, and it didn't. Roy's daughter told him, a year after the driveway, that the thing she'd needed most in her twenties was to hear him say he was proud of her, and that she'd given up on it, and that hearing it at forty-one had "fixed something I didn't know was still broken."

If you're a grandfather, you have a second chance, and it isn't only about the grandchildren. Your daughter is watching how you grandfather. Every time you show up for her children, you're telling her something about how you wish you'd shown up for her, and she hears it. Be the man with time now, because you've got it.

## If you're the man in the truck on Tuesday night

Kenny, and every divorced father. You can't be at the kitchen table every morning, and I know that. Here's what you can do, and it's more than you think.

Never speak against her mother, not once, not when your daughter baits you, not when it's true, not when you're tired. Your daughter is half her mother, and every word against the mother lands on the girl.

Be there for everything you're allowed to be there for, and some things you have to fight for. The recital, the game, the parent night. She's counting, and she won't say so.

Call on a schedule. Tuesday at 7:30, every Tuesday, even when she says "nothing" to every question. Kenny's daughter said "nothing" for about eight months. Then one Tuesday she talked for forty minutes. "It was because I'd called the other thirty-four Tuesdays," he said.

Make your house a home she lives in and not a bachelor pad she visits. Her own room, her own things, her drawings on the refrigerator. When she's with you, she's home.

And show her a man who's steady when the world isn't. Your divorce shook her world. A father who's steady, present, honorable, and growing is the ground she stands on while it shakes. You may be the empty chair at her mother's table. You can be the full one at yours.

## If you have no daughter

Single man, or father of sons only, or man whose daughter is grown and gone: there are girls in your church who don't have any of this. Their fathers left, or they're there and they're storms, or they're there and gone. And those girls are learning what a man is from whoever happens to be around.

Be a man they can watch. That means honoring the women in your church where the girls can see it. It means coaching, teaching Sunday school, being the uncle who shows up, and doing all of it in the light: never alone with a girl who isn't yours, always visible, always above reproach, so that what she learns from you is that a good man is safe and honorable and keeps his distance appropriate. That isn't a restriction on your usefulness. It's part of what you're teaching her.

I've watched a single man in his thirties become the reason three fatherless girls in a small church know what a man looks like when he's kind to a woman. He didn't do anything dramatic. He treated their mothers with respect, showed up when he said he would, and never once made any of them uncomfortable. They're watching him the way Anthony's daughter watches her father. He knows it, and he carries it like a man.

## The face at the door

Anthony changed what his face does. He told me it was harder than it sounds, because he had to actually feel something when his wife walked in, and he'd been numb for a couple of years, and you can't fake delight in front of a seven-year-old, because she'll know. So he had to go back to chapter 22 and study his wife again, and to chapter 23 and lay some things down, and the delight came back slowly, the way feeling comes back to a hand that's been asleep.

His daughter still watches his face. He said she's started smiling when she does. Jesus was tender with daughters. When a synagogue leader's little girl died, He went into the room and took her by the hand.

:::margin
Luke 8:54 (NIV)
"But he took her by the hand and said, 'My child, get up!'"
:::

My child, He called her, and He took her hand. That's the Man you're following, and He was gentle with a girl who wasn't His, and He gave her back to her father. Your daughter is watching. Give her something worth seeing.

## Before you turn the page

If you've read this chapter and realized your face has been teaching your daughter the wrong thing, I don't want you to leave it in shame, because shame is exactly what a father shows on his face when he's ashamed, and she'll read that too. Anthony's delight came back slowly, the way feeling comes back to a hand that's been asleep, and it came back because he went and did the work with his wife first. The Man you're following took a little girl by the hand and gave her back to her father. May the Father whose face changes when His children walk in change yours this week, and may your daughter catch you being glad she exists.
