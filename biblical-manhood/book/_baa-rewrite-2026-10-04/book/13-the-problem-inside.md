# 13. The Problem Inside

:::epigraph
All these evils come from inside and defile a person.
Mark 7:23
:::

Cody's first truck had a bubble on the front fender about the size of his thumbnail. Every spring he sanded it to bright steel, primed it, sprayed on the closest match the parts store sold, and told himself it was handled. By every fall it was back, wider, with a couple of smaller ones gathered around it. Third year, he gave up and drove it to a body shop. The man didn't say a word about Cody's handiwork. He pushed on the bubble with his thumb and the fender gave like soggy cardboard. Rusty water dribbled out. "Paint's not going to fix that," he said. "It's coming from the inside." Behind the panel, where none of the touch-ups had ever reached, the steel had been rotting all along. Cody had spent three years making the front of the problem look nice.

This is the hinge of the book. We've spent a lot of pages on what got handed to you, fathers who stayed or left, a blessing no one spoke, brothers who weren't there, older men you never went to. All of it shaped you. But I know men who've done every bit of that work. They left home. They asked the older man. They named the empty chair. Then they walked into the next room and wrecked it anyway. If this book ended with your father, I'd be giving you a can of primer and a pat on the back. The trouble is in you. It's in me. Scripture has a one-syllable word for it, and as long as you won't put that word on yourself, you'll keep sanding.

It's sin. And men find two ways around saying it. The first way is to trade it for something gentler. Struggle. Baggage. An issue. A struggle is weather, and it lands on you. Sin is a choice, and you did it. Weather you manage. A choice you repent of. The second way belongs to men who heard the word used on them like a belt when they were boys. I'm sorry that happened. Those preachers never owned it, though. The word is God's, and when He says it, it's a doctor reading an X-ray, not a man winding up a punch. The body man wasn't insulting Cody. He was the first person to tell him the truth about his truck.

Sin bends a man inward. Left alone, it curls him around himself like a fist. Before Cain lifted a hand against anybody, God told him sin was crouched at his door like something hungry, and that he had to rule over it. That warning cuts two ways. It's yours to answer for. It also isn't stronger than the God who's telling you to stand against it. Cain shrugged Him off, the crouching thing got him, and Abel never came home from the field. Same animal sits at every man's door.

Give yourself the dad you always wanted. Home at six. Gentle and strong. Said he loved you and you never doubted it. Put a hand on your shoulder at eighteen and blessed you. Surrounded you with brothers and found you an older man to walk behind. You'd be steadier for it. You'd still lie to your wife about what the card got used for. You'd still take the second look. You'd still keep that private list where you rank a notch above the man in the next pew. Adam is the proof. No wound, no bad father, a flawless garden, honest work, a wife who was pure gift. And he reached for the fruit. What's broken in a man didn't begin with his dad. It passed through his dad, from Eden on down, and the best home in the world couldn't have filtered it out. Jeremiah called the heart crooked beyond a man's own repair. Jesus told men who polished their outsides daily that every evil they could name came out of the heart, from inside, and that's what fouls a man.

Your father, the culture, the wound, those may have opened doors. They didn't build what came through them. A newborn's sleepless months work the same way. They strip a man down to what was already there, but they don't put it there. Here's why that's mercy and not just a hard word. If every bit of it was done to you, you're stuck, because there's no getting a new father. And you've got an excuse that never runs out. You can't repent of your dad's sin. You can only stew in it, repeating Adam's line about whoever God gave him. But when you own what's yours, God has something to forgive and something to rebuild. He isn't offering you a tidier account of your childhood. He's offering you a new heart. If you're divorced, I'm not going to hand you the whole blame. God has the whole picture, and so do the two of you. You do have a share, and the healing starts when "I did this" can stand without "but she" stapled to the back. Paul said God showed His love for us this way, that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us. He didn't wait for us to get our story straight. Use His word, then. The cure travels with that word.

Watch him in the church foyer. He wants to know what you thought of the message. He spots the new guy alone by the coffee and walks over, and the welcome is warm because he means it. Now follow him home. Somewhere between the front door and the stove his voice crosses a border. Same shirt. Same ring. The weather in that house is something else entirely. That's what the trouble looks like on a man. It doesn't need a wreck. A nice finish works fine, and being capable works even better. Sin is cut to fit men in particular ways, and if you can't recognize the pattern, you'll stand at the bathroom sink every morning and tell the guy shaving that he's all right.

One shape is the locked drawer. A second set of books. A search history. A friendship he describes at home with the wrong word. Sometimes there's no object, just a fear he's posted No Trespassing over. A boy's secret corner becomes a man's bolted room, and that's where things go bad. If you're single, give a brother your passwords and permission to ask, and empty the drawer while it's nearly empty. Another is the fortress. How are you? Fine. Need anything? No. The whole family leans on him and nobody gets leaned on in return. From the road it can pass for Pick Up the Weight, but a man carrying his own load still lets a brother grab the other end, and the fortress exists so that never happens. Gray in the beard makes it worse, because long years make a man expert at needing nothing.

Some men keep a scoreboard. In his head he's always tallying whose truck, whose title, whose wife, whose kids, whose bonus. Another man's good news stings, and his own life never feels like enough. Saul kept that tally, and it had him throwing spears at the young shepherd who'd just won his war for him. After a divorce the scoreboard moves right in, her place against yours, whose holiday table looked fuller. Put the board down. Your Father isn't comparing your table with hers. Some men stand at a podium. That's sin in its Sunday best, the kind Jesus seemed angriest at, gentle in the lobby and a thunderhead on the drive home. Some men sit on a throne. Every disagreement at home ends with him on top, by volume or a cold shoulder or a line that bruises. He calls it leadership. His wife would tell you her real job is handling him, and the kids read the mood of his truck pulling in the way ranch kids read a sky. Paul fenced the whole thing with one line when he told husbands to love their wives and not be harsh with them. He knew that husband was coming. With a new baby the throne gets loud, and she's more worn out than you, so apologize before bed every time. And some men take the recliner, laziness dressed up as rest, a tired guy cashing in an evening he figures he earned while the house pays in loose shingles and quiet kids. If you're winning at work, notice whether your title has started answering questions only your soul should.

Sin lies to whoever has it on, so use checks that don't run on feelings. What would you hate for a brother to see? Phone, history, accounts, calendar, that one friendship. Having a drawer at all is the problem. When did someone last tell you no and you took it? If you can't think of a time, you're up on a throne. Who are you in the lobby, and who are you alone in the cab at eleven on a Tuesday night? The distance between those two men is the act. And leave respect out of it for a minute. Who in your house is scared of you? If any name comes to mind, step down, and get on your knees in front of the people who've had to learn your weather.

One man in the Bible wore several of these in a single season, and nobody in his generation stood taller. If David could go down this road, you can. It started with staying home. Spring was fighting season, and David had always ridden out with his men. This year he sent his general and the army and stayed in Jerusalem, his own boots by the door. Nobody in the palace would have called that sin, since the king had put in his years. If you want the spot where a mark first gave way, it's here. Stop Waiting was the call, and David sat it out. Empty hands, long evenings, and one of those evenings he went up to pace the palace roof. You know what a bored man and a blank evening can get into.

He saw a woman bathing and asked who she was. Bathsheba, he was told, wife of Uriah, one of his own soldiers, who was out at the siege that very night. David sent for her anyway. When a king summons you, saying no isn't really on the table. He took her, because in that hour the hour was all that mattered to him. Weeks later word came that she was pregnant. David pulled Uriah back from the front, poured him wine, and steered him toward home so the baby could be passed off as his. Uriah slept at the palace door instead, out of loyalty to friends sleeping on the ground near the enemy. So David sent him back with a sealed letter for Joab. Put him where it's hottest, then fall back. Uriah carried his own death order to the wall. Once the mourning was done, David moved the widow into the palace, and to anyone at court it looked settled. The Bible adds one line. What David had done displeased the Lord.

The kingdom hummed, and hardly anybody knew. For close to a year David ran Israel with a dead soldier hidden and the soldier's wife under his roof, and by every outward measure he ran it well. That's where skill and holiness part company. A church, a business, or a family can look solid while the man at the center goes soft with rot. David wrote about that year later. His body wasted, and God's hand pressed on him day and night. A good lock doesn't keep the drawer from working on you. A man can run a kingdom well and rot inside it.

Conscience never won on its own. God sent Nathan. He began with a story about a rich man with whole herds who took a poor neighbor's one pet lamb to feed a guest. David came out of his chair. Then Nathan pointed and told him he was that man. A few plain words, spoken to a king's face in his own hall. Men rarely blow their own cover. Usually a wife stops keeping quiet, or a brother won't accept "fine," or a bank statement lands on the counter. David's road was the ordinary one. Everybody needs a Nathan, and every man ought to be willing to be one. That's why you sit with brothers. Someday one of them will have to look across at you, enthroned and comfortable, and point.

Then David answered. He said he had sinned against the Lord, and he stopped there. One short sentence, and a period. No mention of where she chose to bathe. No complaint about Uriah. No speech about the weight of the crown. A confession ends where the sentence ends. Nathan told him on the next breath that the Lord had taken away his sin. The consequences came too, and they were brutal, but David didn't have to outlast them before he was right with God. The drawer opened, and he was restored.

Wade's turn came in a twenty-minute check-in. Another man asked him what was weighing on him. He opened his mouth to say the job, then didn't. A long quiet. The evening before, his little girl had gone small in front of him. He hadn't hit her. He hadn't needed to. His voice climbed, she shrank, and he saw his own boyhood in her face. For years he'd filed that under temper. Sitting there, he called it sin. The man across from him didn't look away. He said thank you. He asked what Wade was going to do about it. Then he prayed. Wade had expected to feel stripped. He felt washed, and he couldn't remember the last time. James tells us to confess our sins to each other and pray for each other so we can be healed. Notice the word healed. Forgiven is a verdict. Healed is what happens to a man after the verdict, when somebody who loves him has heard the worst and stayed in his chair.

Paul wrote down every man's week. I want the good thing and don't do it. I hate the bad thing and do it again. You promised yourself patience with the kids and you barked. Then he asks who will rescue him. A who. You don't get rescued by a method. I've watched a lot of men run programs, and most of them land on try harder, which is just primer with a new label. A capable man can run his life on skill for years, and a polished résumé still isn't a pure heart. What holds is plainer. Say it to God, out loud, specifically. Not "I've had a rough stretch." I lied to her about the money. Reading the Sunday confession along with everyone isn't the same as naming your own sin alone in the cab, and a man who's kept the paint fresh for fifty years can forget there's steel underneath. David said that keeping quiet dried him out to the bone, and that when he finally spoke, forgiveness met him. One sentence stood between the two.

Tell guilt and shame apart. Guilt points at the deed. That was wrong, and it was me. Shame points at you. You are the wrong thing. Guilt is the rusty water on the fender, ugly and accurate, and it settles once you've said it. Shame never settles. It keeps up its lies and herds a man back to his temper, his bottle, his screen, or his long silence, the very things he leans on to dull it. Then change direction. Feeling rotten can go on for years without the feet moving. Repenting moves them. Confess. Repair what you can. Put something in the way of tomorrow's door. Get back to your work. Do it again tomorrow if tomorrow needs it. From the inside, following Jesus looks a lot like a son who keeps drifting and a Father who keeps opening the door.

And ask for a new interior. Fresh off the worst thing he ever did, David didn't ask God to fix his situation. He asked God to make a pure heart in him and put a steadfast spirit back inside him. Rot coming from behind the steel shrugs off another coat. The panel has to be replaced, and only God does that kind of bodywork, at any age. Jesus had no drawer. Nothing in Him was held back from His Father. On the night of His arrest He reminded the high priest that every word He'd taught was spoken in public, and His accusers had to go looking for false witnesses. That's the man God is shaping in you, a man whose private and public start to match, so your wife and your brothers and your God can look straight at you without wincing.

The body man cut the rotten section out and welded in a new one. That fender stayed smooth. Cody stood in that shop feeling like a fool for three springs of wasted paint, and lighter than he'd felt in a while, because somebody had finally said the true thing, and it had a fix. Letting go of the case against your dad and naming your own sin goes about the same way. A short sting of foolishness, then a deep relief. Let your Nathan in. Confess, and stop at the period. The One who saw behind your panel all along has already covered the cost. Paint was never the fix. Name what's inside you. The cure comes with the name, and it's already paid for.

## Your Turn

**Name it**

Tell God, out loud, with the real thing in the sentence. "I sinned. I ___." Leave "struggle" out of it. Then pick your shape, the drawer, the fortress, the scoreboard, the podium, the throne, or the recliner, and point to where it showed up in the real hours of this past week. Whoever has the shape on is always the last to see it.

**Do this week**

Each night, right before sleep, give God two minutes and name that day's sins one at a time. Thank Him, since the forgiveness is already yours in Christ, and notice when shame tries to take over the conversation. Once this week, ask your wife where she sees you hiding and where you come across harsh. Single? Ask a brother. Don't defend yourself. Thank them.
