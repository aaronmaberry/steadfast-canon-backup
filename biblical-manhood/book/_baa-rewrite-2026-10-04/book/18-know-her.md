# 18. Know Her

:::epigraph
Husbands, in the same way be considerate as you live with your wives, and treat them with respect as the weaker partner and as heirs with you of the gracious gift of life, so that nothing will hinder your prayers.
1 Peter 3:7
:::

On the windowsill over his sink there's a spiral notebook. When the wrens returned this spring, he logged it that same morning, the date, the weather, which box they chose. He can hear one note through the glass and tell you chickadee or nuthatch. He's been at it since boyhood. But if you asked him what scares his wife this year, all those pages wouldn't help him. Years of meals across from her. He could point to her chair blindfolded. Somewhere along the way he started thinking the chair was her. Before we talk about a husband laying his life down, there's something underneath it. You have to know the woman you married.

Start with how Jesus does it, because that's the pattern. He called Himself the good shepherd, the one who knows His sheep and is known by them. Christ knows His church down to the name, the fear, the old wound, the thing she's never said out loud. And what He knows doesn't run Him off. He stays, keeps drawing it up into the light, and the sheep end up knowing Him back. That's what a husband is aiming at. Not a file on her. A shepherd's attention that doesn't quit.

Peter wrote the line at the top of this chapter straight at husbands. Be considerate. Older translations stay nearer the Greek and say dwell with them according to knowledge. Live in that house as a man still learning the woman in it. Most men read right past the end of the verse. So that nothing will hinder your prayers. A man who lives with a woman and won't bother understanding her has a problem bigger than the marriage. It reaches God. How you know her and how heaven hears you sit in the same sentence. Men will pore over the things they love for decades. Fishing. Engines. Box scores. Plenty of husbands studied their wives just long enough to get along, shut the book, and have run on those early notes ever since. She kept changing. He kept the notes. Sharing an address won't do the studying for you, and a woman can sleep beside a man for thirty years and remain unknown to him. Stop Waiting, in a marriage, looks like study.

:::break
:::

Ask, then wait. Most of us ask and start loading our answer before she's through her first sentence. When she stops, count to ten in your head. She probably isn't done. The true part tends to come after the pause. James says to be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to get angry, and a husband could build a whole marriage on the first part. Listen before you fix. Everything in you will want to solve it. Sit on that. Tell her you're still with her and ask what else. A man who repairs every sentence teaches his wife to stop handing him sentences. A man who listens gets let all the way in. Watch her. What she does when she's worn out. What gets a real laugh out of her when nobody's performing. Where she turns when she's low. Write things down. Her sizes. Her friends' names. Something she wanted and mentioned once, back in March. The date her dad passed. The fear she finally admitted. When a thing she said one time comes back to her from you months later, she notices.

Ask her what you get wrong about her. Don't defend yourself. Take notes. Thirty minutes of that beats a year of guessing. And pick one night a week to ask her something you can't answer for her. When did you feel most like yourself this week? What do you wish I'd noticed? Take your turn too, so it's a conversation and not an interview.

"Know your wife" is advice men nod at and never act on, so let's get specific. Learn her story. How her dad was with her, and what that taught her to expect from men. What happened before you that she's never told you all of. Ask her to tell it slow. You'll finally see why she flinches at things you'd filed under nothing. Then her actual fears. Ask what scares her most right now, then be quiet. Given room, most wives name something that was nowhere on the husband's list. It's rarely money. It's being left. It's the kids. It's turning into her mother. It's whether he really sees her. Find out what fills her up and what drains her. A packed room lights some women up and empties others out. Plan the wrong weekend enough times and you'll start calling her difficult, when you just weren't paying attention.

Notice how love lands on her. Men tend to give the love they'd want back, doing, fixing, providing, handling it. Some wives feel that. Others feel it somewhere smaller, when you remembered the name of the coworker who's been wearing her down, or you saw she'd gone quiet at the party and took the long way home. Ask her when she's felt most loved by you. Then do that again. Know her body too, without getting cute about it. Her cycle, so you pick up more in the week she has less. Pregnancy. The months after a baby. The later changes plenty of husbands sail right past. She lives in that body every hour. Ask about her walk with God, how she prays, what she's wrestling with, which verse she's holding on to, and then you can pray for something real instead of a foggy "bless her." Get to know her ordinary day, hour by hour. Three in the afternoon with the kids. The worst stretch of her shift. A man who's never asked will say things that land like a slap and then wonder why she went quiet. And find out about her people, her friends, her mom, and what those ties cost her, and what she still wants, what she set down, what she quit mentioning because nobody asked. Honor those. Look for ways to hand a few of them back. Proverbs says the purposes of a heart are deep water, and a man of understanding draws them out. She won't float all of it up for you. Drawing it out takes attention, attention takes time, and time takes deciding she's worth it.

:::break
:::

New baby in the house? Then the woman you married is hauling a load you can't see. Months of broken sleep, maybe. Sorrow over the life she had before, and guilt for feeling that sorrow. Shrink your question down to the next sixty minutes. What would help right now? At 3 a.m. nobody can answer the big ones. Keep track of the basics for her, food, a shower, some sunlight, one laugh. If she pulls away from your touch, take her tiredness at its word. It's the load talking, and loads lift. Pay attention, too, for a darkness that runs deeper than fatigue. Doctors see it all the time and know how to treat it, and the husband is often the one who spots it first. Weeks of her not seeming like herself. Lying awake while the baby finally sleeps. Saying things about herself that scare you. Don't wait it out. Make the appointment and sit in the room with her. Knowing your wife includes guarding her. A mother of a newborn once said a sentence I wish every husband had taped to his mirror. "I do not need you to fix it. I need you to know how hard this is."

If you're in the middle stretch, juggling jobs and kids and a mortgage, odds are you haven't updated much since the honeymoon. Whatever question you've been avoiding is probably the one the marriage needs. Married a long time? Then the risk is that you know who she used to be. Your picture of her froze somewhere back there while she kept on living, with a body that changed, new worries, and losses piled on losses. Ask what she hopes for in the time you've got left. Be ready to be surprised.

If the marriage is over and you can see now that you never really knew her, let that be part of what you own when you say "I did this." You're not done learning her, either. Raising kids together without a war means knowing their mother, her worries for them, what lights her fuse, what brings her back down. And if God gives you a wife again someday, open the notebook on the first day. Single? Get your reps with the women God has already put around you, treating them as sisters. Ask your mom something real and don't interrupt. Do the same with your sister, or a sister in Christ at church. Or ask a married woman you serve alongside, with her husband right there, what she wishes men understood. No fixing, no flirting, just listening. The man who learns to hear his sister is learning to hear a wife.

The man with the bird book finally asked her. Nothing clever. What are you afraid of this year? She gave the easy answer first. He waited it out, and she kept going. He wrote while she talked. Then he turned to a fresh page and put her name across the top. He's still filling it in. He told me it felt like meeting her all over again, in the same house where they'd stood side by side the whole time. Those years beside her weren't the study. The study is the page you keep open. Feeling late to this? Nearly every husband is, and late isn't too late. She's watching to see if you'll ask. The Shepherd who calls each of His sheep by name can make you patient enough to keep lowering the bucket, one question at a time. She kept living after you stopped taking notes. Ask her one more question.
