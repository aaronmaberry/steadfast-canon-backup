# 9. Anger, the Itch, and the Drift

:::epigraph
"In your anger do not sin": Do not let the sun go down while you are still angry, and do not give the devil a foothold.
Ephesians 4:26-27
:::

Owen found out on a Monday that the fall contract had gone to someone else. He'd already spent that money in his head. He ran the numbers twice, closed the laptop, and told nobody, God included, in any honest way. He was scared, and he put the fear where men usually put it. A couple of nights later he spent forty minutes locked in the bathroom with his phone. Nothing on that screen wanted anything from him, which was the whole point. That weekend he went flat on the couch under a ballgame, and when his wife tried to plan July around her sister's offer to take the kids, he gave her "whatever you think" from a long way off. And when his daughter loaded the dishwasher wrong, he shut it so hard a glass bounced off the top rack, and the kitchen went silent the way it had learned to. Ask Owen and he'd have called it a rough week and a short fuse. It was three escapes from the same fear.

You've already seen that an empty chair never stays empty. Pain is like that. It won't sit still in a man. It leaves, and it has three ways out. Anger throws it outward. Appetite pulls it downward. Absence carries it off somewhere quiet. Under pressure a man grabs the escape he trusts, and after twenty years he calls it his personality. Way back, a boy figured out how to stop feeling something, and the trick became a groove. Block the groove he likes and the pain cuts a new one. Owen used all three in under a week. I've felt every one of these tug at me. I won't soften any of them, and I won't use them to shame you. Shame is lighter fluid on all three.

:::break
:::

Absence is the soft escape. I keep the closest eye on it in men past their peak working years, because nothing looks wrong and nobody's sounding an alarm. The drifting man leaves without going anywhere. He shows up at home and at work. Nothing has exploded, nothing has been found out. He's just not there. His wife quit asking after the answers dried up into "fine." His kids learn that a father can occupy a couch and still be missing. The chair we looked at earlier, the one that looked occupied and wasn't, gets built exactly this way.

There's usually an injury under it. Somewhere along the way, without saying so, he decided caring was too expensive. Or he was truly worn out, and resting turned by inches into withdrawing. Solomon watched it happen to a house. Laziness lets the rafters sag, he wrote, and idle hands let the roof leak. No one stomped through that ceiling. It leaked because the ladder stayed in the garage and the recliner stayed occupied. By the time someone says out loud that you've drifted, the water stain is already spreading.

If this is your escape, own it to your wife, or to whoever shares your roof. "I'm sorry. My body's been here and I haven't." None of it is news to her. Hearing it from you shifts the whole house. No wife? Tell a brother, since unwitnessed drift just keeps going. Then win back one habit. One evening a week, screens dark and your mind present. One question a day, and you stay until the answer is all the way out. And go find the boy who quit caring because it cost too much. A brother or a good counselor can help you look. That's the ladder.

The downward escape is appetite. Sex has the strongest hold, so I'll spend my time there, though the same itch drives the drink, the online cart, the betting app, and the feed that never hits bottom. Comfort on a short fuse. No demands. No witnesses. Behind that bathroom door, Owen wasn't really chasing pleasure. He was chasing relief. Relief from the numbers. Relief from the hush he'd caused in his own kitchen. The screen asked nothing of him, least of all to be a man. That's the hook, a place where nobody needs you. And the lie lives right next to it, because being needed as a man is the very thing that would start to heal him.

James describes the slide. A man is tempted, he says, when his own desire drags him off and baits the hook. Desire gets pregnant and gives birth to sin, and sin, once it's grown, gives birth to death. Dragged. That's how it feels. Nobody plans on two in the morning. And the death at the end is rarely a headline. It's a slow leak. His attention dies, then his hunger for an actual woman, then his ability to hold his wife's gaze. She knows something's off long before she could name it.

Paul's word to the church at Corinth was flee. Joseph sprinted out of a house because a woman inside wanted him. That's the speed. If this is your escape, the work starts with one brother and the full story, what and how often and how long and what it's cost. No hiding behind "I struggle with purity." This thing feeds on secrecy, and once another man knows, it begins to starve. Next, cut off the supply. Filters on every screen, passwords in his hands, your phone charging in the kitchen overnight. You'd do as much for a kid in middle school. Do it for the grown man who acts like one after everybody's asleep. Then go after the loneliness underneath, which was always the real issue. There's more on brothers further on. Short version, a man who's known itches less.

:::break
:::

Most men's favorite escape is anger, because anger looks like strength. Peel it back and there's usually fear, or shame, or plain helplessness underneath. Money that won't stretch. A particular thing he's ashamed of. Something he loves cracking where he can't reach. Every one of those feels like weakness in the gut, and anger feels like power, so he snatches it the way a freezing man snatches the nearest coat. That dishwasher didn't slam itself because of a bad load. It slammed because of a lost contract.

God's Word takes it for granted that you'll get angry. What it watches is what comes next. Paul gave the Ephesians a deadline, and you read it at the top of this chapter. Look where it starts. You're already mad. Then the deadline, sunset. Then the risk, a foothold. Sleep on it, wake up with it, carry it to bed again, and the enemy has wedged his boot in the doorway. Your temper becomes the climate your kids grow up in. If this is your escape, learn ninety seconds. A surge of anger, left unfed, peaks and starts falling in less than two minutes. So walk to the mailbox and back. Leave the loaded sentence in the chamber. When you come back inside, say the thing under it. "I'm scared about money." A wife can plan with a man who admits he's afraid. Settle it while it's still daylight. A man who learns that becomes the calmest person in his house.

The season changes the costume, but the doors stay unlocked. If you're the guy everyone says is killing it, look again. The itch can pass for ambition, and the drift can pass for being laid-back. Older men, watch for anger and absence traveling as a pair. They usually park themselves in front of the TV. One night this week, turn it off after supper, ask her one real question, and let her finish. The first stretch after a divorce can fling all three doors open at once. Rage aimed at her. A loneliness you never used to know, feeding appetite. Absence, because caring is how you got hurt. Healing, when it comes, often wears the face of an older brother who phones every Thursday and won't settle for "fine."

New dads, anger will ambush you. Three in the morning, a baby who won't stop, and a voice comes out of you that you last heard from your father. Take your ninety seconds. Then tell your wife the honest thing, that you're tired enough that you frighten yourself. Young and single, the itch is probably loudest. Keep an eye on the drift as well, a job you don't care about and every evening surrendered to scrolling. Numb at twenty-two isn't calm. It's a dashboard light.

:::break
:::

The angry man was wounded by fear as a boy, so he swapped it for something that passed for power. The man with the itch learned no one was going to see him, so he bought comfort that never asks his name. The drifting man learned what caring costs and closed the account. Different doors, one purpose, which is not to feel the chair. Different doors, one result. He's gone from his own house. Jesus never used any of these exits. He was angry without sinning. He was tempted in every way we are, and He never took the downward way out. The night before the cross, everything in Him wanted another road, and He said "not as I will," and stayed. If you've already gone out one of these doors, He can meet you on the other side and walk you home without shame. White-knuckling it hasn't worked, and you know that. Take the pain somewhere it can heal instead of somewhere it can go numb. There's a Father who stays. Your anger doesn't rattle Him. Your itch doesn't repel Him. He didn't drift away when you did.

One quiet night Owen put the numbers on the table in front of his wife. He told her he'd been afraid for three weeks, and that the fear had come out at the dishwasher, behind the bathroom door, and on the couch. She'd already sensed something. The fear itself she could stand inside with him. His absence was the part that had hurt them. She didn't say any of that gently. She was angry the first night, and not about the contract. She'd spent three weeks in a house with a man who wasn't in it. He took it without a defense. It was most of a week before the kitchen felt like theirs again. The good kind of quiet came back eventually, and he walked to the mailbox and back. He'd said the true sentence. The contract was still gone. He just wasn't hiding from it in his own house anymore.

Your escape has a name now, and you can hand a named thing to your Father. He'll meet you at the mailbox, at the screen, at the couch, and the first small no you say this week can turn into a habit. Every escape walks you out of your own house. Pain was looking for a Father, and an exit was the wrong address.
