# 26. What You Are Building When Nobody Claps

:::epigraph
God is not unjust; he will not forget your work and the love you have shown him as you have helped his people and continue to help them.
Hebrews 6:10
:::

In July around here, concrete crews start pouring foundations at four in the morning. They have to. Pour at noon and the slab cures too fast and cracks. So they work under lights, screed and float in the dark, and by sunup they're gone. All that's left is a flat gray rectangle in the dirt. Nobody photographs that. A family will live on it for thirty years without a thought. Kids will learn to walk on it. Couples will fight and make up on it. All of it sits on something poured while the neighborhood slept. A man's life is mostly slab. Out of sight, holding up everything you can see.

Start with Jesus. He lived thirty years before anybody outside Nazareth knew His name, and Luke covers the whole stretch in about a sentence. He went home to Nazareth with His parents and did what they told Him. The Son of God spent His twenties sanding boards in a town people made jokes about. No crowd. Then He waded into the Jordan, and the Father spoke out loud and called Him His beloved Son. One sentence of praise from heaven, followed by three years of being doubted and a cross at the end. He never went hunting for a crowd to make up the difference. He'd been seen for thirty years by the only eyes that mattered. That was enough. It can be enough for you.

Think back over the last seven days. The 2 a.m. bottle. The hotel where you shut the laptop and went to sleep. The lunch you've packed for your wife every morning for eleven years. The true answer at work that cost you, when a lie would have been easy. Nobody clapped. Nobody's going to. The world's answer is poison. If nobody saw it, it didn't happen. Scoreboards taught us that, and it's why men perform and drift toward whatever room is applauding. If you're single, almost nobody sees your evenings. If your job hands out applause on a schedule, home may be the only place nobody grades you. Either way, Jesus flatly disagreed with the scoreboard, more than once in the same sermon. Go into your room, He said, shut the door, and pray to your Father who is unseen, and your Father who sees what's done in secret will reward you. In the hidden thing, nobody can mistake the crowd for your motive. It's just a son and his Father. This is Work for God's Pay with nothing else mixed in, and the pay is the Father's well done and a son's inheritance, never wages. You know who was in the room.

Paul looked ahead to the day every man's work gets inspected, when fire will show what each man built and test what it was worth. Everyone builds with something. Build for applause and you're building with straw. From the highway, straw can look better than gold, because straw is bright and gold just sits there being heavy. Then the fire hits, and everything done for an audience is ash in a second. What survives is what you did for the Father when nobody was looking. That four a.m. pour is gold. The 2 a.m. bottle is gold. A sermon preached to be admired is straw. So is a donation that buys a plaque. So is the dad who's all in at the ballpark and checked out by the time the truck hits the driveway.

:::

Roy's grandson rides the school bus past a barn his great-grandfather raised. The barn's still up. Nobody on that bus can tell you who built it. That's legacy, something still standing. Towns forget, churches call new pastors, grandkids move to Denver. Build to be remembered and you'll be disappointed. Build so something stands, and you get your barn. After Roy turned in his badge, the hardest thing, he told me, was finding out how fast the people he'd served for thirty-one years had forgotten. Then it settled on him that it didn't matter. He'd told himself it was for them. It wasn't, really. It was his to do, and God was watching. Roy found Work for God's Pay at seventy. Someday Hollis's Thursday table will be forgotten too. The two men who sit at it will still be standing.

Look at what's going up on your slab. A floor under your kids. They won't remember one regular dinner out of a thousand, but their bones will know whether somebody was steady. A marriage still standing at seventy. Roy's has gone forty-four years, and it ran on coffee, not anniversaries. It got better once he retired and started making the coffee himself. When you meet an old couple who still like each other, you're looking at fifty years of pours in the dark. A name that gets there before you do. Reputation is what people learn later about what you did unwatched. A boy's first inheritance is his father's last name, and the credit a man gets at a feed store two counties over comes from forty years of bills paid on time that nobody else ever saw.

A church is on that slab too. It runs on men nobody thanks, the one stacking chairs, the one sitting with an old-timer in the hospital, the one who's taught fourth-grade boys for twenty-two years. The pastor gets the stage. The men who poured the floor under it are standing in the parking lot afterward, hands in their pockets, at peace. And so is a self. Men forget this one. Jesus said faithfulness in very little is how you get trusted with much, and the very little is the stuff nobody sees. Eli's hidden work this year was telling a customer the truth when a lie would have been free, and leaving his phone in the kitchen at night when nobody would have known. Then there's treasure in heaven, which is Jesus' name for where all of it went, kept by a Father who loses track of nothing.

There's a stretch in hidden work when you can't see a thing. The slab's down and no house goes up for months. A year of packed lunches and she still feels far away. Eight months of weekly calls to your son, and his answer to "what's new" is still "nothing." And the thought shows up that every hour was wasted. Isaiah's servant felt it. He said he'd worked for nothing and spent his strength for no reason, and then he said that what was owed him was in the Lord's hand anyway. He didn't pretend the weight away. He changed where he looked. Whatever is coming to him sits in God's hand, not in the results, and not with a teenage boy who says "nothing." Before the boy finally opened up, God had watched every one of the thirty-four calls. Paul says don't quit, because harvest comes in its season. Pecan season, not yours. Those trees of Mr. Pruitt's are knee-high. He'll never eat from them. It doesn't wear him down, because he never thought the pecans were his pay.

You've had big days. A wedding. A baptism. A morning at a creek when you spoke a name over your son. A retirement plaque and a handshake. Those are stones on the riverbank. You live on the ordinary days after, where nobody claps and everything gets decided. So a man needs a rhythm under the moments people see. Roy built his after the hardest marker of his life. He's still up at 5:30. The first hour goes to the Word, a chapter and a prayer that begins with "Father," before the phone gets touched. Then the floor, the family things that don't move, written where he'll see them. Twenty minutes with his brother in the same slot every week. One good thing done in secret. Before noon, a job somebody's counting on him for. A blessing, hand on a head, when there are kids in the house, and a call when they're grown and gone. A notebook with one line a day about something his wife said. At sundown he carries nothing to bed. The apology's made, the anger's handed to God. On Sunday he's in his Father's house as a man with a job there.

It shrinks to fit. Tomás has a four-month-old and ten-hour shifts. He gives the first fifteen minutes of the two-to-five feeding to a Psalm on his phone. His floor is twenty minutes after his boots come off and a prayer over the crib. At nine, dishwasher humming, he calls his brother. His secret good is letting his wife sleep an extra hour on Saturdays and never mentioning it. His sundown sounds like, "I'm so tired I scare myself, and I'm sorry, and I'm staying." No slack in that life. The rhythm lives in the cracks. Every-other-weekend dads, your card has its own lines. The hour before the kids wake up. The midweek call. The weekend you never trade. So write the card. Five lines or ten. That card is your slab, and everything people see sits on it.

God isn't unfair, Hebrews says. He won't forget your work, or the love you've shown Him by helping His people. So pour. Pack the lunch, make the call, and let your Father be the one who sees. Long quiet grinds on a man, so let Isaiah's servant have the last word one more time. What's due me is in the Lord's hand. Keep your rhythm seven days and tell no one. The Father who watched a carpenter's son in Nazareth is watching your pour this week, and He'll remember every day you kept. The crowd gets straw. The Father gets the gold you pour in the dark.
