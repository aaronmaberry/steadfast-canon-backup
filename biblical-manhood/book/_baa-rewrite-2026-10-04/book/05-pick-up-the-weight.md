# 5. Pick Up the Weight

:::epigraph
for each one should carry their own load.
Galatians 6:5
:::

Tomás had a broke Tuesday. The car payment drafts on the fourteenth. His paycheck lands on the fifteenth. Diapers and formula had already gone on the card, and even after picking up a Saturday shift he was forty dollars short. Sitting there with the laptop open, what he felt wasn't greed or even panic so much as a pull to hide. Close the screen. Let her stumble on it in the banking app sometime next week. Instead, after the baby finally went down, he stayed at the kitchen table, turned the laptop so she could see the numbers, and said, "That's mine. We're short." Then he walked her through what he'd cut. Nothing got solved that night. The math was just as ugly at bedtime. But she wasn't alone with it, and neither was he.

That's the second of the four marks, and it has a plain name, Pick Up the Weight. A steadfast man finds the part of the load that's his and gets his hands under it, even when it isn't fair, and even when somebody else dropped it, once it lands in his house or his lane. Paul put it about as short as a sentence goes. Each man should carry his own load.

Notice what Tomás said, and what he didn't. He didn't say, "I can see how you might be frustrated." He didn't open with how the bank times its drafts. He said, "That's mine." That's the whole move. Look at the person and claim it. Then stop. A period belongs right there. The instant "but" shows up, a second speech has started, and everybody listening hears the gears shift. What you're claiming is the outcome, not the intention. A wet spot spreading on the ceiling is an outcome. The Saturday you planned to get up on the roof is a story. If you tell your kids you meant to cook supper, you've described a plan. They'll describe an empty table.

:::break
:::

I've fought this. I've stood in my own kitchen and eased a load onto somebody else with one tidy sentence and told myself I was just being honest. We come by it naturally. The first thing a man said to God after the fall was a handoff. God asked what happened, and Adam pointed two directions at once, at the woman, and at God, since He's the One who gave her to him. His wife gets half, God gets the other half, and Adam walks off holding none of it. He got to his "but" before he'd finished one sentence. A generation later, Cain had the move down cold. The Lord asked him where his brother Abel was. "I don't know," Cain said. "Am I my brother's keeper?" The answer to Cain was yes, and it's still yes. Yes for the manager who says that's another department. Yes for the stepdad who says that's her kid's issue. Yes for "she knew what she signed up for" and "nobody ever told me."

Both men were protecting a story where the weight belonged to someone else. That story is called blame, and it makes an afternoon feel lighter for a while. Good men get stuck right here, because they confuse fault with ownership. Fault looks backward and asks who caused this. Ownership looks at today and asks whose it is now. You can carry zero fault for a layoff, a cancer diagnosis, a daughter's panic attacks, or a dad who left you nothing but his debts, and still find your name on every one. Fairness is a fine question for a judge. A load-bearing wall doesn't get to ask it.

Most houses have one wall that can't come out, and you can't tell by looking. Same paint, same nail holes from the old school pictures. But the ceiling joists all rest on it, and every bed and dresser and sleeping kid upstairs is riding on that wall. A contractor knocks, glances up, checks which way the floor runs, and points. That one. You can still open up the kitchen, but only after you set new support under the load. Pull the wall out bare and the second floor sinks a little at a time, until next year a bedroom door won't latch. Every family carries weight, and so does every job and every single life. It lands somewhere, fair or not.

And a household needs a floor that holds. A teenager who shoves back is testing whether it will, and the dad of a new driver is mostly carrying his own temper. A wife who finally says the hard thing is trusting you to stay planted while she says it. A divorced man is still the floor under two addresses. You can feel shaky and stay. When it gets loud, stay again. Nobody upstairs ever thinks about the beam. They just walk across the floor. That's the beam doing its job.

Plenty of men know the verse about providing, and most were taught it means a paycheck, full stop. Paul told Timothy that a man who won't provide for his own people, and especially his own household, has denied the faith. Look at "household." That's everyone under the roof, and money is a sliver of what a dad owes them. A man can bankroll a house he barely sets foot in. He can pay for ten years of piano lessons and leave his folding chair empty at the recital, and the empty chair is what his daughter remembers. Groceries are the floor of providing, not the ceiling. Showing up is part of it. Short on money, Tomás still gave what mattered most that night, himself, at the table with the bill.

:::break
:::

What's actually yours is a shorter list than you'd guess, but a handful of things won't negotiate. Your own life comes first. Your body, your bank account, your word, your habits, your soul. No volunteer will schedule your physical, pay off your card, or open your Bible for you. Those left your mother's hands years ago. Living alone, that's most of your load, and it's heavier than it sounds, since nobody sees a man quit in an empty apartment. If you share a home, that's next. A marriage runs warm or cool, and you answer for the trend. A new father is carrying a wife who hasn't slept in a week. Children under your roof are being shaped, and you're the one shaping them. A coach helps and a youth pastor helps, but the voice your son grows into will sound like whoever was actually around.

Then today's work. Do it like the owner is standing behind you. One is. Then the people tied to you. The buddy in trouble. The man at church whose wife moved out this month. The boy two doors down whose dad is mostly a rumor. Keeper is your job. Savior is already taken, by One Man.

Last comes the weight men drop first, and it's the heaviest, your own wrong. Not the wrong done to you. We'll get to that later. I mean the thing you said at supper you can't take back, the years you were home in body only, the thing she still hasn't been told. Put that down and it doesn't shrink. It slides onto everybody else in the house and grows on the way. With grown kids, it's mostly the oldest weight, the mistakes from their childhood. It's not too late to lift it. Usually that starts with a phone call.

A few sentences before Paul tells us to carry our own load, he says what sounds like the opposite. Carry each other's burdens, he writes, and that's how you keep the law of Christ. Both are true because he uses two different words. The burden he means first is a crushing weight, more than one man can lift. The load he means second is a soldier's pack, gear issued with your name on it and sized for your back. When Jesus called His burden light, that second word is the one He chose. Something another man dropped joins your pack once it lands in your house or your lane. Until then it's his, and if it's crushing him, that's the first kind. Your pack goes on without a speech. When a brother is pinned under the crushing kind, you get under it with him, invited or not. And the order matters. A man who helps everyone in town while his own pack sits in the driveway is hiding, and his family can tell.

Pride gets this backward. It loads one arm until a man staggers, because an empty hand feels like failure. His wife's entire happiness. Every choice his kids make. The spiritual condition of the whole church. He calls that devotion. It's overload. Moses hit that point in the desert, a whole nation griping about the menu, and he said what pride usually swallows. He told God he couldn't carry all these people by himself, and the weight was too heavy for him. God's answer was seventy elders. Some weights He left off your list on purpose, and grabbing them anyway is pride in a work shirt. Your wife's joy belongs to her and to God. Your grown son's choices belong to your grown son. So name the weight God never handed you and give it back. Let two or three men take the piece that's flattening you. That isn't quitting. It may be the most faithful hour of your year.

:::break
:::

Long before any of this was said about us, it was said about Him. Isaiah wrote that He took up our pain and carried our suffering, while the people watching figured God was punishing Him. He didn't cause the wreck and didn't owe a cent of it. He never once said, the woman You gave me. The cross is "That's mine" spoken by the only man who could have refused and been telling the truth. There was joy ahead of Him while He carried it. Your Father sees the beam nobody upstairs mentions, and what He has for you is a son's inheritance, not a wage. That's why Pick Up the Weight is a mark and not a chore.

If something settled on your shoulders while you read, that's what being the wall feels like. It's meant to rest on a frame built for it, and God sizes a man for the load He issues, with seventy more stationed around him for the rest. Hold up your corner, and when it gets heavy, keep your finger from pointing. He'll plant you under what's yours and keep you honest about what He's taken back, and your house will learn that the floor holds. Absence purchased with a bigger paycheck is still absence. Stay in the room.
