# 15. Two Men, Two Ways

:::epigraph
For as in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive.
1 Corinthians 15:22
:::

A seed packet is stuck to the fridge with a magnet, and it knows more than the backyard does. Tomato. Full sun. Plant after the last frost. Without that packet, the bare stretch in the third row is plain dirt. With it, you know a plant belongs there. A row can't fail until you know what it was meant to grow. Get men talking and it comes around to their marriages, their tempers, their sons. Underneath, they're nearly always asking one thing. What was I supposed to be before I got bent? If you've never looked at the original, you can't sort damage from personality. So let's go back to the first week, before anybody hid or blamed, and look at what God made when He made a man, back when He was still calling everything good.

Start with the dirt. God shaped the man from the dust of the ground and breathed the breath of life into his nostrils, and the man came alive. If you've ever knelt by a fire pit the morning after, found one orange coal under the ash, and put your face down near it to blow until it caught, you've seen a picture of this. Clay in His hands. Then His face close enough to breathe. Nobody assembled you at arm's length. God leaned in. And what that dust was shaped to look like is the very first thing the Bible says about you. God made mankind in His own image, male and female. Sinner is coming. It isn't first. Before your first trophy or your first wreck, you were made to resemble God. Nobody earns that, and failing doesn't erase it. Women bear it as fully as men. Manhood doesn't lift you a step above the women in your house. It's one of two ways of carrying one likeness, and God dreamed up both.

God's opening words to the people He'd made weren't instructions. They were a blessing, and only then a charge to be fruitful, fill the earth, and bring it under their care. Identity, then assignment. The Father keeps that order at the Jordan, calling Jesus His beloved Son before Jesus has preached a sermon or healed anyone. And the assignment is no small one. Raw ground was meant to come alive under a man's care. That's why a fence line you set true, a faucet you finally fixed, or a table you built from rough boards makes you feel like you for an evening. That's the original wiring. Subdue was never permission to bully. God governs by making things grow. Ruling only curdled into grinding people down after the fall. In Eden, ruling looked like tomatoes coming in. If there's a new baby in your house, you're living inside be fruitful right now. Bone-tired doesn't mean you're off the design. It means the curse got heaped on top of the blessing.

Look where God set him down. He planted a garden in Eden and put the man there. Adam wasn't turned loose to roam until he found himself. He had an address, and the address was part of how he was made. So do you. Most men treat theirs like an accident, or a waiting room until real life starts. The apartment, the job site, the small town you keep meaning to leave, that's the plot God handed you. A man forever squinting at the next county never tends his own. And that plot was a workplace from day one. God put the man in the garden to work it and take care of it. Nobody has sinned yet. No curse has been spoken. And already there's soil under the man's nails. Work came before the curse. Thorns came later, and work came first, and it was good. So a man with nothing worth doing isn't really resting. He's idling rough. A layoff, a retirement with no purpose in it, a back that can't do the old job anymore can flatten a man, because he was built to tend a garden and someone took it. Abe went hunting in that verse for a retirement clause once the paychecks quit. He stopped introducing himself as let go. "Same job," he said. "Different garden." Grandkids now, and younger men, and a wife who's carried plenty.

Take care of it means watch over it, guard it. The man who turned the soil also walked the fence. And part of what he guarded was a boundary God Himself set. Listen to how God opens. You're free to eat from any tree in the garden. The whole orchard is open except one tree, and eating from that one means death. Adam got that straight from God before Eve was there, and keeping the garden meant keeping that line too, against any voice that might talk them over it. Something is about to slip through the fence.

Men tend to skim the next part. By the seventh day God had finished His work, so He rested, and He blessed that day and made it holy. God wasn't tired. Rest is where good work is headed. He put the stopping day into the calendar before the man ever lifted a tool. Picture a shop on Saturday evening, every tool wiped and hung back on its painted outline, floor swept, lights off. That isn't a lazy shop. It's a shop that knows what it's for. You aren't a machine. Six days on, one day off the clock. Grinding through the seventh isn't a promotion. If the whole outfit calls you on Sunday, hear this. A business that can't spare you one day in seven has started to own you. A man who never stops goes numb, and numb isn't holy.

Then, with all that good stacked up, God says something that stops you cold. It's not good for the man to be alone. Every day so far had ended good. Then God looked at one man in a flawless garden, with meaningful work and God's own voice in his ear, and said not good. He hadn't sinned. He had God. And God still said solitary wasn't good. Needing people isn't a weakness that crept in after things broke. It was drawn into the plans. So God walked the animals past him to see what he'd call them, and whatever the man called each one, that was its name. God let the man's words count. Yours stick too. A son tends to become what his father calls him. In a marriage, a husband's words become the walls she lives inside. Even a quiet man's few sentences land like hammer blows.

None of the animals was the answer. A good dog is fine company, but he needed someone like him and not like him. So God put him under, shaped the woman out of his side, and walked her over. Then, for the first time in Scripture, a human being speaks, and he says she's bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh. The first recorded words of a man were poetry, and the poem was about her. Ask a husband when he last said something to his wife that wasn't logistics, and watch him squint. The design is a man so taken with his bride that words come out of him. He leaves home and clings to her, and the clinging was his to do. But hear not good wider than a wedding. Brothers. An older man out ahead of you. A church that knows your name. If you're divorced, I know that sentence can sting. God hasn't torn up the plan, and He isn't pushing you toward the altar either. This is what He's handing you. If you're single, you're inside that sentence, not outside it. Almost everything in that first week was given to Adam while he was still on his own. A wedding doesn't make you a man. Tend your plot and walk your fence now.

Then, right before it all breaks, one more line. The man and his wife were both naked, and they felt no shame. Fully seen, and not afraid. That's a man before the fall. Think about closing day on a house. You walk through before the movers come. Empty rooms, late light across bare floors, not a scuff anywhere. Then the years happen. Crayon down the hallway, a dent where the dresser hit, a water ring under the window. One day you catch yourself talking as if the stains were the house. They aren't. It was good before the marks, and its Builder remembers exactly how it looked. Every locked room in a man since Eden is a long, sideways try at walking around unashamed again.

:::break
:::

Most nights, once the kids are finally down, he walks out to the strip of dirt beside the garage. Boots, no socks. He pulls the hose over, straightens the tomato cage that leans after every rain, and stands there awhile. Drive by and you'd never guess anything that matters happens there. Same for Eden from the road, and for an olive grove outside Jerusalem the night a Man lay flat on that ground and prayed. The Bible hangs the whole human story on two gardens and two men, and each had a night when everything rode on him. The first man was given everything and lost it in one afternoon, mostly by keeping his mouth shut. The second Man arrived at His garden with almost nothing left except a choice. He made it lying on the ground, and that choice rescued the world. Paul says that one man's disobedience made the many sinners, and one Man's obedience will make the many righteous. Which of those two are you standing in?

The serpent slides in and skips Adam. He goes to Eve, and he goes after what God said. Did He really tell you that? A small bend in the words, and a suggestion that God is tight-fisted. And where's the man? She took the fruit and ate it, and gave some to her husband, who was with her. He was right there. Men have lived under that phrase ever since. Adam heard the command first, before Eve had been made, so guarding that ground was already his job. Then the thing he was put there to keep out came right in, and he listened to God's word get twisted and said nothing. Then he ate. The Bible's first sin has a silent man standing in the middle of it. Most of us know that silence from the inside. Something false got said at the table, answering it would have cost the evening, and we let it ride.

When God comes looking that evening, the two of them duck out of sight. Adam says he heard God in the garden and was afraid because he was naked, so he hid. Fear, then cover. The man who should have stepped out to meet that voice crouched behind leaves. And when God asks him plainly what he's done, Adam's answer starts with her. The woman. The one You gave me. Count the names in that sentence. One is missing, and it's his own. Under all of it sits the grab. You'll be like God, the snake had promised. Adam wanted the gift and was willing to lose the Giver to get it. Nobody had to teach you that groove. You found it the way rainwater finds the low spot in a yard.

Thorns follow, and death, and a shut gate. But inside the curse sit two mercies. God makes them clothes from an animal's skin, so the first covering for shame cost a life. Then He turns to the snake and speaks a curse with a promise folded inside it. A son of the woman would crush the serpent's head, and the serpent would strike his heel. A son is on the way. When the enemy comes to His garden, He won't stand there mute. It will cost Him a wound, and He will crush the thing that got in. Before the first garden stopped smoking, God was announcing the second Man.

:::break
:::

Night, and a hillside He knows well. His friends come along, and He brings three of them in closer. Then He goes on a little farther by Himself, falls with His face to the ground, and asks His Father to take the cup from Him if there's any way, and then says, not My will, but Yours. In Eden the offer was fruit that promised to make a man a god. Here it's a cup that will make God's Son look like a cursed criminal, and everything in Him pulls back. Luke says the sweat came off Him like drops of blood. And still He prays what Adam never prayed. That cup held sin He never committed, and He drank it with our guilt laid on Him. Three times He prays it. Then torches come up the path, and He stands. He tells His friends to get up, because it's time to go and the betrayer is here. He walks straight at them.

Knowing all that was about to happen to Him, He went out and asked who they wanted. No search party needed. An hour after sweating blood, He met the clubs and lanterns in the open, His mind on His men. He told the soldiers He was the one they were after, so they should let these men go. Seconds from being bound, He points to His own chest. Take Me. Leave them. He plants Himself between the soldiers and the friends His Father put in His care. If you're divorced, sit a long time with that line. Adam's instinct is to aim a finger, at her, at the lawyers, at the God who brought her into your life. Jesus said, Me, not them. Your kids shouldn't have to haul your war around. Shield them. Name your own part out loud. Hand hers to God.

Then the cross. And at the end He doesn't cry for rescue. He reports in. He says it is finished, bows His head, and gives up His spirit. Every piece done, and He says so before He dies. The crowd filed Him away as one more failed teacher on a cross. Three days later the Father answered by raising Him.

The Bible doesn't stop at telling you to go copy the second Man. It says you already live inside one of the two. In Adam all die, and in Christ all will be made alive. Birth put you in Adam. That's why going quiet comes easy, why blame stays loaded, why ducking feels like plain sense. You were in him the way a whole family carries the name of the man at the top of the tree. So you aren't told to try harder. Willpower never pulled one man out of Adam. God offers a transfer. By faith He lifts a man out of the first and sets him inside the second, and the second garden is counted as his. Christ's obedience goes on that man's record, and "It is finished" gets spoken over him for good. Jesus called this being born again. It's the gospel, and there isn't another door. Paul put his own new address this way. He'd been crucified with Christ, and the life he lived now he lived by faith in the Son of God, who loved him and gave Himself for him. The Spirit who raised Jesus lives in a man who belongs to Christ, and that's where his strength to stand comes from. Not grit. Life.

If you've never done this, do it now. Tell God that Eden's way is the only one you've ever lived and you're finished with it. Ask Him to put you in His Son, Christ's garden laid over yours, Christ's record where yours used to be. The door's open, and He's the one who opened it. If you've already done that, hear the rest. Everything this book asks of a man, Jesus did first, and He did it for you. The marks in these pages aren't a ladder you climb so He'll take you. You're already His. They're the family face, starting to show on a son who belongs.

:::break
:::

Your garden won't look like an olive grove. One will look like a kitchen. She says something that isn't quite fair, and going cold or firing back would both feel good. That's the garden. The other way might be one calm sentence. That one stung. Could we sit down once the kids are in bed? Another looks like a phone at eleven, with the charger down the hall where you could just walk it. That one comes with or without a ring on your hand. The second Man never married, and nobody has ever been more of a man. With a baby in the house, a lot of them happen at 2 a.m. He asked the Father to take the cup away, and then He stood up. You can stand up too, even if it's only to get to the end of the hall. Or a project gone sideways at work with your prints on part of it, and a room that would happily let the vendor take the blame. The other way is five words. That piece is on me. Eden came down to one piece of fruit. Most of your days will come down to things about that size.

Paul calls Jesus the last Adam. Last, not next. Nobody comes after Him. He did the job the first one dropped, all the way through. Now He's drawing men into Himself and leading them through ordinary gardens, His Spirit inside them, His record covering them. Adam's last words in Eden were an excuse. Christ's were a finished report. Cal started praying something new in his later years. Lord, whatever's still mine to do, let me finish it. The road gets shorter, and there are still gardens on it.

Sit here a minute while the first garden is still whole in your mind. Shaped by hand. Breathed into. Blessed before you'd done a thing. Given ground to tend and a day to stop. Then look at the second garden, where the Man who was owed everything stood up and walked toward the torches for you. Tonight, stand where you are as someone who's in Christ. And if you've never asked Him to bring you in, don't go to bed without asking. You were born in the first man's garden. In Christ, the second Man's "It is finished" is the last word on you.
