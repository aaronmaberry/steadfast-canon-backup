# 23. You Were Made for a Work

:::epigraph
In his defense Jesus said to them, "My Father is always at his work to this very day, and I too am working."
John 5:17
:::

After a hard rain, Gil's out on the county motor grader before sunup. Same machine he's run since his youngest was born. Forty miles of gravel, and after every storm he scrapes the ruts back out. School buses use those roads. The ambulance does too. One Sunday he cornered me after church. Every sermon he'd heard on calling had cut his week in half. The real stuff happened in the building. His hours behind the blade just paid for the privilege of showing up. "I'm just running a blade," he said. Then he asked me straight out whether any of it counted with God. Think about who rides on that road. God saw it before Gil did. Underneath his question is one most of us carry. Is my job part of my life, or the toll I pay until life starts? If it's only a toll, most of your waking hours don't matter. The Bible won't let me say that.

Yes, it counts. Paul answered that question for people whose jobs were far worse than Gil's. He was writing to slaves, men with no say at all over their work, and he told them to work at whatever they did with all their heart, as working for the Lord and not for human masters, since they knew they'd receive an inheritance from the Lord as their reward. It's the Lord Christ they were serving. That's Work for God's Pay, at five in the morning in a county truck. Nobody in that county knows Gil's name. The Lord does, and He's the one Gil is really working for. A time card turns into an offering.

Hear the word pay correctly, though. It isn't a wage. The pay is the inheritance the Lord gives His children, handed over by grace to sons already in the family, never a paycheck that buys your way into heaven. Nobody earns an inheritance. You get one because you belong, and in Christ you do. Gil doesn't grade roads to get saved. He grades them because he's already the Father's son, and sons like doing their father's work well. It also means the job isn't who you are. You're a son first, maybe a husband and dad, a man God trusted with some ground. Your job is one plot of it. Make it your name and you'll die a little when it ends. And watch the quieter trap. Work applauds in a way home rarely does. One more hour at the office sounds awfully reasonable when the new baby won't stop crying. Stay late because it's easier than walking in the door, and a good gift has turned into a hiding place. Providing is holy. Ducking out isn't.

You've already seen where work started, in a garden, before anything broke. And God Himself works. When the religious leaders complained that Jesus healed on the Sabbath, He told them His Father was still at work, and so was He. You bear the image of a God who stays on the job. That's why losing the work cuts so deep. And for most of His years, Jesus worked with His hands for paying customers. We talk about three years of ministry. The rest was sawdust.

Work feeds people. Paul said whoever refuses to work shouldn't eat, and he meant refuses. The man who can't is under grace. A divorced dad may be feeding two households and a lawyer. Take the hours if you must, and stay reachable. A two-minute call from the truck costs nothing, and a dad who goes to work and comes back gives shaken kids ground to stand on. Work serves people, too. Forty honest hours are forty hours of loving your neighbor, and careless work is careless love. Paul even told thieves what to do instead of stealing. Quit, he said, and work with your own hands at something useful, so you'll have something to share with people in need. The taker becomes a giver. And leave a thing better than you found it and you get a small taste of what God felt on the sixth day. That good tired feeling is the image showing through.

:::break
:::

You met Alton at the creek, and heard about the morning after the retirement party. The Bible doesn't hand you a recliner. It hands you a new shift. Under the law the Levites quit the heavy work at fifty, but they didn't quit being Levites. They stayed on at the tent of meeting, standing beside the younger men still hauling the poles. Alton never turned off his alarm. Sleep in, he says, and drift owns the morning. He reads a chapter aloud on the porch. Every day he finds one thing somebody is counting on, a footing, a widow's gutters, the church van. He told me once, "A man with no job by noon is a man in a chair by two." He spends time with a couple of younger men who need somebody out in front. His grandson gets hills. Sometimes the body quits before the calendar does. I know a man who can't leave his wife for more than an hour, so he uses that hour to call widowers and ask how they're holding up. Drift likes older men, and anger rides in with it through the TV. Carry less. Help more. Keep passing along what you know.

They call you in on a Thursday. By Friday there's nothing on your calendar. The money usually isn't the first thing that hurts. For years you've introduced yourself with a sentence, and now it ends at "I'm." Tell your wife that same day, before you have a plan. Men sit on a layoff for weeks, and she ends up hurt more by the hiding. Tell a brother that week, and when he asks what's heavy, answer him. Then make the search your job. Same alarm, shave, at the kitchen table by eight. Take temporary work if it keeps the lights on. Nothing honest that feeds your family is beneath you. And refuse the shame. Guilt points at something you did. Shame points at you. Losing a job is neither. It's a thorn, and it didn't surprise your Father.

Some of you can't stand your job, and hearing that it's worship sounds like a slap. Fair. Bosses who don't know what they're doing, people who lie to your face, systems built to grind you down. Solomon had every comfort there is, tried every sort of labor, and still landed on this. A man can't do much better than to eat and drink and find some satisfaction in his work, and even that comes from God's hand. You can still find something to hold. Get good at the craft, because skill belongs to you. Notice who's beside you. A younger guy is learning how to handle a rotten Tuesday by watching you. Somebody on your crew is losing his marriage and needs one person to talk to. And settle who you're working for. Work for a manager and his moods own you. Work for the Lord and they don't. If it's time to go, go well. A bad week isn't a resignation letter. Make a plan, talk it through with your wife if you're married, line up the next job or enough savings, and let a brother test your reasons. Some men storm out. Others stay thirty years somewhere that's burying them and call it faithfulness.

Some work doesn't look like work, even to the man doing it. The budget worked out so you're home with the kids. Or you run the house while your wife works the long shifts. Nobody hands you a check for it, and it's as real as any job in this book. That's the keeping half of the garden. Tend it. Guard it. Your Father notices what's done in private, and He doesn't measure you by how many people drive past.

:::break
:::

Hours before His arrest, Jesus told His Father He'd brought Him glory on earth by finishing the work He'd been given to do. The bench, the preaching, and the cross were one assignment, and He saw it through. Every man wants to say something like that when his time's up. Yours might be a grader, a classroom, a crew, or a kitchen full of kids. Gil still runs the blade. Last spring he started praying for each house as he crowns the road in front of it. People drive his gravel every morning with no idea the man who smoothed it asked God to keep them safe.

If you've figured your hours were wasted on God, carry a different picture in tomorrow, Jesus with sawdust on His arms for most of His life. Laid off, worn out, or holding a retirement plaque, you haven't been set aside. You've been moved to a different plot. The Father who never stops working can put one job somebody needs into your hands before lunch tomorrow. Go do your work. When the old plot is gone, God gives you a new one. Finish yours.

## Your Turn

**Name it**

Which purpose has your work mostly served, worship, feeding your family, helping people, or leaving things better? Write it down, then be honest about the drift. Is the job becoming your name, your escape, or your idea of how you get saved? Single? It may be your biggest plot right now. Work it like ground you'd be glad to share someday, but don't marry it.

**Do this week**

Choose one part of your job this week, do it for the Lord, and keep it to yourself. That's worship you can actually finish. Between jobs? Monday is a full workday of looking. Retired? Decide tomorrow's job before bed and have it done before lunch.

**Tell a brother**

The man hiding in extra hours almost never sees it himself. Tell your brother whether work has been your way out, and what it's cost at home. If your paycheck years are over, show him how you're spending your days and ask him to keep you honest.
