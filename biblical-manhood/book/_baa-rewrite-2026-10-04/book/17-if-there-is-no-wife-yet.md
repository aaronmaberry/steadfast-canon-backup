# 17. If There Is No Wife Yet

:::epigraph
I am saying this for your own good, not to restrict you, but that you may live in a right way in undivided devotion to the Lord.
1 Corinthians 7:35
:::

A lamp sat on a moving box, long enough to press a ring into the cardboard. KITCHEN, in black marker, still faced the bed. In the box were two plates he'd bought for a table he didn't own, a skillet with the price sticker on the bottom, and a dish towel he was saving for later. Micah said he was waiting for his life to begin. I asked him what he'd call the room he wakes up in every morning. Press him and he'll tell you real life kicks off when some woman agrees to marry him. A good stretch of what's ahead is about marriage. If you're not married, this chapter is yours before it's anybody else's. Then stay. Everything that follows is something you can build on now.

"Single" covers at least three men, and their years look nothing alike. One has never been married. Maybe he aches for a wife. Maybe he's halfway in, or he's quit looking. With nobody real in the picture, it's easy to dream up a woman no actual woman could match. If you're young, hear this. These years count. Rooms you can live in, money you can account for, a brother, a clean conscience. A husband needs every one of those. Start now. The second man was married, and it ended. You're mourning what was taken, or what you wrecked, or both, with your life split across two addresses while the church tries to figure out where to seat you. All I say to the first man, I'm saying to you. The third man stood at his wife's grave. You lived in that house together, and then her side of the bed went still. People don't know what to say, so a lot of them say nothing. I'll say something. Keep working, and keep showing up. We want you here.

If you're in the divorced seat, single again is single. Everything this chapter says about building a home, feeding people, finding brothers, and keeping clean is yours, plus fathering in two houses. Grief comes with the seat, whether you filed or fought the filing. Something died, so bury it. Men who skip the funeral build the next house on soft dirt. Cry. Tell a brother what's gone. Then own your share. Healing starts when "I did this" comes out of your mouth with no "but she" behind it. Your kids come before any new woman. Dating hard while they're still finding their feet puts things upside down. Stay at church even if people are clumsy with you. Don't race toward another wedding. Sit down with your pastor. A man who remarries in the first raw year to make the pain stop usually just passes the pain along.

If she died first, you know it's not good for a man to be alone in a way young men can't, because you had the answer and now she's gone. Grieve, out loud, as long as it takes. Keep hold of what remains. The garden. Grandkids. One close friend. Scripture, and your seat on Sunday. Drift loves a widower, and it nearly always shows up calling itself rest. Mr. Pruitt didn't let it in. Married fifty-one years, he planted forty pecan trees the spring after he buried his wife, and Ed still comes for coffee on Wednesdays. Marrying again is between you, God, your grown children, and her, if there's a her. Some men remarry. Some never do, and those years are full anyway. Your life didn't end when hers did.

:::break
:::

Churches can love single men and still preach over their heads. Every illustration starts with "your wife," and nobody looks at the guy alone in the third row. Give it a few years and he starts to think manhood gets handed out at the altar. The Bible won't back that. Jesus never married, and no one has ever lived a fuller life. Paul was single, and so was John the Baptist. Plenty of the Bible's most fruitful men never had a wife or a child. Look at the order in Genesis. Adam got nearly his whole assignment while he was still alone. Made in God's image, blessed, set on the ground, given work and a command, naming each animal as it came by. He was a man with a full job before there was any bride. God brought the woman because He'd named the one thing that wasn't good. She didn't come to flip Adam's switch. If you have no wife yet, you're already living your life. The ground already has your name on it. If God brings a wife, she'll walk into work you've already started.

Most of you want to marry, and that's good. Proverbs says the man who finds a wife finds a good thing and receives favor from the Lord. Finding means moving. Charm fools people and beauty fades, and Proverbs praises the woman who fears the Lord. Attraction counts. Just weigh it last. First find out how she talks to God, whether she's honest, and whether she's kind to people who can't do anything for her. Pursue her out loud. Name the evening, and give her a reason she can understand. Circling her for twelve months is a stall. So is an endless text thread that never becomes a plan. She can tell pursuit from hanging around.

Then decide. Seven days is too quick for a lifetime. Seven years without an answer burns through hers. If you know her character and you're still standing in the doorway, the true name for that is passive. That's what Stop Waiting is for, and it applies to courting too. Move toward a covenant, or let her go honorably. Let an older man get to know her. Watch how she treats her father and her friends. You're getting a preview. Keep your hands off. Sex outside the covenant clouds your judgment and trains both of you to treat vows as optional. I've never met a man who regretted waiting. Marry when you can carry it. Feeling ready won't steer you. Steady work, an honest budget, a place to live, brothers, a body you've guarded, and a prayer life. When those are there, and she fears God, and older men say go, go. Paul said it's better to marry than to burn. If you can lift it, lift it.

:::break
:::

Tend the place and guard it. That pattern still holds when the lease has one name on it. Live in your home instead of camping in it. Clean enough that a brother could crash on the couch. Meals you made. Bills paid on time. If a woman comes along, your apartment will tell her more than any speech on a date. Feed people. You don't need a marriage license to put chili on the stove. Have the younger guys over. Invite the old man from church who eats alone. Get brothers now. You need them more than the married guy does, because an empty apartment won't notice when you disappear for a week. Find one man who's further down the road, and don't give drift a whole year.

Give the Lord the hours this season hands you. Paul told the unmarried he wasn't trying to fence them in, only to help them live well and give the Lord their undivided devotion. A dad with three kids under six can't spend a Thursday evening like you can. Burn those hours waiting and you've wasted the one real edge this stretch gives you. Be a father to somebody. Boys at your church are missing a man, and you've got Saturday afternoons. Toss a ball. Fix his bike. Pray with him in the truck before you drop him home. Paul called Timothy his true son in the faith. The floor plan's different. The work is the same. Stay clean. The marriage bed is a covenant, and keeping it starts years before the wedding. What you do with your eyes and your hands now is either saving something for her or spending it early. Paul told Timothy to treat older women as mothers and younger women as sisters, with absolute purity. That covers every woman you aren't married to, including the one you're hoping you will be.

Micah opened that box on a Saturday. No woman had called. An older guy from church had told him to quit asking permission and stop living like a guest in his own place. So he hung the skillet. He set out the plates and cooked for a few men who actually came. He wrote a budget and stuck to it. He started picking up a fatherless boy from church every week. He still wanted a wife. He just stopped treating her like the switch that would make him a man. None of it took hold in a week, and some Saturdays the skillet never left the hook. But he'll tell you that's where it started, years before any woman said yes. Not yet is an honest answer. If it's yours, say it. Then open the box. Hope and work can share an apartment.

Read what's coming like a man framing a house before the family moves in. A lot of married men are patching walls. You can set yours plumb the first time. And there's a fatherless kid somewhere in your church who's watching how you live. Most of you will marry. Until you do, the ground is yours, and the Father's well done doesn't wait for a second name on the mailbox. So stand up. Somebody has to build this house. Shut this book without the old whisper that you're only half of something. The most complete Man in history never stood at an altar with a bride. Today your ground is asking for you. And if papers or a casket are what sat you in this chair, your sorrow is real and you still belong here. The Father who saw that a man shouldn't be alone will put brothers on your porch and settle the matter of a wife on His own timetable, while you open the box and go to work. No wife yet still leaves you a man, with ground to work and a Father who already calls you son.
