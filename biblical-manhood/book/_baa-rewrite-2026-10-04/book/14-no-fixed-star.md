# 14. No Fixed Star

:::epigraph
This is what he showed me: The Lord was standing by a wall that had been built true to plumb, with a plumb line in his hand.
Amos 7:7
:::

He keeps the two-foot level on top of the dryer. Today he's putting up a laundry-room shelf, and he's hung enough of them to know a wall can look square and lie to you. He sets the level down on the plank. That little bubble wanders, settles, and stops just outside the lines. The shelf he'd have bet money on is crooked. He doesn't argue. The bubble has no opinions. It only knows which way is down. Masons use the old version. Tie a weight to the end of a cord, let it hang beside the brick, and wait for it to quit swinging. Every time, the cord points dead at the center of the earth. Gravity doesn't poll the crew. If the wall has wandered, the plumb line says so. Most of us measure our lives against things that wander.

Judges closes with a line I'd put up against any sentence in the Bible for sheer sadness. Israel had no king in those days, and everyone did whatever looked right to him. What makes it sad isn't that every man was a villain. It's the yardstick. Each man's own judgment was his north, so whenever he turned, north turned with him. Drive a farm-to-market road in heavy fog, steering by whatever fence post is in your headlights, and you can end up miles off without feeling a single curve. That's a lot of the men I know. Capable. Booked solid. No heading. So we grab for something to steer by, and most of what we grab moves.

Dad usually comes first. Whatever he was, that's what a man looked like. Then you got older and realized he'd been drifting too. And if he walked out, or never really showed up, you didn't even get a post to sight on. Learn from your father. Don't steer by him. If you're single, the same goes for the wife you're hoping for. Marriage is a good gift. It isn't the heading. Next come the guys around you. I earn more than my brother-in-law. I'm home for supper more than anybody on my crew. At least I never pulled what that deacon pulled. That's one leaning wall grading itself against another. You can own the straightest wall in the neighborhood and still be off plumb. At nineteen, guys your age usually set the measure, and how you treat a woman becomes whatever got the biggest laugh at the job site. A chapter of Mark read in your truck before the shift won't change its mind when the laughter does. If you're divorced, the wall you'll grade yourself against is hers. Quit sighting off it and hang the line against your own.

Then the culture. What made a man in your granddad's eyes and what your boy's phone is selling him are two different norths. An older man may have steered by the world that raised him, and now he hears it mocked on every channel. Scripture will fix what his generation got wrong and keep what it got right. The cord doesn't care what year the wall went up. Then feelings. It just doesn't feel right anymore. I sense God moving me somewhere else. Feelings are real, but they rise and fall with your sleep, your lunch, and your last argument, and steering by them is navigating by clouds. A new dad on three hours of sleep concludes the marriage is failing when it's only worn out. Ten minutes in Scripture before anyone picks up a phone does more than an hour of building a case against your marriage after the kids go down.

And then success, the most convincing of all. The company's growing, so I must be okay. We're still married, so we must be fine. I know a man whose work kept telling him he was doing great, year after year, while at home his wife was quietly building a life that didn't include him. A profit-and-loss sheet can't do a plumb line's work. Remember David, running a kingdom smoothly for a year with a murdered soldier hidden away. Success just means the wall hasn't come down yet. It can't tell you whether it's true. Proverbs warns about a way that looks right to a man and ends in death. Looks right. That's drift, seen from the driver's seat. The road only tells you its real name at the end.

:::break
:::

There is a fixed point, and Amos got a look at the One who holds it. The Lord stood beside a wall built true, a plumb line in His hand, and asked the prophet what he saw. A plumb line, Amos said. Then God told him He was hanging that line in the middle of His people. He hung His cord right against their actual lives, and no wall got a vote. That's Scripture. It has commands in it, but more than that, it holds still, so a drifting man can finally see how far he's gone. The psalmist called God's word a lamp for his feet. A lamp at your feet doesn't light the whole county. It shows you the next step, and that's all a Tuesday morning asks for. Paul put the same thing in working terms when he told Timothy that all Scripture is breathed out by God and good for teaching, for rebuke, for correction, and for training a man in what's right, so he's ready for every good work. Teaching shows you what's true. Rebuke is the cord hanging beside a wall that's started to lean. Correction walks the wall back. Training keeps coming around until your own hands lay brick straight. Equipped like that, you quit borrowing other men's wandering norths. Take everything this book has asked of you so far and hang it against that cord, and you'll find out fast which part of your life has started to lean.

You met the wise builder back in the first chapter, the man who heard and then did. Here's the part that matters now. He didn't check his walls against the neighbors on the sand. He checked them against the rock.

Most men were never shown how. They open Genesis on New Year's Day, bog down in Leviticus by February, and quit. That's a gap in training, not character, and four weeks of a different habit will close it. Some say it feels like homework. Then stop reading it like an assignment and start reading it like a son listening to his Father. Read it aloud in the cab. Walk in with one question, what's this asking a man to do? Some say there's no time. There's ten minutes. Most of us already spend them on the weather and the box scores, so give the same ten to the one thing in your life that doesn't move. Some say they don't feel anything. Feeling wasn't the point. A level never gave anybody goosebumps either. It just tells you whether the plank is true.

:::break
:::

For the short Gospel, start with Mark. For the deep one, John. A section a day, and within a month you'll have spent real hours watching the Man you say you follow. After that, try Proverbs, a chapter each morning for a month. Hugh started there somewhere in the middle of life, on the morning it dawned on him that his dad's opinions had been steering him for decades, and his dad had been in the ground for years. He read one chapter aloud every day on his drive in. His copy is coming apart at the spine. A younger guy once asked how he always knew how to handle money, women, temper, and friends. One chapter a day, Hugh said. Proverbs is a father talking to a son, and nobody outgrows that. When you can't find words to pray, open the Psalms and pray one aloud as if you'd written it. David meant them to be used that way. Whatever you read, carry the same question. What does this ask of me on an ordinary morning with nobody watching, and will I do it before lunch? Read with a brother if you can, each of you bringing the verse that wouldn't let go of you that week, and say it aloud where you'll hear it, in the cab, on the porch, at the supper table.

God knows your story, the shapes we traced and the locked room included. But your chapter isn't the whole book, and if it's the only part you read, you'll read it wrong. The harm done to you isn't the first page. Your sin won't be the last. Using the fixed point as a rulebook and nothing more is using it small. The whole account opens in a garden with a man made for work, and it closes with the Man who finishes what the first one dropped. Lose your fixed point and it's worse than a bad week. You lose your place in the story, and you forget what you were made to do.

You may hit February with a lot of Bible still unread. That's fine. Hang the line against your own wall and begin there. The Father will light the next step under your feet this week, and He's patient while your hands get used to the work. A fixed thing can save you before you understand it. You don't need to know why it holds. Put the string on the wall and trust the weight.

## Your Turn

**Name it**

What did you actually measure your life by this year? Your father, the men around you, the culture, your feelings, or your success. Write one honest sentence. Any north you won't name keeps steering you long after you think you've let it go.
