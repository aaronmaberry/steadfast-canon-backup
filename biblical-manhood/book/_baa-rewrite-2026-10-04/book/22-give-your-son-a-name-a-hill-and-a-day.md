# 22. Give Your Son a Name, a Hill, and a Day

:::epigraph
These commandments that I give you today are to be on your hearts. Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up.
Deuteronomy 6:6-7
:::

The hospital gives you a clipboard and a cheap pen on a chain. You fill in the lines while the baby sleeps through his own paperwork, and it feels like the big moment. It's the smallest one. The real naming starts once you get him home, and it runs for years, mostly in sentences you won't remember saying. A boy grows toward whatever he's called. Call him trouble and he'll find ways to prove you right. Call him nothing and he'll go door to door until somebody fills in the blank for him. Plenty of people are lined up to do it. Most of them don't love him.

Ray didn't think he had a naming problem. Then his wife pulled up a clip from their son's birthday. Candles, a pile of kids, everybody off-key, and under the noise, a little left of the camera, a voice Ray knew was his. "What is wrong with you, bud?" He couldn't place the moment. That scared him more than the line did. A man forgets what he says on autopilot. On the screen his boy flinched, then his face went smooth again, like he'd filed it for later. Ray ran those four seconds back so many times he quit seeing the cake. Most of us have a version of that, and it isn't always words. A long breath out through the nose. The look over the top of the phone. I know men past fifty who can still repeat the exact thing, and their voice goes quiet when they get to it. He's getting named everywhere else too. Kids have handed out labels since recess was invented, slow, weird, scrawny, too much. And failure talks to him all day, because boys miss far more than they make. If no man interrupts that voice, he'll take its word for it. I'm just bad at stuff. I'm not above any of this. I've got a sigh that comes out before I've decided anything, and I've watched it land on people.

Under everything you call him, there's one name, and it sounds different on different days. Most days it sounds like belonging. You're mine. He hasn't earned it and he can't lose it, and you aren't leaving. Before Jesus had done one public thing, before a miracle or a sermon, a voice came down over the river and called Him the Father's beloved Son, the one He was pleased with. The Father said whose He was before anybody had seen what He could do. A boy who's sure he's wanted can strike out, flunk, back the car into the mailbox, and still come home. A boy who isn't sure turns his whole life into a tryout. Some days the name sounds like I see you. He sends a ball through the neighbor's window, then walks over and knocks on the neighbor's door. "That was honest. You're an honest guy." Now he's got a word for what he just did and something to grow into. Hear it often enough and lying starts to feel like a shoe on the wrong foot. Paul talked to his churches the way a good dad talks, comfort and push in the same hand. Push alone wears a boy out. Comfort alone leaves him soft.

And at some point the name has to say man. Most of us never heard this one from our fathers. Your son needs you to tell him, at an age he'll remember, that he's becoming a man, and what you mean by it. Don't recite anything. Talk like you do on a long drive. Maybe, "A man doesn't sit around waiting to be told. He sees what's his and gets after it. And when he blows it, he owns it and fixes what he can." That's Stop Waiting in a dad's voice. The question is already in him. Am I there yet? A girlfriend, a fistfight, a paycheck, or a lifted truck will answer it if you don't. Get there first. And if your son is grown and never got that word from you, it isn't too late. Tell him you're proud of the man he is.

You can't hand this over in one talk. It gets in by repetition. The small stuff is daily. He carries his grandmother's groceries without being asked, and you tell him, "That's what a man does." Five seconds. Over a few years they pile up into a floor. Start before he can follow the words. A newborn knows your voice, so leave the phone on the counter and say it over him. Dads of infants often feel like spare parts, and this is the job. A grandson can hear "that's what a man does" from Grandpa and believe it in a way he won't from his worn-out dad. Once a week, give him a blessing. Pick a night and protect it. Hand on his head, a few honest words in your own voice. It might come out, "You're my son, and I'm not going anywhere. I watched you own up to that grade on Wednesday, and I was proud of you. God made you for something, and I'm going to help you find it. Now get some sleep." You'll feel silly. Say it anyway. That's the old charge at the top of this chapter, Moses telling Israel's fathers to keep God's words in front of their kids at every hour, sitting and walking and lying down and getting up. Impress is a stamp pushed into leather, again and again, until the shape holds.

If you're divorced, he's hearing names in a house you don't run, maybe a stepdad's, maybe your silence. You can't fix the other address. You can own the Tuesday night call and make it the blessing, closing it the same way every time. You're my son, I love you, I'll call next week. Never use him to carry messages or report back on his mom. Go-between isn't a name he should have to wear. He's yours wherever he sleeps. Once a year, on his birthday, write him a letter, one sheet in your handwriting, saying what he's like right now, what changed in him this year, and where you think he's headed. Put it in a shoebox and keep going, and someday you'll hand him the box. Brag on him in public. "My son helped me hang the gate Saturday. Stuck with it till dark." Keep the correcting behind a closed door, because shame in front of people turns into a label, and so does honor. No son in your house? Some boy at church or on the ball field hears nothing from anybody. Catch him doing one thing right and say it where his friends can hear.

When he fails, pull apart what he did from who he is. Don't shrug off the lie. Call it what it was, then show him the way back. "You're my son, and that was a lie. My son goes and makes it right. Come on, I'll drive." Tell a boy he's a liar and he'll either fight the word for years or become it. Tell him he's honest and just did a dishonest thing, and he can see the road home. Then point past yourself. Your name over him is good shade, but it isn't the tree. You'll let him down, and one day you'll be gone. Tell him God made him on purpose, and that the Father who never leaves claims him too. The psalm calls children a heritage from the Lord, and your son is that gift sitting at your kitchen table, asking who he is all day, mostly without words. Ray started late and awkward, with nobody to copy. Once he went looking for things to name, he found plenty. A few months in, his daughter dropped a lamp and burst into tears, and Ray heard his boy tell her, "You're not bad. You did a bad thing. Go tell Mom." The boy had started naming people himself.

Lyle figures he was eleven or twelve. An October morning, and the faucet over the kitchen sink gave a cough and went dry. That house drank from a spring on the rise behind it. His grandfather had boxed the spring in concrete a generation back and run a line down through the pasture, one branch to the house, one to the cattle. No water meant somebody was walking up there. He'd been up there before, but only as the kid holding the bucket. This time his dad looked out the window and said, "Spring's quit. Go see to it." That was it. No "come on." No "I'll show you."

Lyle told me this at seventy-something, the place long since sold. He got to the gate at the edge of the yard and froze. The lid on that box was heavier than he was. He turned to say he couldn't, and his dad had already carried his coffee outside and settled onto the back step. He wasn't getting up. So the boy climbed. It ate the morning. The lid slipped and pinched his fingers. A possum had drowned in there and lay across the screen, and nobody else was going to move it. His arms went numb from that water while he dug out the mud. He fit the pipe wrong, saw it, did it over. Every so often he looked back toward the house. His dad hadn't moved. Before he reached the yard he could hear water running into the trough. He walked up soaked, filthy, a couple of knuckles split open. His dad studied him a long second. "Well. I figured you could."

It took Lyle years to understand why the man sat. If his dad had walked up with him, Lyle would have come home believing he needed help. Since his dad stayed put, he came home knowing he didn't. He's been sending boys up hills ever since, his own, his grandkids, a Sunday class full of other men's sons, some with nobody ever waiting at the bottom. He says grandsons are easier. He's got time, tools, and a patience he didn't have at thirty. I asked him what he'd learned. "Pick the right hill. Stay on the step. Say the right thing when he comes down." Then he grinned. "And don't tell his mother what was in the box." You named your boy. Now hand him something steep enough to make the name true. Calling a son strong and never giving him anything heavy is just being nice.

Start with your part, because it's the hard one. Hand it over. You'd be faster. That's the point. If you finish it, he doesn't get it. The man who fixes things for a living has it hardest. You could solve it with one call, and he'd learn that Dad is the answer. Let it be hard for him. Let the lid pinch. That starts in diapers, when you let him wrestle the toy a minute before you rescue it. A man who can't bear the baby's crying usually can't bear the teenager's sulking either. His mom may find this harder to watch, and that's love in her, not weakness. Decide it together, gently. Rescue a safe boy and he learns he needs saving. Leave him to work and he finds out he can manage. Here Stop Waiting flips. The thing that's yours to do is give him the job and keep your seat. The passive father is the one who grabs the shovel. Everybody's more comfortable, and nobody grows. Losing a little now is a gift. Forty bucks gone because he didn't plan is cheap tuition. Shield him from every scrape and his first real one shows up alongside a wife, a house payment, and nobody watching from the porch.

When he gets back, ask. How'd it go up there? What did you figure out? What would you change? Let him think. Then name it. I figured you could. You stuck with it. Without that sentence, a hard day is only a hard day. Then make the next one steeper, and the young man who leaves your house will have a man's hands. Hebrews says no discipline feels pleasant at the time, but later it brings a harvest of righteousness and peace for those trained by it. Neither of you should expect pleasant. You're both waiting on later.

Boys turn into men by facing things slightly too big for them while a man they trust watches and refuses to jump in. Protect a boy from all of it and you starve him. Watch Abraham and Isaac going up the mountain. Abraham lays the wood on his son and carries the fire and the knife himself, and the two of them walk on together. The heavy, awkward load is on the boy. The father keeps the dangerous things and stays alongside. Then there's Jesse, who didn't give David a pep talk. He gave him sheep. When David stood in front of Saul, his whole résumé was that when a lion or a bear carried off a lamb, he went after it and took the lamb out of its mouth. The lion wasn't a drill. It came because the job was real. By the time the giant showed up, David had a history of hard things behind him. Even Jesus grew up learning a trade under Joseph's eye, and Luke tells us He grew in wisdom and stature. Growing takes something to push against. A boy who gets affection and nothing difficult goes hungry, and he'll feed himself. What boys pick on their own tends to be dangerous. The fight. The dare. Gravel at night with the headlights off. Or he disappears into his room for years, because nobody showed him how to climb and every slope looks bottomless.

You can tell a real hill. Somebody besides him needs it. The house really has no water until he's done. Boys know when a chore is fake, and a boy made to sweep a spotless garage for his character will sweep it and roll his eyes. It stretches him without breaking him, harder than what he handled last month, nowhere near a grown man's full weight. He can fail at it. A pinched finger is allowed. Coming back down with the job undone is allowed. Lyle's dad knew that box, and he knew the snakes were already holed up for the cold. He picked the hill. Then he picked the step. The finish belongs to him. A boy says, "I tried." A man says, "It's done," or, "It isn't yet, and here's my plan for tomorrow." And somebody speaks when he comes down. Before the climb, "you can do this" was your hope. After it, he's got proof in his arms. Fit the hill to the actual kid, not the number on his cake. Your daughter needs hills too, and a dad who calls her strong when she comes down. It could be every grocery bag, however many trips. A row of tomatoes, a dog, the trash to the curb on Thursday with nobody reminding him. A summer working for a man who won't grade on a curve. A night in the woods that feels scary and that you've quietly made safe. Someday the hill is his whole life, and your part is answering the phone.

Some things only look like hills. A sport where it's really the dad who needs the win. And screens, which hand him the rush of climbing with none of the weight. He can beat level after level and the sink is still dry. Some dads start with a cliff, and the boy decides climbing means getting crushed. Some swoop in at the last minute, calling the coach or covering the bill, and every swoop tells the boy he couldn't. Some mock the stumble, and from then on he steers around anything hard. A divorced dad has his own version, weekends only, guilt in charge, anything tough edited out so the boy will want to come back. Give him real work at your house instead, and leave something half-built so it's waiting for him. Paul told Timothy, his son in the faith, to take his share of hardship like a good soldier. That's who hills make, a tested man who can walk up alone and fix what's broken. Your boy is standing by that gate right now, looking back to see what you'll do. Sit down. Stay put.

There's a boy I know who keeps a brass plumb bob in his pocket. It's small and surprisingly heavy, with his name and a date cut into the side. His dad says he'll pull it out in a grocery store parking lot for no reason, just to hold it. A few miles from his house, on a gravel bar under a county bridge, sits a little pile of creek rocks nobody has moved. Some afternoons he rides his bike down there to look at them. His dad told me about the morning both of those things happened. Most of us never got a morning like that. We kept waiting for somebody to tell us we'd arrived, and the waiting quietly turned into a whole life. Your son doesn't have to wait like that.

God has always given His people things to bump into. When Israel crossed the Jordan, Joshua had men haul stones out of the riverbed and stack them on the bank, so that when their children someday asked what the stones meant, the fathers could tell them how God stopped the river. A pile of rocks built to start a conversation with a kid who hadn't been born yet. Passover had a child's question built right into the meal. And Jesus heard His Father speak over Him, out loud, at a river, before His work began. Most Christian homes have nothing like this for a son. Birthdays, graduation, sure. But no morning when the men in his life look at him and say plainly, you're a man now. So he grabs a marker from whatever's lying around. A girlfriend. A drunk night. A fight. Hand him the real thing instead.

His voice had started to crack that year, and he'd taken to studying grown men the way younger boys study trucks. His dad told him to be dressed by five, and wouldn't say why. They drove out in the dark and parked by the bridge where he'd been baptized two summers before. Nobody talked on the walk down. Five men stepped into the creek. Alton, his grandfather, went in ahead. His dad. His dad's brother. The friend his dad meets with every week. A neighbor who'd taught the boy to fish, a single man who'd never been asked to anything like it. The water barely covered their ankles and it was cold enough to knock the air out of you. Each man bent, came up with a stone, waded back, and set it at the boy's feet.

Alton read the Joshua passage. Then the men talked, one at a time. His uncle told him the worst thing he'd ever done, and what he did the morning after. When it came around to his dad, he laid his hand on the boy's shoulder and said the name he'd been saying over him since the hospital, my son. Then something new. "You're becoming a man. That's what this morning is about." Then what he meant by it, in his own plain words. "A man does what's his to do, even on the days nobody's looking. That's most of it." He'd written it all out by hand on a card so the boy could keep it. Alton reached into his coat, came out with the plumb bob, put it in the boy's palm, and told him what it was for. Alton had hung a plumb bob against every wall of the house the boy's father grew up in, and he didn't give a lesson on it. He kept it to a few words. "The weight's on purpose. A light one drifts in any breeze. Be the heavy kind, hard to push off true." The name on the side, he said, is for the day the boy forgets who he is. After that, every man laid a hand on him and prayed, and they all went to breakfast, where the men talked to him like a man. His dad says the boy heard the difference, and the bob has already come out of that pocket a few times, once over going along with the crowd at school, once over the truth about a grade.

Your boy's keepsake doesn't have to be brass. A Bible with the date inside, a great-grandfather's watch, a good tool. Pick weight over price, and say what it means when you hand it over. The turn in him tells you when, the voice, the shoulders, the way he's started watching men. If you've missed it, give him the morning anyway. Keep it small, a few men he already looks up to who'll still be around long after breakfast, because a crowd turns everybody into a performer. Don't have those men yet? Start looking now. Go outside, early, somewhere that means something. Let his father speak last, in words that sound like him and nobody else, and if he chokes up, let it show. A boy who watches his dad cry while blessing him learns what no program could teach him. The leader guide at walksteadfast.com has the planning details, and if you want one verse read aloud, Paul's charge to stand firm in the faith and be strong is hard to beat.

The morning doesn't finish anything. I know a family that poured everything into one beautiful day and went right back to treating him like a little kid by Monday. What makes it true is how he's treated afterward. Bigger hills. His opinion asked for and then actually used. Grown-up work, grown-up correction, a grown man's standard, and mercy when he blows it. The day marks the spot. The weeks after it do the handing over. Alton worked for the county most of his life. He says his retirement dinner was the best night of his working life, and the morning after was the worst. Applause, then nobody needing him. So the very next week he called his grandson to come help pour a footing. The creek told the boy he was a man. The footing started making him one. Granddads, that's your opening. You've got standing, maybe a keepsake in a drawer, and the years to make the weeks afterward real.

Maybe nobody ever did this for you, and you can feel it in your throat. You may think you can't give what you never got. That's fair to feel, and it's wrong. Giving it heals the same place receiving it would have. I know fathers who never heard a word from their own dads, who stood by the water, came apart halfway through blessing their boys, finished anyway, and drove home changed. Want it closed out? Afterward, ask a man further down the road to lay a hand on you and say it. You're a man. You did right by that boy. It isn't too late for you. Nobody named you? You can still name him. Ray didn't feel qualified the night he first put his hand on that boy's head. Putting it there is part of how Ray grew up.

Someday a grandson will pick up that brass bob, or ask about the rocks under the bridge. The answer will be a gray morning, a cold creek and the men who waded it, a card in a father's handwriting, and the bob a grandfather hung beside every wall he built. And if we've done our part, whoever's telling it will go set up a morning for his own son. The Father who called Jesus beloved before He'd done anything, and who watched Abraham load wood onto Isaac before He laid a cross on His own Son, knows what it costs to name a boy and then watch him carry weight. He can give you the words, the right hill, the men, and the nerve to say the new word out loud. Your boy will grow toward whatever he's called, so make sure the voice doing the calling is yours. One morning tells him he's a man. A hundred ordinary weeks afterward are what make it so.

## Your Turn

**Name it**

Men rarely hear their own sighs. Write down what your son has been called by you lately, the words, the looks, the good ones and the bad ones. Is he climbing anything real right now, a stretch that's his to finish? And is a day coming for him, or is he left to guess? If nobody ever named you or gave you a day, write that down too. Ask the same for your daughter. Hers may be a supper instead of a sunrise, but marking your son and leaving her guessing is doing half.

**Do this week**

Each day, catch one thing worth naming and say it. One night, put your hand on his head and bless him in words that sound like you. Before Sunday, hand him one hill that fits him and sit on your hands. Then pick the date, the men, the place, and the keepsake for his morning, even if it's months off. Divorced? Talk to his mom first and keep it about the boy. No son? Find a fatherless boy, give him a real job, and offer to stand in the water at another boy's creek.

**Tell a brother**

Over coffee, walk him through what you've been calling your boy and how hard it was to keep still on the step. Ask what his father called him and what hard things his father gave him. Then ask him to wade in with you, with something true to say to your son. Nobody ends up standing in that water without being asked.
