# 19. Lay It Down

:::epigraph
Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her
Ephesians 5:25
:::

She started crying, and for a minute he honestly didn't know why. Back up ten minutes. Two people in a bathroom, water over the tile and soaking into the baseboards. Months ago she went with the cheaper plumber. He said the repair wouldn't hold. It didn't. The other quote, the one he pushed for, sat folded in his back pocket like a winning ticket. He could feel the line climbing his throat, the one that settles it. Five words and he wins. Instead he took out the paper, crumpled it, and tossed it in the wastebasket by the sink. "I'm sorry I got loud," he said. "We'll figure it out." That's when the tears came. Later he worked it out. Through every year of that marriage he had never once walked away from an argument he was winning. She was looking at a husband she'd never met.

Learning her costs you something. This costs more. You lay your life down for her. The wedding, rented tux and flowers, was the easy hour of the vow. A soaked floor on a night when you're the one who's right is where the vow stops being words. Guys know which line in Ephesians they like. It's the one telling wives to submit. It's in the Bible, Paul meant it, and I won't tiptoe around it. But read one more sentence. The part for husbands is longer than the part for wives, it asks for more, and I've never seen a man highlight it with the same enthusiasm. Christ didn't wait for the church to clean up first. He loved people who kept failing Him, and He kept going until it killed Him. Plenty of us would like the headship without the dying. You don't get to split them. Headship is a cross. The one in front absorbs what's coming, and he does it for her sake, not to guard his position. Paul tells us to have the same mind Jesus had, who was God by nature and didn't cling to it, but made Himself nothing and took a servant's form. No one in history stood on higher ground. He stepped down off it, tied a towel around Himself, and washed feet. That mind, in you. Picture it with your socks wet and your proof in your pocket.

First let's clear out the fake. It has wrecked as many homes as the bully has. The fake says, "Whatever you want, babe," and goes back to the couch. He quits making calls, quits stepping out ahead, quits deciding anything, and labels it sacrifice. It's leaving while still sitting in the house. She'll feel the hollow even on nights she gets her way. That's Stop Waiting run backward, a husband who waits forever and gives it a humble name. Jesus gave Himself up and kept leading the whole time. He led on His knees with a basin. He led nailed to a beam. Strength picks her good over its own comfort. Weakness picks a quiet house over doing the job. The doormat is the same fake in a different coat. Jesus told people He loved things they didn't want to hear. If you never tell your wife a hard truth, you haven't laid down your life. You've abandoned your post. Love waits its turn, listens to the end, picks a good hour, keeps its voice soft. Then it says the thing. And some men just disappear and think it's godliness. She said yes to someone with a spine and a heading. Let your rights go, and your comfort, and your pride. Don't let you go. She needs a man in there. So here's how to tell, after you've given something up. Look at her. If she's more settled, more like herself, more awake, it was the genuine article. If the house got harder and you called your vanishing humility, it was the counterfeit.

"Lay down your life" sounds like one heroic afternoon. In a marriage it mostly happens in installments, on days only she will remember. The money might be first. If the accounts are your private country and she gets a visitor's pass now and then, that's governing her. Open the books. Give her full sight and a full vote. Then the argument you're winning. You'll win plenty on the facts, and most of those victories charge her more than they pay you. Nobody's asking you to say the facts changed. You just decide the marriage outweighs the point. Notice the first word of "we'll figure it out." That "we" is the thing you're protecting. Then your phone. Flip it over at the door. A buddy of mine once stared at his screen through an entire hard conversation with his wife and then insisted he'd heard every word. She didn't believe him. Neither did he. If the two women in your life land on opposite sides, your wife gets the tie, and Mom hears it from you. When the kids or your parents or the guys from work are watching, you take her side. She can survive a tough conversation in private if you stood with her in public.

When she's sick or the baby's screaming at two, you're the one out of bed. A man who protects his sleep while she runs on nothing is using her up to keep himself fresh. Golf isn't sin, and neither is picking the restaurant, but when home needs you, those lose, without a sulk or a scoreboard. Don't unload the heat in front of her. Take it to God, to the cab of your truck, to a brother, and come back with whatever was hiding under it. For the high performer it's often the title, being the one who decides. And for nearly all of us it's the next ten years we mapped out in our heads with her nowhere in the room. Tear that map up. Draw a new one at the kitchen table with both your hands on it. Jesus said whoever tries to save his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life for Him will find it. Read it as a husband. Give up winning, and you find a wife who brings you the truth because she's learned you won't use it against her. Give up the Saturday, and two decades on your marriage is still alive. Give up the sleep, and she never forgets who got up.

Russ had one boot off and the other in his hand when he heard Renee through the mudroom door, talking to her sister. "Oh, he's a good provider." She said it kindly, the way you'd talk about a furnace that has never quit on you in January. Nothing in it was false. He'd worked the sixty-hour stretches to make it true. But standing there with the boot, he heard it two ways at once. It was the nicest honest thing she had. And if nothing ever got added to it, it was his eulogy. "You know where people say that?" he asked me later. "About a man at his funeral, when nobody can come up with a second thing." I've sat through enough funerals to know that silence. The room waits for the next sentence, and it never comes.

"Good provider" is a furnace sentence. It tells you the house stayed warm and not much else. Good men get turned around right here, because a paycheck can be counted and being there can't. Scripture honors the work, and so do I. Pour all of yourself into what's measurable, though, and you'll end up with a lovely house and a wife who's lonely inside it. The job wants more, so you hand it more, and it feels like love. Then the signs show up. She stops asking when you'll be home. You learn what's going on in your own house from the kids. By eight at night you're too spent to be anybody. And you've decided she wants this life without ever once checking with her. She needs you, and a bigger paycheck can't fill the spot where you're supposed to be standing.

Russ checked. He sat down across the kitchen table and asked one question. "What do you need from me that you're not getting?" Renee didn't have to hunt for it. "Look up when I walk in." The man had paid for every room in that house, and somewhere along the way he'd stopped raising his eyes when she came into one. He kept the job. What he changed was smaller and harder. He laid down a floor, a few things that stay put whatever the job does, supper at the table four nights a week, Sunday mornings, bedtime prayers with the kids. He gave her an hour and kept it. Six forty. Then six forty again the next day. When it was going to slip, he called before he was late, and he called her, not the family thread. He quit handing her the leftovers of himself. For the first hour the phone lives on top of the fridge, and she gets the opening fifteen minutes while there's still something in the tank. And he stopped playing to the crowd. Work claps for you. Home mostly doesn't. Russ quit chasing the clapping and started doing the job for a Father who notices a supper table. This spring Russ came in through the same mudroom and caught Renee on with the same sister. She didn't say provider. She said, "He's here," and went right on to something about their mom, like that was the most ordinary news in the world.

Being home and being present aren't the same thing. A man can sleep under that roof every night and never really be with her, and she won't be able to explain why she's lonely. Your attention has to walk in the door with your body. In the newborn years, that means being awake beside her at three even when there's nothing for you to do. She also needs your words, out loud and specific, sometimes with the kids in earshot. Proverbs has a husband who praises his wife in front of everybody and tells her that plenty of women do noble things and she's better than all of them. That's not a greeting card. It's a man telling his wife she's the best there is, with witnesses. Find your own version and say it before Sunday. And retire the word "help." The dishes are yours. So are the practice schedule and the dentist appointments. A husband who "helps out" has announced that the house belongs to her and he's a volunteer who drops by. Eve was called a helper, and that's a strong title, not a small one. Christ came to serve. Neither of those gives a man a pass to sit while she runs the place around him.

Your hands matter too, in ways that aren't heading anywhere. A palm on her back at the counter. Her feet in your lap after a long day. Your arms around her while she cries, with every fix you can think of left in your pocket. She already knows the bump in the hallway, and she knows the touch that's angling for the bedroom. Give her the third kind. She needs to not be afraid of you, and I don't mostly mean your fists. I mean the voice that drops, the three-day silence, the sarcasm with an edge, the truck leaving the drive too fast. A wife who checks your weather before she speaks is on guard in her own kitchen. Proverbs says a gentle answer turns away anger. A soft answer takes more strength than a loud one, and once she knows your anger won't land on her, she gets a freedom a lot of wives never taste.

She needs to know you're staying. A wife can sense an "unless" tucked behind the vows even if you've never said it, and nobody rests in a marriage with a door sketched on the wall. Erase it. Tell her, plainly and more than once, that you're here for good. Malachi says you belong to God body and spirit, so guard yourself and don't break faith with the wife of your youth. Staying includes your body, too. Book your physical. One fear she won't say out loud is coming home to find you on the floor. The guy who first asked her out should still be asking. Plan a Thursday and tell her early so she can look forward to it. Get caught watching her across a crowded room. She also wants to know where the house is headed so she can finally fold up the map. Make the hard call yourself and own how it lands. Leave her as the only grown-up and she'll steer because somebody must. Once a week, hold her hand and pray out loud. Hearing her husband bring her name to God settles something in a woman. And let her see you move. A husband who's frozen in place, same guy as ten years ago only grumpier and wider, is one more load on her back. A husband who's changing, slowly, with failures on the record, is a man she can walk next to.

If your marriage ended, you might be staring at everything you wouldn't give up while you had the chance. Let that hurt. Tell God your share of it. Then take the next right step. Stop trying to beat your kids' mom. You'll be right sometimes, and they still need parents who can sit in the same row at the recital without a scene. Somebody has to make that happen, so let it be you. Tearing her down from the other house might win a round, and your kids pay for it. Drop the story where she was the whole problem. Leave the rest with God. If you're single, or nineteen with a ring nowhere close, now is when you practice. A man who's never given up a preference won't suddenly learn between "I do" and the cake. Let the roommate choose dinner. Spend a Saturday helping an older man from church whose gutters are overflowing. Little deaths, on purpose, on regular days. That's how you train, and you're building the man she'll walk beside.

With a newborn at home, take the night shift before she has to ask twice. Stop keeping the silent ledger of who's had a shower. Leave the bedroom alone until she's ready. Put the gym and the guys on a shelf this year. Love the tired woman in front of you. Your Father sees you at three in the morning. Decades into a marriage, give up the retirement you dreamed up alone. Ask what she wants those years to be, then go build it. Hand over the remote, and more of the decisions too. She's able, and she's been waiting. When sickness arrives, the illustrations stop working. Mr. Alvarez married his wife in 1968. For three years now, since illness took most of her away, he has bathed her every morning. Her mind has let go of his name. He has never let go of hers. He promised "in sickness" long before he'd seen that room. The Father sees those mornings.

Look back over all of it and you'll see Christ in every line. He doesn't leave. He calls Himself gentle and humble in heart. He went looking for the lost and still says follow Me. He spoke over bread and said it was His body, given for them, and then He knelt with a towel and a basin. He cried at a friend's grave. What your wife wants is a little version of Him, under one roof, on a plain Tuesday night. Not one of those small losses goes to waste. Your Father notices the fight you threw, the 2 a.m. bottle, the bath she won't remember by lunch. This isn't a pay scale. You're already a son by grace, and what's coming is a Father's well done. The Son who gave up everything will look at a husband who gave up a little and say that's how He did it.

He was right that night on the wet floor. He threw the proof away anyway. Months later his wife told me that's when she started bringing him the things that scared her. A husband who'll let go of a fight he's winning is a husband who won't turn her honesty into a weapon. If your stomach just dropped, take heart. Hardly anything in this book turns around as quickly. Mostly she's asking you to lift your eyes. The One who watched His Son give up everything can make you strong enough to let one thing go this week, and you may come home to a different house because of it. Sometime this week, you'll be right. Being right is not the same as being a husband. Lay it down.
