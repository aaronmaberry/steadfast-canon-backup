# 27. Write the Next Move

:::epigraph
Write down the revelation and make it plain on tablets so that a herald may run with it.
Habakkuk 2:2
:::

I was sitting on Hollis's porch when I asked him which line in his little notebook had been the hardest to cross off. He didn't have to think long. "Probably 'Tell Cole he's a good man.' That sat on the page about three weeks. Finally I thought, if it isn't written down it isn't going to happen, and it was written down, so I'd better." He shrugged. "Took thirty seconds. He sat in his truck and cried for twenty minutes. I'd have put it off another year if I hadn't had to look at it every night." The notebook rides in his shirt pocket, about the size of a deck of cards, and he's carried one since he was nineteen. Every night he writes tomorrow's first three jobs in it, in order. That afternoon's page read fix gate latch, call Cole, read Proverbs 14. He's seventy-four. Fifty-five years of tomorrow, three lines at a time.

His reason fits on one of those pages too. "A man who knows what he's going to do tomorrow gets up and does it. A man who's going to figure it out in the morning figures out he'd rather have another cup of coffee." If you close this book on a good feeling and nothing else, the feeling will be gone by Christmas. So before the cover shuts, put the next move on paper. Not every move. The next one.

Most of us can preach the whole year to ourselves on the drive home, twenty minutes of resolve with the radio off. Then we sit down at the kitchen table with a pen and the page stays blank, because talking to the windshield cost nothing and writing feels like signing something. It is signing something. That's the point. God has a long habit of making His people write things down, and He told Habakkuk why. Written. Plain. Clear enough that somebody else could pick it up and run. That leaves two ways to miss. One man never writes anything. He leaves the retreat with wet eyes, and by the first rough week the fire's out, because a thing that never touched paper never quite became real. The other man writes forty pages of mission statements and core values, and the binder suffocates in a drawer. A herald can't run with a binder.

When God wants something carried, the Bible's shape is small. One surface. Ordinary words. Nehemiah rebuilt Jerusalem's wall in fifty-two days, after months of prayer. He rode the rubble at night until he knew every gap. He asked the king for exactly what the job required. He gave each family its own stretch of wall. And when his enemies kept inviting him down for a meeting, he already had his answer. He was doing a great work, he told them, and he couldn't come down, because the work would stop while he was gone. Once a plan is written, the work has a name and so do the distractions. You can say both out loud, and you can say no.

One page holds it, or an index card if you write small. You've done most of the thinking already, every time you worked a page in Your Turn. At the top, in your own handwriting, write the sentence this book has been carrying. A steadfast man stops waiting, picks up the weight, goes first, and works for God's pay, not the room's. Remember what that last word means. God's pay is the inheritance a Father keeps for His sons, by grace, not wages you earn your way in with. Now circle the mark that's faintest on you. Just one. The whole page leans toward it. Under that, a single word for how you usually leave the room when it gets hard. Anger, the itch, or the drift. You already know which.

Next, one sentence, with a verb in it, about the season you're actually in and what God has set in front of you there. A retired man might write that he'll walk alongside two younger men and his grandson this year. A man in his first year of being single again might write that he'll drive straight, drive slow, and call his kids every week. Then a brother, by name, with a day and an hour. "Dale, Thursdays at noon, by phone." If there's no name yet, write "Ask somebody by Friday." That counts. It may be the most important line on the page. Then the man further down the road, his name and the first question you'll bring him. If you're the older one now, turn it around, and write his name and what you plan to offer. Then the rhythm, the handful of quiet things that hold your week together. Five, maybe ten. Not thirty.

Then the house. One move toward your wife, or toward the wife who isn't here yet. One toward each child, or toward a kid with no dad around if you don't have kids. If a son or daughter is nearing the line into grown-up life, a date on the calendar. Then the work, in one line. One man writes that the plant gets his whole heart, for the Lord. Another writes that his wife is going to hear the truth about his hours. Then the drawer, the hidden thing, and the name of the Nathan who's going to hear it. Men skip this line more than any other. Leave it on the page. And at the bottom, the move. One thing, this week, that everything above it is asking for. It's often small. "Ask Dale." "Phone sleeps in the kitchen tonight." Sign it and date it. Men need markers. This page is one.

A goal is a place you'd like to end up. Closer to God. Gentler at home. Square with Dad. You can't do a goal on a Thursday. You can do a move. A move has a verb, a person, and a day. Thursday, after the kids are down, ask your wife what she needs most from you right now. Sunday afternoon, call your father and thank him for one specific thing. Either it happened or it didn't. There's no other score. Hollis has never written "become a good man" in that notebook. He's written something like twenty thousand small moves, and the good man is what they add up to. Solomon saw the difference when he said the plans of the diligent lead to plenty, as surely as hurry leads to want. Diligence isn't a Sunday word. It lives on weekdays.

I've watched a lot of these pages die, and they mostly go one of a few ways. Some are too long. If you can't read it in the truck in ninety seconds, it's too long. Some are kept secret, and a plan in a drawer is a wish. Hand a copy to your brother and give him permission to ask. Some have no dates. "Soon" is fog. Thursday is a square on the calendar. Some were born in a feeling and nowhere else. Retreat fire is a fine place to start, as long as you reread the page on some dull afternoon, keep what still stands, and cross out the rest. Some are never looked at again. Every ninety days, sit down with your brother and the page. What got done. What changed. What the move is now. Then a clean sheet. And the one men miss is a page with God left off it. James says that instead of bragging about tomorrow, we ought to say that if the Lord wills, we'll live and do this or that. The whole page lives under that line. You hand it to Him, and He's allowed to edit. Held in an open hand, a plan is something He can use. Clutched in a fist, the same plan turns into an idol with bullet points. Proverbs says a man plans his course in his heart, and the Lord sets his steps. You plan. He decides which moves land, and when. Write it, pray over it, and let Him keep it.

A new baby in the house means a three-line page, and three lines is wisdom. Time at the counter with her. The night feeding you take. Nine o'clock, your brother on the phone. A man newly single again starts his first-year page with a rule, no detours. Under it, the floor. A weekly call to the kids. Their weekends, never traded away. The brother. Church, awkward room and all. Rebuilding is a year-two project. An older man's page has room for lines a young man's can't carry yet. The hill he's asking God for. The younger man he's moving toward. The conversation with a grown child he keeps putting off, with a date written next to it. Roy's page said "Say it to my son on the porch" for two months. He finally said it, because it was written down, and because Hollis asked him about it every Thursday. A man whose wife has died may have the shortest page of all. After she passed, Mr. Pruitt's first one said, in full, "Coffee with Ed Wednesdays. Trees."

Near the end of his life, Joshua gathered the whole nation and made them choose who they'd serve. Then he told them his own answer, out loud. He and his household would serve the Lord. Men have carved that over their front doors for three thousand years. He didn't wait for a better season or for the feeling of readiness. He named his next move where witnesses could hear it and hold him to it. Your page is that sentence in your own hand. Plain, dated, signed, read out loud to a brother, held loosely before God. Then you run with it.

Writing a move down will make you feel exposed, because now it can be checked. That exposure is a mercy. Once a brother has seen the page, your good intentions stop being a weight you carry by yourself. The Father who sets a man's steps can set yours before this week is out. Do the thing at the bottom of the page. Fifty-five years in, Hollis will tell you the next thing was always enough. It will be for you too. Paper makes a plan real, with a verb, a person, and a day. You plan the course. The Lord sets the steps.
