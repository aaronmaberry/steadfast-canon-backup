# Introduction

:::epigraph
Therefore, my dear brothers and sisters, stand firm. Let nothing move you. Always give yourselves fully to the work of the Lord, because you know that your labor in the Lord is not in vain.
1 Corinthians 15:58
:::

If you added up the hours of my grown life that mattered, a lot of them would turn out to have happened at tables. A kitchen table nobody wiped after supper. A folding table in the back of a church hall. The cab of a pickup in a dark lot, engine ticking as it cooled. A diner counter where the waitress pours before you ask because she knows which stool is yours. The men around those tables with me have been nineteen and they've been seventy. They've driven trucks and run companies. And under all of it, they kept circling the same three questions. What is a man? Am I one? If I'm not, is it too late?

This book is my long answer, the one I give after the coffee's gone cold and the man hasn't left yet. It isn't the only answer you'll find. It's the one I've watched hold up in plain houses over a lot of years, and I've written it the way I'd talk if you pulled up a chair.

At the center of these pages is one sentence. I didn't discover what's in it. Every piece is older than all of us, and it's been sitting in plain view in the Bible the whole time. My work has been putting it into words a tired man can carry to his job on a Tuesday. I've tested it against my own life for a long time, and against men in every season you can name, and I haven't found the spot where it gives way. Here it is. A steadfast man stops waiting, picks up the weight, goes first, and works for God's pay, not the room's. Those are the four marks. Early in the book we'll lay them out side by side, and then we'll take them one at a time, Stop Waiting, Pick Up the Weight, Go First, and Work for God's Pay.

Notice what kind of sentence it is. It doesn't describe what a man feels. Plenty of good books start inside a man's chest, with longing and wild country, and a few of them helped me when I was young. I'm not arguing with them. I'm making a plainer claim. The four marks are things you do on ordinary days, and people can see them. They show on a man the way thirty years of work shows on his hands. You can see them when they're there and you can see when they're missing, and no amount of feeling hides either one. One word on that last mark, so nobody misreads it. God's pay isn't a wage, and you can't work your way into His family. It's the inheritance a Father gives to sons He already calls His own. We'll spend real time there later in the book.

Every season is already sitting at this table, so find your chair. You might be single and still waiting for somebody to say out loud that you're a man. You might be newly married, or buried so deep in the baby years that nobody in your house has slept a full night since March. You might be providing everything except yourself. You might be divorced and still a father, every other weekend on the calendar and every day in your head. The kids might be gone and the house too quiet. You might be retired and unsure what a man does with a morning nobody scheduled. You might be standing in a kitchen where her coffee cup is still upside down in the drainer. You're not a footnote here. You're who this is for.

I'm not only writing to the man with the mortgage and the minivan. When the counsel needs to bend toward your season, I'll turn and say so right there in the paragraph. I won't stop every chapter to take roll. Near the end of the book we'll sit with each season on purpose, and until then, trust that I see you.

And if the house is loud and the page count looks impossible, here's a shorter road. Read the opening chapter, and for each chapter you read, work its page in Your Turn at the back of the book, one a week. Then read the one near the end that walks through a man's seasons, one chapter from the house that fits where you sit, and one about your kids if you have them. Come back for the rest when life gives you room. The book will wait.

We start with an ordinary day, because that's where a man's life actually gets settled. An ordinary Tuesday names the problem. Next we tell the truth about the confusing room you're standing in, and then we put the four marks in your hands until you can say them without looking down. After that we go back to what you inherited and what's still yours to answer for. The empty chair at the table you grew up around. The anger, the itch, and the drift. The Father who doesn't leave. The handoff you may never have gotten from home, and how to leave it without slamming the door. The brother beside you and the older man ahead of you. The trouble inside you that would still be there if your dad had been perfect, and the Word that gives a man a fixed point again. Then we go further back, to the two gardens at the front and the center of the human story, so you can quit living as if you were the first man to try this. Then the house, with a wife if there is one and a life with real purpose if there isn't, the marriage bed, a daughter, and a son. And then the long road of work, feelings, seasons, the years nobody claps for, a page you write in your own hand, and the men who stay.

Tuesday runs underneath all of it. Not the altar call or the retreat weekend. The day with nothing on the calendar, when nobody's watching. Somewhere in every chapter there's a picture about that size, and that's on purpose.

Each chapter opens with one verse from the Bible and ends on one sentence to carry out the door. The rest of the Scripture is told in plain words inside the story, and the Scripture notes at the back show where each piece comes from, so you can open your own Bible and check me. The work for each chapter waits in the back of the book too, in a section called Your Turn, one page per chapter, with the same four short lines every time. I'll explain them here once.

The first line, Name it, asks you to say, out loud and in your own words, what the chapter touched in you. Men are good at nodding and bad at saying "that's me," and nothing moves in a man until he says it. The second line, Do this week, gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday. Only one. If that's all you take from each chapter, you'll have done more with this book than most men do with any book. The third line, Tell a brother, asks you to say it to another man. A mark you keep to yourself never sets. If you don't have that man yet, there's help for that further on in the book. Until you get there, telling a brother can mean writing it where you'll see it and telling God, who has heard worse. The fourth line, Carry this, is the same sentence the chapter ended on, the kind of line you could text a friend without feeling like you're preaching at him.

I know how a worn-out man reads a book like this, so one more thing. At some point you'll reach a chapter without having done the last one's thing. When that happens, don't skip ahead in shame and don't quit. Tell your brother you didn't do it. That counts as the third line. Then do it this week. Every man in these pages has missed a week. The ones who changed are the ones who said so and went again.

You'll meet men here by name. Roy, who turned in a state trooper's badge. Tomás, whose baby will not sleep. Kenny, divorced, who calls his son every Tuesday. Micah, who sat at the single men's table at weddings for six years. Anthony in a doorway. Walt, whose shop got bigger than his house. Others too. They're composites, built from real conversations with real men, with the names and details mixed so nobody can be picked out, and so you might find yourself in them without finding your neighbor.

This book can stand by itself. Read it, do one thing a week, and find one other man to do it with. That's enough for a man to change. If there's no other man yet, don't wait for him to start. Write it down. Tell God. Your brother can catch up when he shows up.

We also built a twenty-four-session training on the same backbone, with videos, printable guides, and a class that a church, a small group, or one man on his own can run. There's a short map of those sessions in the back of this book, and the full guide is at walksteadfast.com. The book is the book and the training is the training. Use either. Use both. Just don't binge four chapters on a Saturday and call it done.

Paul told the believers at Corinth to stand firm and let nothing move them, and to keep giving themselves to the Lord's work, because nothing done for Him is wasted. That's the whole book in a sentence older than any of mine. Get your coffee and pull up a chair. Let me tell you about a Tuesday.
