# 2. You Are Not Crazy

:::epigraph
So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them.
Genesis 1:27
:::

Most men I know can't get a clear look at what a man ought to be, and it isn't because they're slow or don't care. Three things stand in the way. They've been handed too many instructions, and the instructions fight each other. Almost nobody older ever sat them down and drew them one clear picture. And somewhere along the way they decided the confusion was their own fault, so they quit asking out loud. That's why so many good men feel lost inside a life that looks fine from the street. So here's what I want you to hear first. You're not crazy. The confusion is real, and much of it comes from outside you. The gap you feel is an actual gap.

A man I'll call Garrett laid the whole thing out for me over breakfast at a diner. He's somewhere in the middle of his working years, good at his job, the kind of man people call when a project's on fire. A few minutes in, he pushed his plate to the side and barely touched it after that. Then he started counting on his fingers. Six different sets of instructions for being a man, gathered over a lifetime, and every one of them canceled the other five. He'd been trying to obey all six at once, as if they added up to one plan.

He wasn't angry. He was tired, the kind of tired that comes from chasing a target somebody keeps moving while somebody else keeps score. One voice had told him manhood was a costume he could take off. Another said it was the deepest thing in him. Another said he got to decide what it meant, and the next said it had been decided long ago and he was flunking. Church had handled him as a problem to manage, the locker room as an appetite to feed. After enough years of that he'd started to wonder if the fog was out in the world or if he'd brought it in himself, folded up in his coat pocket. I told him across that table what I just told you. He wasn't crazy. Neither are you.

I knew every word Garrett said, because I've carried my own pile. Be strong. Wait, be gentle. Take the lead. Wait, step back. I was told the push inside me to build and lead was a threat, right up until the day I sat on it, and then I was told sitting on it was why home felt like a cold room. I knew the pile by heart. It never got me through one ordinary Wednesday.

Crossed instructions lock a man up, for one thing. When somebody has condemned every direction, holding still feels like the only safe move, and a lot of what we call passivity in men is exactly this freeze. I've been a man on a tailgate with the tools still zipped in the bag, telling myself a man that unsure had no business touching anything. It sounded humble. The bag never got unzipped. They also turn him into a performer. I know the costume changes. One face on Sunday, one at the job, one for the guys after work, one when he walks in his own door. Whoever woke up that morning keeps getting replaced by whoever the room seems to want. Nobody can do that all week and stay full. Under the costumes is a man who never got a picture solid enough to keep, so he borrows one from whoever's nearest.

And they leave him ashamed, and shame shuts a man's mouth. Hand him orders he can't keep, orders at war with each other, and he'll convict himself every time. A low hum starts up under everything. He won't mention it, sure that once it's said out loud the verdict is final. Garrett had the hum. So did I. Most of the men I know have it. It's the noise a decent man makes while trying to please six maps before the weekend.

:::break
:::

So where did all those instructions come from? Spread them out where you can see them. Once you see which ones were never real maps, you can stop following them. The first came from home, from whoever sat at the head of the table. He taught you what a man is before you could read, and most of us got that lesson in fragments. Maybe he was there and silent. Maybe he'd already gone. Maybe he blew through the door like a storm front. Even faithful fathers left gaps. We'll walk into that room properly later in the book. For now, notice that you left home with a torn piece of map and went looking for the rest.

The culture was glad to supply it. That second map looked at the itch to build, compete, protect, and take charge, and called it outdated wiring. Then in the same breath it mocked guys who never grew up or committed or took on anything hard, and never admitted it had talked them out of all of it. The third map glowed on a screen. Be the sitcom dad lost in his own kitchen, or the brute some boys were taught to admire, or the loner hero of the movie every kid runs in his head. That hero has no worn-out wife, no father who phones twice a year, no teenager who stopped talking, no church, no friends, no sink full of dishes. His life has no street address. We were trained to want it while living in a real house with a real mortgage.

The fourth came from the job and said a man equals his paycheck. The fifth came from the phone and said he equals his feed, what he watches and who watches him. The sixth came from church, and it tore down the middle. Some pulpits handed you "spiritual leader" and never said what it looks like on a Wednesday. Others cranked "warrior" and "king" so loud there was no room for a car note, a mother in the ICU, or the graveyard shift. Neither one told you what to do on Monday.

Six maps, and none of them agree where north is. One true picture was never in the box. You can haul all six. You can't navigate by them. Each new heading wipes out the last, and the man who keeps trying gets dizzy and then decides the dizziness is his own fault.

Where you are in life changes where it hurts, but the bruise is the same. At nineteen, school calls your restlessness a disorder, your phone says manhood is followers, youth group says servant leader, and older guys say it's how many girls. You try the whole menu, crash, and blame yourself. Blame the maps. Single at thirty, you hear without anyone saying it that you're on pause until a wedding makes you a man. You aren't. This life already counts, and it's already yours to steer. As a new dad, inside one week you're told to earn more and be home more, to lead and to defer, to be a rock for the baby and stay in touch with your feelings. It won't fit in the calendar because it was never built to.

In the middle years, plenty of men quietly quit. Manhood shrinks to the job and the drive home, and they tune out every voice since the voices cancel anyway. It passes for peace. It's a man who folded the maps and sat on them. Some of those men run whole companies and still couldn't give their own son one sentence about what a man is. A title is not a definition. Later on, a man looks at the picture he was handed fifty years ago while his grandkids scroll past videos mocking it, and he wonders if he built on sand. Some of what he got was true and some wasn't. The true parts didn't spoil. He needs a friend to help him sort the pile and keep the good pieces standing. Every age carries the same maps, and you're not alone with them.

:::break
:::

Now let me answer that hum, slowly. Your sense that being a man is something real? You're right. God thought of it. On the first page of the Bible He made mankind in His own image, male and female, both bearing that image, and He called the work good before either of them had done a day's labor. Manhood is one way that image walks around in the world. It was His design from the start.

Your sense that the instructions don't fit together? Also right. You've been navigating with crossed maps, and the lost feeling goes on their bill, not yours. And the hunger to hear someone say plainly what a man does? That's a good hunger. Older men were supposed to hand you a picture. Plenty of us never got one, and we've been guessing ever since. What's ahead is simpler than a shelf of books and costs more than one. It fits in a sentence. It has to be lived on regular days, including the ones when you feel nothing. A rush of feeling won't carry it, and neither will your best Sunday.

Hauling a contradiction around is exhausting, and that's the tiredness you've been feeling. You've spent strength on freight that was labeled wrong. If that hum has run under your days for years, let it settle tonight. You can see the maps now, so lay them down, and let God take off your back what He never put there. You don't need the rest of your life figured out first. Other hands drew the maps that got you lost. The line coming next is short enough to learn by heart and big enough to spend your life on.
