# 7. Work for God's Pay

:::epigraph
Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord, not for human masters, since you know that you will receive an inheritance from the Lord as a reward. It is the Lord Christ you are serving.
Colossians 3:23-24
:::

Nearly every man knows the drive home with the radio up and a trophy riding shotgun. That night it feels permanent. Years later one just like it turns up at a yard sale, in a cardboard box on a folding table with FREE down the side in marker. A gold batter missing an arm. A bowling pin glued to marble. A sales plaque from a company that folded. Somebody's Saturdays were in that box, and nights he was sure would define him. By noon nobody had claimed it. By spring it was at the curb. That's how the rewards you can hold tend to go. They're great for a night. Then your hand's empty and you go hunting again.

The fourth mark is Work for God's Pay. Do it for the Lord's reward, not the room's applause. Hear that word the way Paul meant it. God's pay is an inheritance, what a Father gives His sons because they're His, and in Christ you're a son by grace before you've lifted a finger. Nobody earns an inheritance. You work like a son because you already are one. The other three marks lean on this one. Stop Waiting, Pick Up the Weight, and Go First all cost you something today, and a man keeps at them only if he trusts the Father who's watching. Why fix what nobody asked you to fix if nobody ever sees it? Why say "that's mine" if the blame would have slid right past you? Why go first if the street is all there is? Those questions don't go quiet until this mark answers them.

You're already working for some kind of pay. Every man is, from the minute his feet hit the floor. The only question is whose. Your father's approval, alive or not, and more men are still working for that one than will admit it. Comfort, which a new dad is chasing by week six and a retired man on too many afternoons. Being right, which a young husband will trade a whole evening for. The number on the screen, checked before you're out of the parking lot. The nod from across the room. Being wanted, which single men chase quietly and married men forget they're chasing. Being remembered, with your name on something. Some of those are good in their place. Every one of them runs out. The nod dies in the parking lot. The bigger number shows up without the feeling that was supposed to ride along. Your father's approval, if it ever comes, is smaller than the version you rehearsed. Collect the kind of pay that lasts one night and you end up on that folding table.

:::break
:::

Jesus never told men to stop wanting a reward. He told them where to put it. Don't pile up treasure down here, He said, where moths and vermin eat it and thieves break in and take it. Pile it up in heaven, where none of that can reach it, because your heart goes wherever your treasure is. Moths, vermin, thieves. He described the yard sale two thousand years early. Earlier in the same sermon He told them how the lasting kind gets stored. When you give, don't let your left hand know what your right hand is doing. Keep it secret, and your Father, who sees what's done in secret, will reward you.

Every kind of pay on that old list needs an audience. This one asks you to send the audience home. A widow's driveway shoveled while her kitchen light is still off. A check with no story attached. Praise spoken about a man who'll never find out you said it. A single guy's Saturday spent hauling a young family's couch up to a second-floor apartment, with nobody to tell about it that night. Hidden work still has a witness, and He doesn't forget a thing. And it's bigger than what happens at church. Paul's word to those worn-out workers was whatever you do. The invoice and the diaper, the spreadsheet and the graveyard shift. This pay isn't only for the guy on the stage. It's for the man on the ladder and the man at the sink with nobody watching, when he does it for the Lord. Who you're doing it for is what turns it into treasure.

Men get this wrong in both directions, so stay with it. You can't clock in for an inheritance. You get one because you're in the family, and the only way into this family is grace. Paul wrote that we're saved by grace through faith, and that it's God's gift and not our own doing, so nobody gets to boast. Then he said we're God's handiwork, made in Christ Jesus for good works that God laid out ahead of time for us to walk in. Look at the order. Saved by grace, as a gift. Then the good works God already prepared. The work comes after you're His, never as the price of becoming His. So when this book says work for God's pay, it doesn't mean work hard and maybe God will let you in. It means you're already a son, so work like one, and trust your Father to remember every hidden thing. The pay isn't wages. It's a son's inheritance, and the best part of it is the Father Himself.

Jesus told a story about a master who came home and found his servant had been faithful with the little he was given. Well done, the master said. You've been faithful with a few things, so I'll put you over many, and come on in and share my joy. Well done. More put in your hands. A door open into His joy. The pay is mostly welcome. What a son wants at the end of the road is a Father glad to see him coming up the walk.

Moses chose well. He grew up in a palace with the treasury a hallway away, and he walked out to stand with slaves. The writer of Hebrews says he counted disgrace for Christ's sake worth more than all the treasure in Egypt, because he was looking ahead to his reward. He saw past the treasure room to something worth more, and seeing it is what let him walk away. A man can leave almost anything with something better in view. He can't leave much when all that's in the frame is what he's losing.

:::break
:::

You don't need to interview him. A man working for God's pay shows it. He does good and doesn't bring it up. He drops arguments he could win. He stays, in his marriage and his church and his town, on days he'd have every excuse to leave. He takes the low seat without scanning the room. He forgives a man who doesn't deserve it, because his Father has already forgiven him more. He prays for grandkids not yet born. He teaches a boy to work, and that boy will teach another long after he's gone. Mr. Pruitt, a widower who planted forty pecan trees the spring after he buried his wife, keeps them like that. The nuts are for other people. The watering is his. That way of living only makes sense if this life isn't the end of the story. It isn't.

The loud version of this is a counterfeit, so let me say plainly what God's pay doesn't include. No promise of health, a bigger house, an easier year, or kids who land where you aimed them. Staying married doesn't promise warmth by spring. Forgiving a man doesn't promise he'll ever apologize. Preachers who sold those deals left wreckage behind them. Paul wrote down the real scale after he'd been beaten and shipwrecked. He called his troubles light and momentary, and said they were producing a glory that would outweigh them all, so he kept his eyes on what can't be seen, because what's seen is temporary and what isn't seen lasts forever. Light and momentary, from a man covered in scars. He never said the trouble would vanish. He said it was producing something. The hard years stay on the record. They're the raw material the reward is made from.

Fix your eyes, then, every day. Fix is Paul's word, and a glance won't do it. Say one line before you swing your legs out of bed. Today is seen, and today counts. Every week, do one kindness nobody will ever know about, and if the story leaks, you picked the wrong kindness. On the drive home after a day nobody thanked you for, practice hearing "well done" in His voice. Catch yourself performing while it's starting. Jesus was plain that good done for a crowd has already gotten everything it's going to get. The second you look around to see who noticed, you've traded a Father's "well done" for a glance from the room. And pray past Thursday. A single man's apartment is room enough for this. So is a grandfather's porch. It's the same audience to let go of, and the same Father doing the seeing.

Put all four marks on one life and look at him. He doesn't walk around the job that needs doing. He says "that's mine" and lets it stand. He opens the first door while the street watches. And the pay he's after is out of reach of any yard sale. There's an old word for a man like that. Steadfast. A steadfast man stops waiting, picks up the weight, goes first, and works for God's pay, not the room's.

The small rewards pay out the same night, and that's why men keep picking them. Raise your aim. Live for the reward that lasts, and then go back to work. The Father who sees in secret forgets nothing, and He's keeping an inheritance for His son, and the words you're longing for are being kept for you until the day He says them. Some kind of pay already has you. Name it, and aim higher.
