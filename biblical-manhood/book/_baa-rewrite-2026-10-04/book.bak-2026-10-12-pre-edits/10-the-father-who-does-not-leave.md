# 10. The Father Who Does Not Leave

:::epigraph
But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion for him; he ran to his son, threw his arms around him and kissed him.
Luke 15:20
:::

Three o'clock at any elementary school, the doors bang open and a few hundred kids pour out hunting for exactly one vehicle. Watch their faces. The ones who spot their ride go loose all over, backpack swinging, halfway into a story about recess before the seat belt clicks. Then there are the few still scanning. Minivan, not theirs. Pickup, not theirs. One of them ends up on the curb beside a teacher who keeps checking her watch and trying not to let him see her do it.

Curtis is in that line every afternoon. He gets there twenty minutes early and sits with the engine off and the radio off. Years ago he swore none of his kids would ever be the one on the curb, and not once have they been. What he has never had is the other side of it, walking out a door and finding somebody already waiting for him. Nobody came for Curtis. He learned the way home on foot, and he got good at it, the way boys get good at things nobody should have to learn.

This chapter is about a Father who is already parked out front when the bell rings. For a lot of men, though, the word father is exactly where the trouble starts, so that's where we'll start too.

Tell a man that God is his Father and he'll hear it through whoever sat at the end of his childhood table. If that chair was empty, father means somebody who doesn't show, and God becomes a distant fact, out there somewhere, busy, not here. If it held a silent man, prayer feels like leaving voicemails on a number that was disconnected years ago. If the chair was dangerous, a boy learns to check his father's mood before he speaks, and later his faith turns into a daily inspection for whatever God is upset about, with a strong hunch that it's him. Even the men who had a good dad carry something. Good fathers get tired and miss things, and somewhere under those men's prayers sits a quiet guess that God will miss things too.

I've prayed to a copy and called it faith. Most of us have. We hold our fathers up to heaven like an old photograph and assume God must look about like that, and it has soured a lot of prayers. The Bible runs it the other way around. God is the original, and every earthly father is a copy, some of them decent and some of them terrible. So set the photograph face down on the table for a minute. Only one Man has ever seen the original. Near the end of His time with the disciples, Philip asked Him to just show them the Father, as if that would settle everything. Jesus asked him how he could have walked beside Him all that time and still not know Him. Anyone who had seen Him, He said, had seen the Father. If you want to know what God is like as a dad, you don't have to guess from your own. You can watch Jesus.

:::break
:::

Look at the order things happen in. Jesus hadn't preached a sermon yet. He hadn't healed anybody or called a single fisherman off his boat. He came up out of the Jordan, still dripping from His baptism, and the Father spoke from heaven where everyone could hear. This is My Son. I love Him. I'm well pleased with Him. No assignment came first, and no tryout. The blessing came before the work, and the work grew out of the blessing. That's how it runs in God's house.

Some of you had a father who made you earn every scrap of approval, and the scraps were thin when they came. This Father doesn't run His house that way. If you belong to Christ, that same voice of love and delight has already been spoken over you. You didn't earn it, and a bad week can't take it back. If there's a newborn at your house, stand over the crib tonight and pay attention to what's going on in your chest. You adore that baby, and he hasn't done one useful thing in his life. That's closer to how the Father sees you than anything your report cards taught you.

And He carries. Moses, looking back over forty years in the desert, told Israel that God had carried them the way a man carries his little boy, the whole way, through the hard miles too. Jesus said the Father knows what you need before you ask Him, and your needing doesn't annoy Him. If needing anybody used to be dangerous in your house, read that twice. When the Father corrects you, it's because you're His. The letter to the Hebrews says discipline is how you can tell you're a son and not a stray. Correction and rage are different animals. A father who is correcting you hasn't left.

He won't leave, either. On His last night before the cross, with His friends about to watch Him get dragged off in the dark, Jesus told them He would not leave them as orphans. He would come to them. He picked that word on purpose, because orphans is exactly what they were about to feel like. Some of you have felt like one since the second grade. His answer to orphans is Himself. If your marriage has ended, you may need this more than anyone reading. The Father didn't move out when your wife did, and He didn't add His disappointment to the pile with everybody else's. You may have quit praying because you figured He had. He never quit watching the road for you.

:::break
:::

You'd think this would be the easy chapter. For plenty of men it's the hardest one in the book, because a boy who raised himself never got to practice being anybody's son. You can sign off on every line of the doctrine and still lock up the moment the weight is supposed to shift onto somebody else's shoulders. After enough years alone, leaning feels like falling. Curtis said it better than I can. "I believe God's my Father. I just don't know how to be somebody's son. I never got to be on the receiving end." I'd rather hear that than a polished testimony.

Men who learn to be sons late usually learn it the way they learned everything else, by doing it badly until they don't. It starts with the name. Call God "Lord" in your other prayers if you like, but somewhere every day, out loud and alone, call Him Father while it still feels awkward. Paul wrote that the Spirit God puts in His children is already calling out to Him as Abba, and Abba is what a small boy calls his dad. You have permission. If you're sixty and you've never once said it, give it a week of feeling foolish. It warms up after a while, the way your hands do when you come in out of the cold.

Then take Him whatever you've been running to instead. He hears it before any brother or counselor does, and He can take it raw. "Father, I'm about to lose it with the kids." "Father, I'm about to pick up that phone." Let Him tell you who you are, too. Read something every morning that God says about His children, and answer it as though it's true of you, because it is. A man who was never told who he is needs to hear it more than once, and the Father doesn't get tired of saying it. When a load comes this year that's heavier than you are, and one will, pray against every reflex you've got. "This is too heavy for me. Carry it, and carry me." Then let a brother get under one corner of it.

None of this works from a distance. The Father speaks in His Word, His other kids gather in His church, and He listens when you pray. A man who drops by only at Christmas and Easter will find that His voice still sounds like a stranger's on a Wednesday.

Something happens to the man who raised you, or didn't, once God starts fathering you. You see him more clearly, and sometimes that stings. You quit trying to squeeze him into a shape he never had, and you quit searching his face for a look that was never going to come. You can thank him for what was good, name what hurt, and set down the rage. Forgiveness opens up once you no longer need him to pay you back. If you're young and sure you don't need a father at all, learn this now and save yourself twenty years of searching faces. That old chair was always too small for the hollow place in a man's chest. Only one Father fits it.

:::

Curtis started practicing in the pickup line, since a parked car with the engine off was the one place the awkwardness cost him nothing. He'd say "Father," then one sentence about the day. For the first week it felt like talking to the dashboard. He kept at it. Somewhere in the second week it stopped feeling like talking to glass and started feeling like somebody was in the passenger seat.

Then one afternoon his youngest came through the doors, looked down the line, saw their car, and stopped searching. Curtis watched that small face change, and it hit him what was being offered. For years he had been the dad holding a place in line. He had never once been the kid who finds his ride. Now he was being told that part belonged to him too.

Jesus told a story about a father like that. His younger son asked for his share of the estate early, carried it off to a far country, and spent every cent. When the money was gone and he was feeding another man's pigs and wishing he could eat what they ate, he started home with an apology rehearsed, hoping to be taken on as a hired hand. He never got to finish the speech. While he was still a long way off, his father saw him. You only see somebody that far down the road if you've been watching it. In that culture a grown man didn't run in public. This one ran, and he had his arms around his boy before the boy said a word.

That son didn't clean himself up before his father came for him, and you don't have to either. The searching can stop, and so can the tryout. Your place with the Father was settled in His Son, and today's performance was never the verdict. If your career is going well, hear that slowly. A title is not a father, and applause makes a poor one. In one of his letters John stops to marvel that the Father loves us enough to call us His children, and then he adds that this is exactly what we are. So tomorrow, before your feet hit the floor, say the word, clumsily if that's all you've got, and say it again the morning after. He knows sons learn slowly. Let your shoulders drop. You learned the word father from a copy. The original was in the line before the bell.
