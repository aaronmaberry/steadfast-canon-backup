# 28. Give Your Son a Hill

Lyle was eleven, or maybe twelve, the fall morning the kitchen tap coughed air and quit.

The house drank from the hill behind it: a spring a quarter mile up the pasture, boxed in concrete by his grandfather, with a pipe run down to the kitchen and on to the stock trough. When the tap quit, somebody had to climb up and see.

His father had cleaned that box a dozen times with Lyle handing up the bucket. That morning he stood at the dry sink, looked out at the hill, and said, "Spring's quit. Go see to it." Not "let's go see." Go.

"I stood at the yard gate and looked up that hill," Lyle told me a lifetime later, his hair white and the farm long sold, "and I knew I couldn't. That lid weighed more than I did. I looked back, and he'd sat down on the back step with the last of the coffee. He wasn't going anywhere."

It took most of the morning. He pried the lid off and dropped it once on his fingers. He found a drowned possum against the screen and lifted it out with his hands. He dug the silt out in water so cold his arms ached to the shoulder, and he seated the pipe wrong, then pulled it and seated it right. Three times he looked down the hill. Three times his father was on the step, and the coffee in his hand had to be cold by then.

The trough pipe was running before Lyle was halfway down. When the boy walked into the yard wet to the collar, mud to the knees, blood on two knuckles, his father looked at him a long moment and said, "Well. I figured you could."

"He never got up off that step," Lyle said. "If he'd come up, I'd have learned I couldn't do it without him. He stayed put, and I learned I could."

Give your son a hill. You've given him a name, and a name without a hill is only a compliment. A boy becomes what you called him by climbing something that proves it.

## Boys need difficulty

Boys don't become men by being protected from difficulty. They become men by being handed difficulty that's slightly beyond them, with a man watching who doesn't rescue too soon. Isaac carried the wood up the mountain.

:::margin
Genesis 22:6 (NIV)
"Abraham took the wood for the burnt offering and placed it on his son Isaac, and he himself carried the fire and the knife. As the two of them went on together,"
:::

Placed it on his son. Abraham kept the fire and the knife and gave the boy the load, and the two of them went on together, one carrying and one watching. David's father gave him the sheep.

:::margin
1 Samuel 17:34-35 (NIV)
"But David said to Saul, 'Your servant has been keeping his father's sheep. When a lion or a bear came and carried off a sheep from the flock, I went after it, struck it and rescued the sheep from its mouth.'"
:::

Nobody sent David a lion. His father sent him to the hills with something real to keep, and the lion found him there, and by the time Goliath stepped out he'd already been up a few hills. And Jesus grew up in a carpenter's shop under His father's eye, and Luke says He grew in wisdom and stature. That's a boy with a hill.

The boy without one is loved and comfortable and starving. He goes and finds a hill of his own, and the ones he finds are nearly always worse: the fight, the dare, the back road at night with the headlights off. Or he does the quieter thing. He never leaves the basement, because nobody ever showed him he could climb anything, and the whole world looks like a hill with nobody at the bottom of it.

## What a hill is

A hill isn't a chore chart or a travel ball schedule.

A hill is real. It matters to somebody besides him: the house has no water until it's done. Boys can smell a made-up job from across the yard. Sweeping a clean garage to learn responsibility is a lie, and he knows it.

A hill is slightly beyond him. Not what he can already do, and not what a man can do. The next thing. Too easy and he learns nothing. Too hard and he learns he can't.

A hill has real risk, bounded. He can fail, get hurt a little, come down with the tap still dry. No way to fail, no hill. It's a ride. But a father chooses with care. Lyle's father knew that box, knew the copperheads were down for the year, and was on the step.

A hill has an outcome he owns. "I tried" is what a boy says. "It's done," or "it isn't yet, and here's what I'm doing about it," is what a man says, and he learns it on hills where nobody accepts "I tried." The water ran or it didn't.

And a hill is followed by a name. When he comes down, whatever happened, the father speaks. He was called capable, and then he climbed, and now he knows it's true.

## The hills, by age

In the small years, a hill is a real job that matters: every grocery bag carried in, even if it takes four trips. He isn't helping. He's working. Tell him so. Once he's in school, the hill is ownership: a garden bed, a pet's whole life, the trash out without a word from you. In the last years before he's a teenager, a job with real money for a man who isn't you, a challenge that scares him a little, and at least one hill he climbs alone with you in sight and not coming. In the high school years, a boss who'll fire him if he's lazy, money he loses some of, a project he owns to the finish, and a decision with consequences you don't override. Then the launch, and the hill is his own life, and you're a man he can call.

Not on the list: trophies, when the sport is his father's ego and every loss is the referee's fault. And screens, because a screen is a machine for simulating hills without the weight. Ten thousand levels beaten, and the tap still dry.

## The father's part

Give it, and don't do it. You're faster and it's easier, and every time you do the job yourself you've stolen a hill.

Watch, and don't rescue. Let him struggle. Let him drop the lid on his fingers. His mother will find this harder than you do, so lead here, gently, and explain why. Rescuing a boy who isn't in danger teaches him he needs rescue. Watching a boy who isn't in danger teaches him he doesn't. Sitting there with your hands empty will feel like passivity. It isn't. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. The passive father is the one who climbs it himself. It's easier, and it forms nothing.

Let him fail small so he doesn't fail big. Forty dollars lost now because he didn't plan will save him forty thousand later. A boy never allowed to fail fails for the first time with a wife and a mortgage and nobody on the step.

When he comes down, ask what happened, what he learned, what he'd do differently. Not a lecture. Questions, then waiting. Then name what he did: "I figured you could." "You didn't quit." Every hill ends with a name, or it's only a hard day.

And raise the hill. Next time, a little more. A boy who climbs a slightly bigger hill every year of his boyhood leaves your house a man, with the calluses to prove it.

:::margin
Hebrews 12:11 (NIV)
"No discipline seems pleasant at the time, but painful. Later on, however, it produces a harvest of righteousness and peace for those who have been trained by it."
:::

Trained by it. It isn't pleasant for him, or for you on the step. Later on, a harvest.

## How fathers get it wrong

Some give too big a hill too fast and crush the boy, and now the hill is where he fails, and he stops climbing. Slightly beyond. Not a mountain. Some give their own hill instead of the boy's, the sport the father wanted, the business he wants handed down. Some rescue, the project finished at midnight, the call to the coach, the debt paid off, and every rescue says the same thing: you can't. And some shame the fall, so that every hill becomes a place of fear he'll walk around for life. Lyle dropped the lid and seated the pipe wrong. His father never said a word about either.

## Hills in every season

The hill changes with your seat. If your boy's still in diapers, the habit starts now: let him work at the toy before you fix it. The father who does everything for his baby because the crying is unbearable will do everything for his teenager because the sulking is. The divorced seat has a trap of its own, the weekend with no hill in it, because you feel guilty and you want him to want to come. He needs jobs that are his at your house too, and a project you leave unfinished on purpose, because unfinished is what pulls a boy back. If you're the grandfather, you hand out hills better than anyone alive, with time and tools and a patience his father hasn't grown yet. If you have no son, there are boys in your church who've never been up a hill because nobody was at the bottom, and you can be the man who points and says go. And your daughter isn't fragile. She needs real work, real ownership, and a father who names her strong when she comes down. The hills look different. The method doesn't.

## Well, I figured you could

Lyle has handed out a lot of hills since, to sons and grandsons and the boys in his Sunday school class. I asked him once for the secret. "Pick the right hill. Stay on the step. Say the right thing when he comes down." Then he laughed. "And don't tell his mother what was in the box."

Paul told his son in the faith to expect hills for life.

:::margin
2 Timothy 2:3 (NIV)
"Join with me in suffering, like a good soldier of Christ Jesus."
:::

Like a good soldier. Not a protected boy. A man trained on hills, who can take the weight and clear the spring when nobody comes.

Your son is standing at the gate looking up. He isn't sure he can. He's looking back at the house.

Sit down on the step, and watch him, and stay where you are.

## Before you turn the page

Staying on the step is the hardest thing in fathering, and if you've been getting up off it for years, you haven't ruined your son. Boys are resilient, and a hill handed late still counts. The Father who put the wood on Isaac's back and walked up beside him knows what it is to watch a son carry something heavy. May He give you the nerve to pick the right hill this week, and the harder nerve to sit down and stay, and may you get to say "I figured you could" when he comes down.
