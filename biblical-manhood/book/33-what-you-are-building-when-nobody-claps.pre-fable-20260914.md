# 33. What You Are Building When Nobody Claps

In July in Texas, foundation crews pour at four in the morning. The concrete has to set before the heat gets to it, so the trucks roll in while it's still dark and the men work under portable lights, and by the time the sun is up the slab is down and the crew is gone.

Nobody photographs a slab. Nobody drives past a finished house and says, "Look at that foundation." The homeowners will live there thirty years and never once think about what's under their feet. And every wall, every window, every room where a child learns to walk and a couple fights and makes up and a family eats ten thousand dinners, all of it sits on what those men did in the dark before anyone was awake.

That's what this chapter is about. Most of what a man builds, he builds when nobody is watching, and the part nobody sees is the part that everything else stands on.

## Most of your life is unwitnessed

Do the math on your own week: the two o'clock feeding, the Tuesday call from the truck, the temptation you turned away from in a hotel room, the tithe check, the coffee you've made for your wife every morning for eleven years, the apology in the kitchen, the lie you didn't tell at work when the true answer cost you, the hour with the Word before the phone, the twenty minutes with a brother, the widow's gutters, the bedtime prayer, the plumb bob you handed a boy at a fence line with four men watching and nobody else on earth.

Nobody clapped for any of it, and nobody is going to. Most of the men in your life don't know it happened, and they never will.

The culture has an answer to that, and the answer is poison. If nobody saw it, it doesn't count. That's the whole logic of the scoreboard from chapter 16. It's why men perform, why they tell the story so that someone will think well of them, why they drift toward the place that claps. If being witnessed is what makes a thing real, then the unwitnessed life is a wasted one, and a man had better get himself seen. Scripture says the exact opposite, and says it in Jesus's own mouth, over and over, in one sermon.

:::margin
Matthew 6:6 (NIV)
"But when you pray, go into your room, close the door and pray to your Father, who is unseen. Then your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you."
:::

Who sees what is done in secret. The unwitnessed thing isn't the wasted thing. It's the one with the highest return, because it's the only kind where you can be sure you weren't doing it for the crowd. The applause you don't get here is the applause that's stored somewhere the weather can't touch. That's the fourth mark in its purest form: a man who expects the greater reward can do a lifetime of unwitnessed good and not feel cheated, because he knows exactly who saw.

## What you're actually building

Let me be concrete, because "faithfulness in obscurity" is a phrase that floats. Here's what a man is actually building with his unwitnessed Tuesdays.

He's building a floor for his children. The thousand dinners nobody remembers become, in a child, the settled sense that the world is stable and somebody's in charge and they're safe. Your kids won't remember a single Tuesday. They'll remember, in their bones, whether the Tuesdays were there. A grown man who can handle a crisis without falling apart is usually a man whose father was at the table ten thousand times without anyone counting.

He's building a marriage that holds at seventy. Roy's marriage didn't survive forty-four years because of anniversaries. It survived because of coffee, and it's better now because of the coffee he started making after he retired. Ten thousand unclapped mornings build something no vacation can. When a couple in their seventies still likes each other, you're looking at a slab poured in the dark for fifty years.

He's building a name that arrives before he does. A reputation isn't built by the things people saw. It's built by the things people found out later that you did when they weren't looking.

:::margin
Proverbs 22:1 (NIV)
"A good name is more desirable than great riches; to be esteemed is better than silver or gold."
:::

A good name is what the proverb calls it, and chapter 27 said a man's last name is the first weight his son carries. What you do when nobody claps is what makes that name a thing to stand on. The feed store credit two counties over comes from forty years of paying bills nobody knew about on time.

He's building a church. Every church runs on men nobody thanks: the ones who stack chairs, unlock the doors, fix the van, sit with the old man in the hospital, teach the fourth-grade boys for twenty-two years. The pastor gets the platform. The men who built the floor under the platform are in the parking lot afterward with their hands in their pockets, and the church would fall over without them, and they know it, and they're fine.

He's building a town. The man at the wastewater plant. Four thousand people drinking clean water because a man they've never heard of stayed awake. Most of what holds a town together is men doing unwitnessed work. He's building a self, which is the one men forget. Character is what gets built when nobody's watching, and it can't be built any other way, and Jesus said so.

:::margin
Luke 16:10 (NIV)
"Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with very little will also be dishonest with much."
:::

Trusted with very little. The little is the unwitnessed. The man who tells the truth when a lie would cost nothing and no one would know is building a man who can tell the truth when it costs everything and everyone's watching. You don't get the second man without the first. The hotel room at midnight is where the man who'll one day stand in a boardroom gets made.

And he's building treasure in heaven, which was chapter 7. Every unclapped act of faithfulness is stored, by a Father who doesn't lose track, in a place where it can't be stolen or eaten by moths. That isn't a metaphor for feeling good about yourself. It's a claim about where your work went. It went into an account, and it's still there.

## The fire

Paul had a picture for this that every man who has ever built anything will understand.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 3:12-13 (NIV)
"If anyone builds on this foundation using gold, silver, costly stones, wood, hay or straw, their work will be shown for what it is, because the Day will bring it to light. It will be revealed with fire, and the fire will test the quality of each person's work."
:::

The fire will test the quality. Every man is building; the only question is what with. And here's what I've come to believe about that passage after a good many years. What's built for applause is straw. It looks fine, and it may look better than the gold on the day, because straw is showy and gold is heavy. But when the fire comes, and Paul says it's coming for every man, the work done for the crowd burns off in a second, and what's left is what was done for the Father in the dark.

Which means the slab poured at four in the morning is gold. The two o'clock feeding is gold. The Tuesday call is gold. And the sermon preached for the sermon's own sake, the gift given so the giver's name would go on the wall, the fatherhood performed at the ballpark and abandoned in the truck: those are straw. The fire is going to sort it out, and I'd rather be a man with a small pile of gold than a large pile of straw.

## The Tuesday after the marker

Now the most practical thing in this chapter, and perhaps the most practical thing in the second half of this book.

You've had markers: a wedding, a baptism, a retreat where you finally said yes to God with tears on your face, the day you gave your son at the fence line, a retirement ceremony where they handed you a plaque and shook your hand and said well done. Chapter 29 said it: the marker is the stone on the riverbank, and the Tuesdays are the land. Every man who has ever had a marker has learned that the Tuesday after is where it gets decided, and the Tuesday after is exactly the day when nobody's clapping.

So here's a Tuesday rhythm for the man after the marker. It's the unwitnessed structure that holds the witnessed moments up. Roy's is the model, because he built his after the hardest marker of his life, but I'll show you how it scales.

The alarm doesn't move. Whatever time you got up when you had to, keep getting up then. A man whose wake-up time is negotiable has a life that's negotiable. Roy still rises at 5:30.

The first hour is the plumb line. Before the phone and before the news: the Word, a chapter, out loud if you can, and a prayer that starts with "Father." Chapters 17 and 10. This is the hour that makes every other hour straight. If you only have fifteen minutes, take fifteen, but take them first.

The floor, which is the two or three family things that don't move no matter what: dinner at the table, the bedtime prayer, Sunday. Chapter 5 gave you this one, and you should write them down, because they're the corner posts.

The twenty minutes with your brother, same time every week, three questions and a prayer, from chapter 13. The man who drops this after the marker has gone back to being alone, and alone is where markers go to die.

One secret good thing a week, for someone, that no one will ever know about, from chapter 7. It recalibrates you toward the Father who sees in secret and away from the crowd that doesn't.

A job by noon, something real that somebody is counting on, every day, from chapter 30. For the retired man this is the difference between assisting and drifting. For the working man it's already there; make sure it's done for the Lord and not only for the boss.

The blessing, from chapter 27, which is the weekly hand on the head and three sentences if you have children at home. If they're grown, the call. If they're across two houses, Tuesday at 7:30.

The notebook from chapter 22: what she told you this week, a line a day.

Sundown, which means nothing carried to bed: the apology made, the true thing said, the anger handed to God, as chapter 9 and Ephesians 4:26 asked.

And Sunday, in the house of the Father, with His other children, as a man with a job there and not a visitor.

That's ten things, and none of them is dramatic and none of them will get you noticed, and together they're a life, and they're the life that makes the markers mean something.

Now watch it scale. Tomás, with a four-month-old and a ten-hour shift, can't do Roy's version. His looks like this: the alarm is set by the baby, so he takes the first fifteen minutes of the two-to-five shift for a Psalm on his phone in the dark while she sleeps. The floor is the twenty minutes after his boots come off and the bedtime prayer over the crib. The twenty minutes with his brother happens on the phone at nine while he does the dishes. The secret good thing is letting his wife sleep an extra hour Saturday and not mentioning it. The blessing is "you're my daughter and I love you" said over a baby who doesn't understand words yet. The notebook is a note on his phone. Sundown is "I'm so tired I'm scared of myself, and I'm sorry, and I'm not going anywhere." Sunday is church, exhausted, in the back row, with the baby. That's a whole rhythm. It fits in the cracks of a life that has no room, and it's gold.

Kenny's looks different again: the first hour before the kids wake on his weekends and before work on the others; the floor is the Tuesday call and the every-other-weekend that never gets traded away; the job by noon is showing up at a business he owns whether he feels like it or not; the secret good thing is often something for his children's mother that she'll never know he did. Micah's, when he was single, had more room in it and more danger, and his floor was the roommates' dinner and the twenty minutes and the phone in the kitchen at night.

Build yours, with ten things or seven or five, whatever fits, and write it on a card. It's the slab, and everything visible sits on it.

## When you're weary

I have to say something about the long middle, because this chapter can sound easy, and it isn't.

There's a stretch in every unwitnessed work when nothing grows. You pour the slab and there's no house yet, only a flat gray thing in the dirt, for months. You make the coffee for a year and she's still distant. You call your son every Tuesday for eight months and he says "nothing." You teach the fourth-grade boys for a decade and can't name one thing that's changed. And the thought comes, in the language of Isaiah: I have labored for nothing.

:::margin
Isaiah 49:4 (NIV)
"But I said, 'I have labored in vain; I have spent my strength for nothing at all. Yet what is due me is in the LORD's hand, and my reward is with my God.'"
:::

Yet what is due me is in the LORD's hand, and that's the turn. The servant in Isaiah felt exactly what you've felt, that nothing was happening and he'd wasted his strength, and he didn't argue himself out of the feeling. He moved the accounting somewhere else. My reward is with my God. Not with the results, and not with the son who said "nothing." With God, who saw every one of the thirty-four Tuesdays before the one when the boy talked for forty minutes.

:::margin
Galatians 6:9 (NIV)
"Let us not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up."
:::

At the proper time. Not your time; the pecan tree's time. Mr. Pruitt's trees are knee-high and he won't see them bear, and he isn't weary, because he never believed the reward was the pecans. Don't give up, because the harvest is coming on a schedule you don't control, and some of it is coming after you're gone, and the Father is keeping the books.

## The barn nobody remembers

Roy's grandson carries a plumb bob like the one his great-grandfather used to build a barn. The barn is still standing. Nobody in the county remembers the man's name, and there's no plaque. The family that owns the land now didn't know who built it until Roy told them.

That's what legacy is: not being remembered, but something standing. The older men reading this need to hear that, because the reward of being remembered comes for good men late, and it's a small reward dressed up as a large one. The town will forget you. The church will get a new pastor and the young families won't know what you built. Your grandchildren will move to Dallas. If you were building for the remembering, you'll be disappointed. If you were building for the standing, you'll have a barn.

Roy told me the hardest thing about the years after the badge was realizing that thirty-one years of work had already been forgotten by the people he'd done it for. Then he said, "And then I realized that was fine. I didn't do it for them. I thought I did. I didn't. I did it because it was mine to do and God was watching." That's a man who found the fourth mark at seventy. His Thursday table won't be remembered either. The two men at it will stand.

## Building unseen at every age

Men pour slabs in the dark at every age, and the temptation to resent the dark changes shape as they go.

A young man of twenty is building a self, mostly, and he's tempted to think nothing counts until somebody sees it. Eli's unwitnessed work this year was telling the truth to a customer when a lie would have cost nothing, and putting the phone in the kitchen when nobody would have known, and the takeaway for him is Luke 16:10: the man who'll one day stand in a hard room is being built in the ones nobody sees.

A young father is building a floor for a child who won't remember a single Tuesday of it, and the takeaway for him is that his kids will remember, in their bones, whether the Tuesdays were there.

A man of fifty is building a marriage that will hold at seventy, or isn't, and he's tempted toward the podium because work claps and home doesn't. The takeaway for him is the fire in 1 Corinthians 3: what's built for applause is straw, and what's built for the Father in the dark is gold.

An older man is building something that will stand after his name is forgotten, which is the barn and the plumb bob, and the takeaway for him is that legacy is not being remembered but something standing, and that Roy's Thursday table won't be remembered either while the two men at it will stand.

## Thirty years in a shop

Jesus spent thirty years unwitnessed, and Luke gives the whole stretch one sentence.

:::margin
Luke 2:51 (NIV)
"Then he went down to Nazareth with them and was obedient to them. But his mother treasured all these things in her heart."
:::

He went down to Nazareth and was obedient to them. Thirty years of that. The Son of God, planing boards in a town nobody thought anything of, obedient to a carpenter and his wife, and no one saw, and no one clapped, and it was the foundation everything else stood on. When He walked into the Jordan at thirty, the Father spoke out loud in front of witnesses: this is my Son, whom I love. One sentence of applause, and then three years of being misunderstood, doubted, and finally killed, and He never went looking for the crowd's version. The Father had seen the thirty years. That was enough.

It's enough for you too. Nobody is going to clap for your slab. The Father watched you pour it in the dark, and He isn't going to forget.

:::margin
Hebrews 6:10 (NIV)
"God is not unjust; he will not forget your work and the love you have shown him as you have helped his people and continue to help them."
:::

He will not forget your work, so build. Pour the slab and make the coffee and make the call. Nobody's watching except the only One who counts, and He's keeping every Tuesday.

## Before you turn the page

If you've been building in the dark for a long time and the silence has started to wear on you, I'd like you to hear the servant's sentence in Isaiah one more time: yet what is due me is in the LORD's hand, and my reward is with my God. Not with the results. Not with the son who says "nothing." With God, who saw every one of the thirty-four Tuesdays before the one when the boy talked. He will not forget your work. May the Father who watched His Son plane boards in Nazareth for thirty years watch you pour your slab this week, and may you run your Tuesday rhythm for seven days without telling anyone and find that He kept every one of them.
