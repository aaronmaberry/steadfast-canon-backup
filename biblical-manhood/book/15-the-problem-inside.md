# 15. The Problem Inside

My first truck grew a blister in the paint on the front fender, about as wide as my thumb. Every spring I sanded the spot down to bare metal, primed it, and painted it again. Every fall it came back bigger, and it brought two smaller ones with it.

In the third year I took the truck to a body man. He pressed his thumb into the blister, and the metal gave. A little brown water seeped out. "Paint's not going to fix that," he said. "It's coming from the inside." On the back of that panel, where my paint had never reached, rust had been eating the whole time I was making the front look nice.

This is where the book turns. So far we've been talking about what you inherited. Fathers who stayed and fathers who didn't. Blessings nobody ever spoke. Brothers you don't have. Older men you never asked. All of that shaped you. But if the book ended there, I'd have handed you a can of primer and sent you back out to the driveway. Something in you won't give way to a better theory about your father.

The trouble is you. It's me too. It's every man. Scripture has a plain word for it, and you can keep painting over the blister for the rest of your life if you won't say it.

## Even with a perfect father

Picture the good version. Your father came home every night. He was kind, present, and strong. He told you he loved you, and he blessed you when you stepped into manhood. You had brothers around you, and a man ahead of you on top of that.

Would that make you a good man? A better one, probably. But good? Would the lies you tell your wife be gone? The look you had no business taking? The private ranking that puts you above the man one pew over?

The first man answers that question. Nothing around Adam was damaged. His Father, his home, his work, and his wife were all whole, and no wound had ever touched him. He stood by while it happened, and then he ate. He hid. He blamed. The trouble inside a man didn't start with his father. It came down through his father, all the way from the garden. The most faithful upbringing in the world wouldn't have kept it out of you. Jeremiah wrote that the heart is "deceitful above all things and beyond cure." Jesus said the same thing to religious men who kept the outside of their lives spotless.

:::margin
Mark 7:23 (NIV)
"All these evils come from inside and defile a person."
:::

From inside, He says. Your father, your culture, and your wound opened doors. They didn't create what came through them.

That sounds hard, and it is. It's also the only way out. If you blame all of it on what was done to you, you're stuck, because you can't un-have a father. File the whole problem under inheritance and you've filed yourself a permanent excuse. You can't repent of something another man did. You'll spend your years making Adam's excuse with a new name in it. The father You gave me did this. But the moment you own the trouble, God has something to work with, because He deals in what's actually yours. What He offers is to rebuild you.

:::margin
Romans 5:8 (NIV)
"But God demonstrates his own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us."
:::

While we were still sinners. That's when Christ died, before any of us had sorted our wounds into a tidier story about absent fathers. He named the trouble, and He died for it. Name it with Him. The cure comes attached to the name.

## The word

The word is sin, and men dodge it two ways.

Some men call it too harsh and reach for softer words. Struggle. Issue. Baggage. A struggle is something that happens to you. A sin is something you do. You manage a struggle. You repent of a sin.

Other men remember churches that swung the word at boys like a club. Those churches never owned the word. God does, and when He says it, it's a diagnosis. The body man wasn't swinging at me. He was the first one to tell me the truth.

Sin bends a man in on himself. When the moment calls for him to stand up, he hides. When it calls for him to own something, he blames. When it calls for him to give, he takes, and when it calls for him to speak, he goes quiet. Adam did it in the garden. You did it last night. Before Cain ever raised a hand against his brother, God warned him. Sin was crouching at his door, and it wanted him, and he had to rule over it. God described sin as an animal and gave Cain a command about it. The command means you're answerable, and it also means you're not stranded. Cain refused. The animal took him, and his brother died in a field. The same thing crouches at your door. You're not exempt, and neither am I.

## What to do with it

Paul wrote a sentence that covers every man in the room. The good he wants to do, he doesn't do, and the thing he hates is the thing he keeps doing. You aim for patience with your kids, and you miss. You aim to be present with your wife, and your hand goes to the phone. Then Paul asks the question that actually leads somewhere. Who will rescue me? He's asking for a person, and a system can't answer a question that starts with who. He answers it in the next breath. "Thanks be to God, who delivers me through Jesus Christ our Lord!"

In my experience, every program ends up as "try harder," and trying harder is one more coat of paint. What I've seen hold up is smaller than a program.

Say the word about yourself. Say "I sinned," out loud, to God, and be specific. The lie I told her about the money. David said that when he kept silent, his bones wasted away. Then he confessed, and God forgave him. One honest sentence stood between those two conditions.

Keep guilt and shame apart, or shame will steal the confession. Guilt says, I did a wrong thing. Shame says, I am a wrong thing. Guilt is like that blister. It's ugly, and it's telling you the truth. Once you've said the true thing, guilt can sit down. Shame keeps lying. It drives a man back to anger, or the itch, or the drift, because those exits were built to numb the feeling shame keeps feeding.

Then turn. A man can feel sorry for thirty years with his feet still pointed the same way. Repentance is when the feet turn. Confess it. Repair what can be repaired. Make the exit harder to use tomorrow. Go back to your work, and turn again tomorrow. That's what the Christian life looks like from the inside. A man keeps coming back to the Father he keeps leaving, and the Father keeps taking him in.

And let a brother hear it. Sin lives in the dark. When you say it out loud to a man who loves you and stays in the room, the dark goes out of it.

:::margin
James 5:16 (NIV)
"Therefore confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed. The prayer of a righteous person is powerful and effective."
:::

Wade said it about himself to another man for the first time during his twenty minutes, when the question came around to what was heavy. He started to say work, and then he stopped. It got quiet. The night before, he had scared his daughter. He never touched her. He didn't have to. His voice rose, and she got small, the way he used to get small. For years he'd called it his temper. That night he called it sin. The other man waited. He thanked Wade for telling him, asked what came next, and prayed. Wade had expected to feel exposed. What he felt was clean, for the first time he could remember in years. James says healed. That's more than being declared forgiven.

Then ask God for a steadfast spirit. David's worst sin was behind him, and he didn't pray for better circumstances. He prayed for a new inside.

:::margin
Psalm 51:10 (NIV)
"Create in me a pure heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me."
:::

Another coat of paint won't stop rust that's coming through from behind the steel. The metal has to be made new. God does that, and He keeps doing it. The man who has stopped decorating the front is the man who can pray this prayer.

## The same rust in every season

If you learn this while you're single, you'll walk into marriage knowing what a lot of husbands only find out after the damage is done. A new baby will introduce you to parts of yourself that the year won't let you hide. Exhaustion peels the paint. What's underneath was always there, and now you can see it. If you're divorced, you may be braced for me to say the whole ending was yours. I won't. God knows all of it, and so do the two of you. But your part is real. Healing starts when you let the sentence "I did this" end at the period, without her part glued on behind it. In the middle years, competence can run the outside of a life for decades, and competence is a different thing from righteousness. In old age a man can run that outside so long he forgets there's an inside. Saying the prayer of confession along with everyone else on Sunday isn't the same as naming your own sin, out loud, alone in the truck. New metal is offered at every age.

## The right repair

The body man cut out the bad panel and welded in new steel. The blister never came back. Two things hit me in that shop at the same time. The first was how foolish I'd been, three springs and good money spent on the wrong repair. The second was relief, because somebody had finally told me the truth, and the truth came with a fix.

Laying down the case against your father and saying the word about yourself feels the same way. There's a short stretch of feeling foolish, and then the relief goes deep. The trouble inside you has a name. The cure comes with the name, and the One who knew everything inside you has already paid for it.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. All four marks start the day you set down the paint can and say the word.

## Before you turn the page

I've asked you to turn the hardest word in the language on your own chest. Under it is good news. God works on the inside, and the price is already paid. May the Father who met David with forgiveness the moment the words were out of his mouth meet you in that same moment. And on the far side of feeling foolish, may you find the relief of a man who is finally fixing what was actually wrong.
