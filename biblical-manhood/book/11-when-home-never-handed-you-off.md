# 11. When Home Never Handed You Off

A creek can cross a county line without anything on the bank to mark the spot. There's no sign, and nobody standing there to say this is where it happened. That's how most men became men. One day they woke up holding a mortgage, or a badge, or a baby, and part of them was still wondering whether anybody had ever counted them in.

Nate is one of them. He has kids in high school and two vans with his name on the doors. On Saturday mornings he's still at his father's place, handing the old man a wrench. When a disposal dies or a gutter pulls loose, Nate does the work while his father stands close enough to narrate. Good has never been his father's word. His word is "should've used a longer screw." Every time Nate finishes a job at his own house, he texts his father a picture of it. A correction comes back every time. He sends the next picture anyway.

Nate's older brother left the same kitchen by the other road. The summer after high school he walked out in the middle of a fight, and he has spent every year since trying to prove something to a man he barely speaks to. One son slammed the door on his way out. The other one never really left. Neither of them was ever handed off. Ask either one about the morning he became a man, and he won't be able to find it.

## What a handoff is

For most of history, manhood was given. Men took a boy, put him through something hard, told him what a man is, laid a man's weight on him, and brought him into their company. He woke up a man because they said so and meant it.

Watch David near the end of his life, handing a kingdom to Solomon.

:::margin
1 Kings 2:2-3 (NIV)
"'I am about to go the way of all the earth,' he said. 'So be strong, act like a man, and observe what the LORD your God requires: Walk in obedience to him, and keep his decrees and commands, his laws and regulations, as written in the Law of Moses. Do this so that you may prosper in all you do and wherever you go'"
:::

Look at what's there. An older man who knows he's about to die. A word with something in it. He tells Solomon to act like a man, and then he tells him what that means. And a weight, a whole kingdom, with God's law at the center of it. Paul did the same work with Timothy, and he used his hands.

:::margin
2 Timothy 1:6 (NIV)
"For this reason I remind you to fan into flame the gift of God, which is in you through the laying on of my hands."
:::

Paul put his hands on the younger man, and the meaning was plain. You belong with us. Here's your work. Years later, when Timothy was losing his nerve, Paul sent him back to that hour. Remember the handoff. Fan it into flame again.

That pattern has four pieces. A man. A word. A weight. A welcome. Most of you got none of them. Some of you got the weight without the word. Your father stacked duties on you before you were old enough to shave, and he never once told you they were a man's duties. Some of you got the word without the weight. Your dad said, "You're the man of the house now," on his way out the door.

## The counterfeits

Missing the handoff doesn't kill the want. A boy goes looking, and there are substitutes everywhere. I've tried a couple of them myself.

Sex is the first counterfeit. There's no older man in it, no word, and no weight, just a girl who never agreed to make anybody a man. A paycheck is the second. You can pay a boy for years, and he's still a boy. The third is the slam. The fight, the drunk night, the birthday that was supposed to change everything, walking out in the middle of an argument. It's a boy trying to hand himself across the line, and he can't do it. Men give a handoff. A boy receives it.

The quiet fourth is the orbit. A man can spend his adult years circling the house he grew up in, waiting for a voice inside it to tell him he can stop circling. Since he isn't sure he's a man, he won't pick up a man's weight either. He's still waiting for somebody to tell him it's his. Nearly everything else this book asks of you rests on that telling.

## Jacob's night

Genesis tells the story of a man who spent half his life trying to steal what should have been given to him. Jacob wanted his father's blessing so badly that he put on his brother's clothes, lied to his blind father, and walked out with the words. The theft didn't finish the job. Twenty years of running and scheming came after it. All the stolen blessing made of him was a fugitive.

Then came a night alone on the road home. A Man wrestled with him until daybreak, and Jacob finally did what he had never done in all those years. He stopped scheming and held on.

:::margin
Genesis 32:26 (NIV)
"Then the man said, 'Let me go, for it is daybreak.' But Jacob replied, 'I will not let you go unless you bless me.'"
:::

He asked instead of stealing. Twenty years earlier, when his blind father asked who he was, Jacob gave his brother's name. Now the Man asked his name, and this time he told the truth. Jacob. It meant heel-grabber. God gave him a new name and blessed him there, as himself. He walked away limping, and he never walked like a boy again. In the morning he went to meet his brother as a man. Half his life was already gone. If it's dark where you're standing, it isn't too late.

## How a man receives it now

Hear the Father first. He tells you who you are before He tells you what to do, and if you belong to Christ, He has already said it. This is my son, whom I love. Your Father gave you the handoff your dad held back. Any other starting place will give way under you.

Then ask an older man to say it out loud. It will feel awkward. Ask anyway. Find a man older than you who is living what this book describes, and tell him the truth. "Nobody ever told me I was a man. Would you?" Let him put his hand on your shoulder while he says it. More older men than you'd guess have waited their whole lives for someone to ask them something real.

Let brothers take you in. You need a few men who know you and count you as one of theirs. If nobody ever did, find a circle that will.

Pick up a man's weight before you feel ready. David knew his son was young and inexperienced. He said as much, and then he gave the charge. Act like a man. Not long after, Solomon told God he felt like a little child who didn't know how to do the job, and by then he was already king. The acting was part of the becoming. Carry the weight for a year, and the men around you will start treating you like a man. The feeling catches up to a life that's already moving.

Write down the date. It might be the day you decided, or the day an older man said the words, or the night you asked God in the dark. Jacob got a limp. A date written inside your Bible will do. Without one, you'll half wonder for the rest of your life whether you ever crossed over.

## When the man in the chair won't say it

Some fathers will never say it, and it isn't because you haven't earned it. Some of them never received a handoff themselves. For others, keeping you a boy is the only way they know to stay a man in their own eyes. You can honor a father like that and end the orbit in the same week. The next chapter, "Leave Without Slamming the Door," takes up honor. Ending the orbit means your visits stop being collection runs for a sentence he isn't going to say. You go because he's your father. Leaving that way doesn't break anything on the way out. You quit standing in the old kitchen, waiting for a word that won't come. You take the word from the Father, and from men who are willing to say it.

Nate's came from an older man at church who had asked him to help put in a water heater. Afterward they stood at the utility sink washing up, and the man said, "I've watched you a long time. You're a good man. I'd trust you with anything I own." Nate didn't have an answer. He dried his hands twice. A handoff had happened, and his father's chair had nothing to do with it. A man willing to stand in for that chair had done it. God still puts men like that near the sons who need them.

## Handed off in every season

The young man is the one this was made for, and he's the least likely to get it. It can come to him in pieces, from any man who pays attention, so he should go find men who pay attention. A young father is sure he can't hand a boy across, because nobody handed him. He can. Giving it heals a place in him that receiving never reached. A divorce can feel like the title got taken away. The wedding and the role came apart, and he isn't sure what's left. A courtroom didn't cancel what the Father said. What you're carrying now is still a man's weight, so let an older man tell you so. And if you're the older man, you're the other half of the repair. You've seen it in a younger man and never told him. Telling him is the job. Most of the handoffs in Scripture didn't need a stage. They needed a man who spoke, put a hand on a shoulder, and sent the younger man on. A utility sink is enough this week.

## Saturday, a year later

Nate still spends Saturdays at his father's. The old man still explains the work. But the explaining lands somewhere else now, because the sentence isn't why Nate drives over anymore. His father is. There's a date written inside Nate's Bible. The week after he wrote it, he called his brother. They had never talked about their father without the old fight coming into the room with them. This time it stayed out.

You're allowed in the company of men. The Father gave you that permission before anyone else could. Find a man who will say your name out loud as a man's name. Then be a man who does the same for someone else.

## Before you turn the page

If no voice has ever told you that you're a man, let mine be one of them. The Father who spoke over His Son at the Jordan has spoken that same kind of word over you. May God give you the awkward courage it takes to ask, and the plain courage it takes to tell.
