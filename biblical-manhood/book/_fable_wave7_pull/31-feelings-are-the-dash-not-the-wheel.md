# 31. Feelings Are the Dash, Not the Wheel

The summer I was old enough to be useful, Vernon put me on his tractor and rode the fender. Before he'd let me drive he made me read him the gauges: oil, water, amps. "Those tell you the truth about the tractor," he said, "and you look at them every row." Then he pointed at the furrow from the last pass. "That tells you where to go. Keep your right tire in it." I watched the gauges instead of the furrow and put a bend in his field. He didn't say a word. He tapped the water gauge with his knuckle and pointed at the furrow again.

Later the tractor ran hot on a long pull and I kept going, because the furrow was straight. Vernon heard the engine change. "You read that gauge?" I had. "Then why'd you keep going?" I had no answer. A gauge tells you something true about the machine. It doesn't tell you where the field ends. Ignore it and you cook an engine. Steer by it and you wander all over the field. One eye on the dash, both hands on the wheel, and know which is which.

That's feelings, and it's this whole chapter in a sentence. Your feelings are the dash and not the wheel. They're real, they're telling you something, and you should read them. They don't get to steer.

## Two ways men wreck

Most men get this wrong one of two ways.

The first man ignores the dash. He doesn't do feelings. He isn't angry; he's fine. He isn't sad; he's tired. He isn't lonely; he's busy. It's a man driving with tape over his gauges. The feelings go somewhere else: out as anger, down as appetite, away as absence. The man who says he has no feelings has feelings running his life from under the hood.

The second man steers by the dash. "I don't feel in love anymore." "I don't feel like God's here." He reads a gauge, which is fine, and turns the wheel toward it, which is a ditch. He leaves marriages because a feeling changed and churches because the feeling left, and ends up nowhere, and can't understand why, because he was so honest about his feelings.

The Bible's way is neither. It's a man who reads every gauge honestly, says out loud what it says, brings it to God and to a brother, and then steers by something that doesn't move.

## What the dash is telling you

Most men were never taught the names of the gauges.

Fear says something matters to you and you aren't sure you can protect it; ask what you're afraid of losing. Anger says something is wrong, or you think it is; ask what's under it, because it's almost always fear or shame wearing a hard face. Sadness says something is lost; grieve the thing, because men skip this gauge more than any other, and the skipping turns sadness into the drift. Loneliness says not good alone, and it's the most accurate gauge on the dash, because God said it first; read it as "go find a brother," not "something's wrong with me." Numbness says you've run hot so long the gauge burned out; that isn't peace, it's a warning light that quit working. Shame says "I am a wrong thing"; check it against the plumb line, because it lies more than any other gauge. Guilt is a working gauge and shame is a broken one. And joy says you're where you were made to be; notice what you were doing when it came.

The Psalms are a book of men reading their gauges out loud to God. David, as hard a man as ever lived, said things to God most men wouldn't say to their wives.

:::margin
Psalm 42:5 (NIV)
"Why, my soul, are you downcast? Why so disturbed within me? Put your hope in God, for I will yet praise him, my Savior and my God."
:::

Why are you downcast: reading the gauge. Put your hope in God: turning the wheel, not toward the feeling but toward God. That's the whole method, and the Psalms are the manual.

## What the dash is not telling you

A feeling is not the truth about God. "I don't feel forgiven" is a reading, not a verdict; forgiveness is a legal fact established at a cross.

A feeling is not the truth about your marriage. Every long marriage has had stretches when the love gauge read empty. The men who steered by it are divorced. The men who kept the covenant with both hands woke up, often years later, with the needle back up and a marriage the feeling could never have built.

A feeling is not a command. "I feel like God wants me to" is the most dangerous sentence in a Christian man's mouth, because it hands a gauge God's authority. Does the Word say it? Do your brothers see it? Does it look like the four marks, or like an exit?

:::margin
Proverbs 28:26 (NIV)
"Those who trust in themselves are fools, but those who walk in wisdom are kept safe."
:::

And a feeling is not permanent. Every gauge moves with sleep and food and weather, and a man who makes a permanent decision on a late-night reading has handed his life to the weather. Read it again after you've slept, and again with a brother in the room.

## What the wheel is

So what does a man steer by? Four things. The Word, which doesn't move; when your feelings say one thing and the Bible says another, the Bible is describing reality and your feelings are describing you. The covenant, what you promised before God and witnesses; promises exist so they'll hold when the feeling doesn't. Your brothers, who can read your dash from the outside when you can't. And the four marks. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. When you don't know what to do, do whichever of those is in front of you.

Back on the tractor: the water gauge climbs on a long pull. You don't ignore it and cook the engine. You don't steer toward it; there's nowhere to steer toward a gauge. You read it, name it, "running hot," pull out of the row, let it cool, check the water, and get back in the furrow you were already cutting. The gauge changed how you drove. It didn't change where.

## Elijah under the broom bush

Now the heavy end of the dash, because men die of it.

Some of you aren't only sad. You're somewhere darker, and you've been there a while. Elijah had just seen fire fall from heaven, and a day later he was alone in the desert asking to die.

:::margin
1 Kings 19:4 (NIV)
"while he himself went a day's journey into the desert. He came to a broom bush, sat down under it and prayed that he might die. 'I have had enough, LORD,' he said. 'Take my life; I am no better than my ancestors.'"
:::

That's a prophet of God reading his dash out loud, and the dash said "I want to die." God didn't rebuke him or tell him to have more faith. He sent an angel with bread and water and let him sleep. He fed him again. Then He spoke, and gave him a job and a companion. Food, sleep, presence, a word, work, a friend.

So read the gauge and say it out loud, to God and then to a man. If it says "I've had enough," that sentence needs to leave your mouth in front of another human being this week. Take care of the body; God's first two moves with a suicidal prophet were a meal and a nap.

:::margin
Psalm 103:14 (NIV)
"for he knows how we are formed, he remembers that we are dust."
:::

Then get help. Getting help is not weakness. A doctor is not a failure of faith, and a counselor is one of the ways God sends bread and water. If your gauges have read dark for weeks, if the numbness has settled in, if you've thought about ending it, see a doctor and tell him the truth, and if he says medication, take it the way you'd take insulin. That isn't steering by your feelings. It's repairing a broken gauge so you can read the dash again.

If you're thinking about ending it tonight, call a man now, your brother or your pastor, and if there's no one, call 988 and talk to someone whose job is to sit with you in the dark. Elijah asked God to kill him and God fed him breakfast. There's no shame in the broom bush, only the question of whether you'll let somebody find you there. Then the wheel. Elijah went back to work with a companion. The feeling didn't leave first; he moved first, in small ways, with help, and the feeling followed: a meal, a nap, a brother, and the next small assigned thing, until the gauge comes back into range.

## The dash in every season

With a baby in the house every gauge is pegged and none are accurate, so make no decisions about your marriage at three in the morning; say to each other, "This is the sleep talking. We'll talk after a nap." The divorced seat's dash will do things for a year or two it has never done: read every one to God and to your man, and don't turn the wheel for a year. No moving, marrying, quitting, or leaving the church. You don't make turns with broken gauges; you drive straight and slow until they settle. The older man raised to tape over the dash can still learn to read it, and he has gauges the younger men don't: grief as friends die, the fear of decline, the widower's loneliness, which isn't weakness; God made you for company and you've lost some, so go find the rest. And the single man should read the loneliness gauge right. It's saying what God said in the garden: not good alone. Steer toward brothers, not toward whatever promises to quiet the needle fastest.

## The Man who read every gauge

Jesus felt everything. He wept at a grave, He was overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death, and Hebrews says He prayed with loud cries and tears. And He never once let a gauge turn the wheel. He read the gauge that said "let this cup pass," and then said, not as I will, but as You will, and steered straight into the thing the gauge was begging Him to avoid.

Not a man with no feelings, and not a man run by them, but a Man who felt all of it, said all of it, and kept both hands on a wheel fixed to something that didn't move.

Read your dash and tell the truth about it to God and a brother. Then put your hands back on the wheel and stay in the furrow you were already cutting.

## Before you turn the page

If any gauge has read dark for a long time, hear the plainest thing I know: tell one man today. Not this week; today. Otherwise, read your dash tomorrow morning in one honest sentence to God, then put your hands back on the wheel. May the Father who knows how we're formed and remembers we are dust give you rest for your body and truth for your gauges, and keep you in the row.
