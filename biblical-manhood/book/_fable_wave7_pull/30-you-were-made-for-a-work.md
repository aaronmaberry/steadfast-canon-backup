# 30. You Were Made for a Work

Dale runs the county's road grader. He's been on the blade as long as his youngest has been alive, and after every hard rain he's out before daylight pulling the washboard out of forty miles of gravel. Nobody in the county thinks about him. Every one of them backs out onto a road he smoothed last week, and the school bus and the ambulance ride on it, and not one of them knows his name.

He asked me after church one Sunday whether his work counted. He meant for God. Years of sermons about calling had left him feeling that his real life was the few hours a week at church, and the fifty on the grader paid for it.

"I'm just running a blade," he said.

I told him a whole county gets to school and to the hospital because he runs it, and that God noticed before I did. But most men are asking his question. Does my work count? Is it part of the thing, or the thing I do so I can get to the thing?

Most of a man's road is work. If your work doesn't count, most of your life doesn't count, and I don't believe that, and neither does Scripture.

## Work came before the curse

Genesis puts a man in a garden to work it and take care of it before the fall, before sweat was a word in a curse. The curse didn't invent work; it made work hard. A man who calls his job a necessary evil has read the third chapter of Genesis and skipped the second.

Go higher. God works. When the religious men accused Jesus of working on the wrong day, He said so plainly.

:::margin
John 5:17 (NIV)
"In his defense Jesus said to them, 'My Father is always at his work to this very day, and I too am working.'"
:::

The God you serve isn't a God who sits, and He made you in His image, which means He made you to work. That's why a man laid off or retired or disabled goes sideways in ways that aren't about money. The image in him is straining against a life with no garden.

And Jesus worked, and not only for the three years. Most of His life He spent in a shop, making things for people who paid Him. So here's the plain answer for the man on the grader: yes, it counts. It isn't the thing you do so you can get to the thing. It is the thing, or most of it.

## What work is for

Work is for provision. Paul was blunt: the one unwilling to work shall not eat. Unwilling, not unable; there's grace for the man who can't and a rule for the man who won't.

Work is for service. Dale's work is a road the bus can run on. Your job is how you love your neighbor forty hours a week, and the quality of the work is the quality of the love. Paul told thieves to work, and look at the reason.

:::margin
Ephesians 4:28 (NIV)
"Anyone who has been stealing must steal no longer, but must work, doing something useful with their own hands, that they may have something to share with those in need."
:::

Work turns a taker into a giver.

Work is for making the world more than it was. You're doing in small what God did in six days, and that's why a finished job feels the way it does. It's the image showing, and it forms you while you do it.

And work is for worship. Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as for the Lord. Dale is working for the Lord on the blade whether anybody knows it or not. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. The last of those turns a time card into worship.

Now what work isn't. It isn't your identity; the man who is his job dies when the job does, and your name is son, brother, husband, father, keeper of a garden, and the job is one of the gardens. It isn't an escape; work claps and home mostly doesn't, and if you're staying late because the shop is easier than the kitchen, you've turned a good thing into an exit. And it isn't your salvation; you don't earn your standing with God by output. You have it, in Christ, and you work because you're His, not to become His.

## When you hate the job

Some of you are reading this at a job you can't stand, and "work is worship" sounds like a slap. The thorns are real: bosses who are fools, customers who lie, systems that grind. But Solomon, who had every job and every luxury, landed here.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 2:24 (NIV)
"A person can do nothing better than to eat and drink and find satisfaction in their own toil. This too, I see, is from the hand of God,"
:::

Satisfaction in work is a gift, and it comes from three places. From excellence: a man who does even a job he hates with skill has something the job can't take from him. From people: the apprentice watching how you handle a bad day, the coworker whose marriage is coming apart; you may hate the work and love the people, and the people may be why you're there. And from the audience: work for the boss and the job is as good as the boss. Work for the Lord and the job is as good as the Lord.

If you should leave, leave like a man: not on a feeling, but with a plan, your wife in the room, the next thing lined up or the savings to bridge it, and your reasons checked by a brother. Men leave jobs badly in two directions. They quit in a huff, or they stay in a coffin.

## When you lose the job

You get called in on a Thursday, and by Friday you're a man with no garden. The first thing that hits isn't money. It's who you are, because you've introduced yourself by your job for years, and now the sentence has nothing after "I'm."

Here's what a man does. Tell your wife the same day, not after you've figured out a plan; men hide layoffs for weeks, and what she learns when she finds out is that you hid. Tell your brother that week; "what's heavy" has an answer now. Treat the search as your work: up at the same time, shaved, at the table by eight, finding work until five. Take the bridge job; nothing that feeds your house is beneath you. And kill the shame. Guilt says you did a wrong thing; shame says you are a wrong thing. Losing a job is neither. It's a thorn, and your Father saw it coming.

## When your work is the house

Some of you have work that doesn't look like work to anybody, including you. You're home with the children because of how the math worked out. You bathe your wife every morning because she can't, which is the second verb in the garden, keeping what you were given, and it's the hardest work in this book. If your garden is a house and a body that needs you, work it and keep it. The Father who sees in secret doesn't grade on visibility.

## After the last day

You met Alton at the creek, and you know about the morning after his retirement dinner. Scripture has no retirement. What it has is a shift.

:::margin
Numbers 8:25-26 (NIV)
"but at the age of fifty, they must retire from their regular service and work no longer. They may assist their fellow Levites in performing their duties at the tent of meeting, but they themselves must not do the work. This, then, is how you are to assign the responsibilities of the Levites."
:::

Retire from the regular service, and then: assist. The Levites who did the heavy lifting stopped lifting, but they didn't stop; they moved to helping the ones who carried. That's the biblical shape of retirement. Not the recliner; a change of garden.

Here's the rhythm that pulled Alton out; any man past his last day can build it. The alarm stayed where it was, because a man who sleeps until he feels like it is halfway to the drift. He made the coffee and sat with his wife while she drank it: knowing her, after a marriage's worth of mornings spent elsewhere. He read a chapter out loud on the porch, because he needed the plumb line that doesn't move. He gave himself a job every day that somebody was counting on: a footing, a widow's gutters, the church van. "A man with no job by noon," he said, "is a man in a chair by two." He took on two younger men who needed a man ahead of them. He gave his grandson hills.

If your body took your work before your years did, the word is still assist; I know a man who can't leave his wife's side for more than an hour, and he spends the hour calling widowers to ask what's heavy. The danger for the older man is the drift, which usually wears a fishing hat, and its cousin, the anger that comes in through the television. Don't stop. Shift. Carry less, assist more, and declare what you know to the next generation until you're done.

## Work in the other seasons

If you're single, work is your main garden; work it like a man who intends to hand it to a wife someday, and don't marry the job, because the men who do have a hard time un-marrying it. If there's a baby in the house, the extra hour at work because it's quieter than the kitchen will tempt you, so name it; provision is holy and disappearing isn't. The divorced seat carries the tightest money in this book, two households and lawyers, and you may have to work more; do it without vanishing from the children, because the call from the cab on the way home costs nothing, and in a world that shook, a father who goes to work every day and comes home every night is ground they can stand on.

## He finished the work

Jesus said it the night before He died.

:::margin
John 17:4 (NIV)
"I have brought you glory on earth by finishing the work you gave me to do."
:::

That's the sentence every man wants to say at the end. The work You gave me, I did, and I finished it. The shop years and the preaching years and the cross, all one work, all finished.

You were made for a work. A grader blade, a classroom, a crew, a body that needs bathing. It all counts, and the way you do it is most of what your life will have been.

Dale is still on the blade. Last spring he told me he'd started praying for every house he passes as the crown comes up. A county drives his road in the morning, none of them knowing a man they've never met asked God to keep them while he kept the road.

That counts, and so does yours. Go do your work.

## Before you turn the page

If you've spent years thinking your work didn't count for God, drive to it tomorrow with a different picture: the Son of God at a workbench for most of His life. If the work was taken from you, by a layoff or a body that quit or a birthday, you weren't let go. You were reassigned. May the Father who is always at His work give you a job by noon tomorrow that somebody is counting on.
