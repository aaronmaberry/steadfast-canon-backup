# 29. Give Your Son a Day He Will Not Forget

The creek was ankle deep and cold enough to take your breath, and five of us waded into it at first light and each came out with a stone.

The boy watched from the gravel bar, at the age when the voice drops and a boy starts watching men. He knew something was coming; he'd been told to be dressed by five and not why. We'd walked in from the road in the dark to the bar below the bridge where he'd been baptized two summers before. His grandfather Alton went in first, then his father, then an uncle, then the man his father meets with every week for twenty minutes, then me. Nobody spoke. We set the stones at the boy's feet, a stack you could see from the bridge.

Alton read from Joshua, where twelve men carry twelve stones out of the Jordan so the children will ask. Each man spoke. The uncle told the boy the worst thing he'd ever done and what a man does afterward. His father put a hand on his shoulder and said the three names he's been saying over him for years, and then the fourth: "You're becoming a man. That's what today is. Here's what that means." And he said the sentence. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. He handed the boy a card with it in his own hand.

Then Alton took a brass plumb bob out of his coat, heavy for its size, the boy's name and the date cut into it, put it in the boy's hand, and told him what it was for.

The boy carries it every day; his father has seen him take it out in a parking lot and just hold it. The stones are still on the bank. He walks down there sometimes.

Most men never got handed off, and spent their lives waiting for a sentence that never came. Don't let your son wait. Give him a day. Not a party but a marker: men, a word, a weight, a welcome. A day he can point to for the rest of his life and say, that's when.

## Why a day

God builds markers into His people. When Israel crossed the Jordan, Joshua had twelve stones carried out of the riverbed and stacked on the bank, and he said why.

:::margin
Joshua 4:6-7 (NIV)
"to serve as a sign among you. In the future, when your children ask you, 'What do these stones mean?' tell them that the flow of the Jordan was cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD. When it crossed the Jordan, the waters of the Jordan were cut off. These stones are to be a memorial to the people of Israel forever."
:::

God wanted rocks on a riverbank so that a boy in another generation would ask his father a question and get a story. Every Passover a father answered a child's question about why that night was different. Jesus stood in a river and heard His Father's voice out loud before He started His work. Markers. Stones a man can point to.

Most Christian families have none for their boys. Birthdays and graduations, and no day that marks the thing that matters most: the men around a boy saying, on purpose and out loud, you're a man now, here's what that means, and you're one of us. So he guesses. He picks a marker from whatever's lying around: the first girl, the first drunk, the first fight, the license. Counterfeits, reached for because nobody handed him the real one. Give him a stone he can point to.

## The design

Here's one shape of a day that works.

When: as he starts to cross. You'll know it by the voice, the shoulders, the way he's begun to watch men. Missed the window? Do it anyway.

Who: the father and two to four men the boy respects who'll be in his life going forward. Not a crowd; a crowd makes it a performance. If you have no men, you have a year to go get them, and you know how.

Where: outside, early, somewhere with weight. A hill he's climbed, or water he was baptized in. The place should ask something of him just to get there, if only a walk in the cold and dark.

Before: he knows something's coming but not what. You meet the men once and ask each to prepare one true thing: a story of what a man is from his own life, one failure and what he did afterward, one charge for the boy. Five minutes each.

The morning: you arrive by going somewhere, in quiet, so the weight settles on him. One man reads the Word; the verse I've heard most at these mornings is this one.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 16:13 (NIV)
"Be on your guard; stand firm in the faith; be courageous; be strong."
:::

Four commands in a breath. Read it slowly. The men speak in turn and the boy stands and takes it; he'll remember every word. The father gives the four marks, the sentence and a line on each, the card in his own hand, and which mark he's weakest at himself. The father gives the name, hand on the shoulder, the three and then the fourth. If you've never said it before, you'll probably cry. Let them see it; a boy who watches his father weep while naming him learns something no ceremony can teach. Then the token, weighted rather than expensive: a knife, a Bible with the date inside, a great-grandfather's watch, a tool. Then the prayer, every man's hand on him, the father last. Then somebody says it plainly: "You're one of us now." Then breakfast, where the men talk to him like a man for the first time, and he notices.

## The plumb bob

Here's what Alton told the boy.

"This is a plumb bob. I hung it on every wall of the house your dad grew up in. You hang it next to what you're building and wait for it to stop swinging, and when it settles it points straight down. It doesn't care what you think is straight. Gravity doesn't have opinions.

"You'll build a lot, and some of it will be crooked, and you won't be able to tell, because everything around it is crooked too. Then you hang this and wait. God's Word is your plumb line. It doesn't move. Hold your life up next to it, let it settle, and see where you're off.

"And it's heavy for its size on purpose. A light bob swings forever. A heavy one settles fast. A man's supposed to be like that, steady and weighted and hard to knock off true. Your name's on it so you remember who you're supposed to be when you're not sure."

The boy has already held that bob next to a decision or two, his father says: whether to go along with something at school, whether to tell the truth about a grade. It's a piece of brass. It's also this whole book, in his pocket.

Choose your own token, and say what it means out loud when you hand it over.

## The day after

The ceremony changes nothing by itself. I've watched fathers pour everything into the morning and go back on Monday to treating their son like a boy, and the boy learns the day was a nice thing his dad said once. What makes it real is that from then on the men treat him differently. More hill. His opinion asked and weighed. A man's jobs, a man's correction, and when he fails, a man's standard and a man's grace. The day is the marker. The Tuesdays are the handoff. Give him the day without them and you've given him a stone with no story.

Alton built for the county for a working lifetime, and he says the retirement dinner was the best night of his career and the next morning the worst of his life: the dinner said "well done" and the morning said nothing. Markers are stones on a riverbank, not the land. The land is Tuesday. Alton knew it, which is why the next week he called the boy to come help pour a footing. The day said "you're a man." The week after started making him one.

## If you never had a day

Some of you are reading this with a stone in your throat because nobody did this for you, and you doubt you can give what you didn't get. You can, and giving it will heal something in you that receiving it would have. I've watched fathers who were never named stand at the water and name their sons, break down halfway, finish anyway, and drive home different men. And if you want it complete, ask one of the older men at your son's day to say something to you afterward, privately, hand on your shoulder: you're a man, and you did right by your boy. It isn't too late for you either.

## The day in every season

The day changes shape with your seat. The divorced seat can still do this and should: tell his mother what it is and why, make it plain it's about the boy and not the two of you, and if there's a good stepfather in his life, consider inviting him. If she resists, do it on your time, with your men, and keep it clean. If you're single, you may be one of the men at somebody else's day, and there may be a fatherless boy whose mother would thank God if you and three others gave him one; ask her first. If you're the grandfather, offer; fathers often want this and don't know where to start, and you have the standing, the token in a drawer, and the time to make the days after mean something. And your daughter needs a day too, in a different shape: her mother and grandmother there, a dinner more often than a dawn, and a word about who she is and what she's worth and what to expect from a man. Don't give your son a day and leave her guessing.

## What do these stones mean

Someday that boy's son will ask what the plumb bob means, or what the stack of rocks on the gravel bar is for. He'll tell him about five men in cold water at first light, a card in his father's hand, and a grandfather who hung that bob on every wall of a house. And then, if the men did their job, he'll give the boy a day.

Give your son a day he will not forget. Then give him the week after.

## Before you turn the page

If you never got a day, hear what I'd want said to me: it isn't too late, and the day you give your son will hand something back to you. May the Father who set stones in the Jordan give you the men and the morning and the nerve to say the fourth name out loud, and may your son carry something heavy in his pocket that reminds him who he's supposed to be.
