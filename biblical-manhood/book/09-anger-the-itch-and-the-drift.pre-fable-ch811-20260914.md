# 9. Anger, the Itch, and the Drift

Three men sat at the same table at a Thursday breakfast in a town I won't name. They didn't know each other well, and they would have told you they didn't have much in common.

The first one had thrown a drill across his shop the day before because a screw stripped. Not at anybody; he just threw it, and then stood there breathing hard while his apprentice pretended not to have seen. He has a wife who has started to flinch when his voice changes pitch, and he hates that about himself, and he doesn't know what to do about it, so mostly he gets angry about that too.

The second one had been up until two in the morning on his phone again, and not reading the news. He's single and twenty-six and a decent man who's in church every Sunday, and he has lied to every accountability partner he's ever had. He tells himself it doesn't hurt anybody. He knows it does, and he knows who.

The third one hadn't done anything at all, which is the point. He's fifty-two, married twenty-six years, and he can't remember the last time he felt much of anything. He works, he comes home, he watches whatever's on, he goes to bed, and his wife told him last month that she feels like she's living with a roommate, and the honest truth is that he didn't feel much about that either.

Three men, and anger, the itch, and the drift. What none of them knew, sitting there over their eggs, was that they had the same wound. They'd simply taken three different exits from it.

## Three exits from the same pain

Last chapter I said every man inherited something from the chair at the end of the table. This chapter is about where that inheritance goes, because pain doesn't sit still in a man. It moves, and it has to go somewhere, and in my experience it nearly always goes to one of three places.

It goes out, as anger. It goes down, as appetite. Or it goes away, as absence. Every man I've ever sat with has a home exit, the one he takes when things get heavy, and most of them have never noticed that it's an exit at all. They think it's their personality, and it isn't. It's the way they learned, a long time ago, to not feel something, and the way has become a habit they mistake for themselves.

I want to look at each one honestly, and I want to be careful about two things while I do. I'm not going to soften any of them, because every one of them is destroying something in your house. And I'm not going to shame you for any of them, because shame is fuel for all three. Honest and warm, both at once. That's the whole book anyway.

## Anger

Anger is the exit that looks like strength, which is exactly why men like it.

The man who threw the drill would tell you he has a temper, the way you'd say a truck has a stiff clutch. But listen to what anger actually is underneath. Nearly every time I've sat with an angry man long enough to get below the anger, I've found one of three things waiting there: fear, or shame, or helplessness. He's afraid the money isn't going to work. He's ashamed of something he did or something he is. He's watching something he loves go wrong and he can't stop it. And instead of feeling any of those, which would feel like weakness, he feels the one thing that feels like power, which is anger, and he gets mad.

I'll put it another way, and I've never found a better one. A great deal of male anger is grief with its fists up. The Bible doesn't tell you not to be angry. It tells you what to do with anger, and it's specific about it.

:::margin
Ephesians 4:26-27 (NIV)
"'In your anger do not sin': Do not let the sun go down while you are still angry, and do not give the devil a foothold."
:::

In your anger, the verse says, and notice that it assumes you'll have it. Then it gives you a deadline, which is sundown, and a warning, which is that this is where the enemy gets a foot inside the door of your house. Not through the big dramatic sins. Through the anger you carried to bed and woke up with and carried to bed again, until it was simply the weather in your home and nobody remembered a time when it wasn't.

:::margin
Proverbs 29:11 (NIV)
"Fools give full vent to their rage, but the wise bring calm in the end."
:::

Full vent is the drill across the shop and the voice that changes pitch. A fool thinks letting it all out is honesty. A wise man knows that what comes out in full vent isn't the truth at all; it's the pressure. The truth is underneath, and it's usually quieter and more frightened than the rage.

If anger is your exit, here is what's yours to do. Learn your ninety seconds, because anger in the body peaks and starts to fall within about a minute and a half if you don't feed it. Walk out to the truck. Don't say the sentence you've got loaded. Let it peak and let it pass. Then come back inside and say what's underneath it: "I'm scared about the money." "I feel like I'm failing at this." "I don't know how to fix it and that makes me crazy." Your wife can work with a scared man. She cannot work with a storm. And whatever it takes, don't let the sun go down on it. The apology, the conversation, the "I'm not okay but I'm not leaving." Before bed, every time. And know that a man who conquers this doesn't become soft. He becomes the strongest man in the room.

:::margin
Proverbs 16:32 (NIV)
"Better a patient person than a warrior, one with self-control than one who takes a city."
:::

## The itch

The second exit goes down, into appetite, and I'm going to talk mostly about the one that has the most men by the throat, which is sexual. But everything I say applies to the bottle and the plate and the shopping cart and the endless scroll and the sports book. It's all the same itch: comfort, now, without cost, and without having to be known.

The twenty-six-year-old on his phone at two in the morning isn't chasing pleasure, mostly. I've talked to enough men to know that. He's chasing relief, relief from loneliness, from failure, from a Tuesday that went badly, from a body that's tired and a heart that hasn't been seen by anyone in a week. The screen doesn't ask anything of him. It doesn't need him to be a man. That's the pull, and that's exactly why it's a lie, because being needed to be a man is the very thing that would heal him. James lays out how it works in a few lines, and I've never seen them improved on.

:::margin
James 1:14-15 (NIV)
"but each person is tempted when they are dragged away by their own evil desire and enticed. Then, after desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, gives birth to death."
:::

Dragged away is the feeling, isn't it. Nobody plans to be up at two. And the end of the road is death, not usually a dramatic one, but the slow death of a man's ability to be present, to want a real woman, to feel a real thing, to look his future wife in the eye without a hidden room in his chest.

Let me tell you what the itch is actually stealing, because most men have never done the accounting. If you're single, it's stealing from the woman you haven't met yet. Every image trains you to want something she isn't, and she's going to pay for that training on her honeymoon and for years after it. If you're married, it's stealing from your wife directly, and she knows something's off even when she doesn't know what. Jesus said that what a man looks at with intent is already adultery, and He wasn't being dramatic. He was being accurate.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 6:18-20 (NIV)
"Flee from sexual immorality. All other sins a person commits are outside the body, but whoever sins sexually, sins against their own body. Do you not know that your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit, who is in you, whom you have received from God? You are not your own; you were bought at a price. Therefore honor God with your bodies."
:::

Flee, Paul says, and he doesn't say manage or moderate. Flee, the way Joseph ran out of a house with a woman in it who wanted him, because there's no dignified way to fight this one, and there's only running. If the itch is your exit, here's what's yours. Tell one man the whole truth: not "I struggle with purity," but the actual truth of what and how often and since when and what it's costing you. The itch lives on secrecy and it begins to die the day a brother knows. Cut the supply, with filters on every device and the password held by that brother, and the phone charging in the kitchen at night instead of the bedroom. Yes, that feels like a rule for a child, and you've been acting like a child at two in the morning, so a child's rule is appropriate until you're not. Fill the hour, because the itch comes in the empty spaces, and a man who knows where his empty spaces are can put something real in them: a man to call, a walk, a book with pages, a prayer with words. And deal with the loneliness, because that's what this actually is. Chapter 13 is about brothers, and I'll tell you now what I'll tell you then. The itch shrinks in men who are known.

## The drift

The third exit is the quiet one, and it's the one I worry about most in men past forty-five, because nobody notices it and it feels like nothing's wrong.

The drift is a man leaving without moving. He's in the house and he goes to work, and he hasn't blown up and he hasn't blown it. He's simply not there. His attention lives somewhere else: the game, the news, fantasy football, a boat, the job, the phone, the garage. His wife stopped asking him things a while ago because his answers were "fine" and "whatever you think," and his kids learned early that Dad's body was in the recliner and Dad was somewhere else.

This is passivity, the thing we talked about in chapter 4, gone chronic. And what's under it is usually one of two things. Either the man got hurt somewhere along the way and decided, without ever saying so, that caring was too expensive. Or he got tired, honestly tired, and rest turned into retreat, and he never came back from it. The Bible has a picture for this that's almost funny until you see your own house in it.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 10:18 (NIV)
"Through laziness, the rafters sag; because of idle hands, the house leaks."
:::

Nobody kicked the rafters. Nobody punched a hole in the roof. The house simply leaked, slowly, because the man who should have been on the ladder was in the chair. The drift doesn't destroy a home in a night. It destroys it over fifteen years, one evening at a time, and by the time anybody names it the rafters are already bowed.

I want to speak straight to older men here, because the drift is the exit that comes for you hardest once the working years end. You've had a place to be and a reason to get up for forty years, and then it stops, and there's a version of retirement that is really just the drift with a fishing license. The television gets more of you than your wife does. You get angry about the news, which is the drift and the anger holding hands. Roy told me he watched three men from his old unit go that way in the first five years after they turned in their badges, and he'd started to go too. "I was a spectator in my own house," he said. "I'd never been a spectator in my life." What pulled him back was a single decision: he got up before his wife every morning and made the coffee and sat with her while she drank it. That's a small thing. It's also a man refusing to drift, one morning at a time, and it changed the whole shape of his retirement.

If the drift is your exit, here's what's yours. Name it to your wife: "I've been gone. I've been in the house and I've been gone, and I'm sorry." She knows already. Hearing you say it will change something. Take one thing back, not your whole life but one evening a week with the television off and your attention in the room, one question a day to your wife or your kid that you actually wait for the answer to. And find out what you stopped caring about and why, because somewhere back there a man decided it wasn't worth it, and you need to go find that man and talk to him. You might need a brother or a counselor to help you find him, and that isn't weakness; that's a man on a ladder looking at his rafters.

## The same root

I said the three men at that breakfast had the same wound. Let me show you how.

The angry man is a boy who learned that feeling scared got him hurt, so he traded it for something that felt like power. The itching man is a boy who learned he wouldn't be seen, so he found a place that would give him something like comfort without requiring him to be known. The drifting man is a boy who learned that caring cost too much, so he stopped.

Every exit is a way to not feel the empty chair. Every exit works, for a while, which is why men keep taking them. And every exit leads out of the house.

I'm not going to tell you to simply stop, because you've tried that. The men who actually get free of these things get free the same way, and it isn't by gritting their teeth harder. It's by taking the pain somewhere it can be healed instead of somewhere it can be numbed. That's the next chapter. There is a Father who does not leave, and He isn't afraid of your anger, and He isn't disgusted by your itch, and He hasn't drifted from you while you drifted from Him.

He knows the boy under the exit. He's been waiting for the man to bring him home.

## The exits in different houses

Let me turn toward a few of you, because the exits show up differently in different seasons and you may not recognize yours.

If you're young and single, the itch is probably your loudest exit, and I've said what I have to say about it. But watch the drift too. A man can drift at twenty-six, into games and screens and a job he doesn't care about and a church he attends without ever being known in, and I'd rather you notice it now than at fifty.

If there's a baby in your house, anger is going to surprise you. You didn't think you were an angry man, and then it's three in the morning and the baby won't stop and your wife says something and you hear a voice come out of you that you last heard from your father. That's exhaustion opening a door your father built. Take your ninety seconds. Go out to the truck. Then come back and say the true thing: "I'm so tired I'm scared of myself." That sentence will save your marriage more than once this year.

If you're divorced, you may be taking all three exits at once in the first year or two, and I understand why: anger at her, the itch because you're alone and you weren't, the drift because caring is what got you here. Kenny said the first year after his divorce he was "a mad, lonely ghost." What brought him back was one older man at his church who called him every Tuesday night, asked him the same three questions, and wouldn't let him hang up until he'd answered them. You'll meet that method in chapter 13, and it works.

And if you're older, watch the drift and the anger together, because those two like to travel as a pair in a man past sixty, and they usually arrive by way of a television. The remedy is the same as it was at thirty: get up, make the coffee, sit with her, and ask a question and wait for the answer.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

At nineteen the itch is the loudest exit, and I've said what I have to say about it, but the drift is already starting for a good many young men in ways they don't recognize: a job they don't care about, a screen that eats their evenings, a church they attend without being known in. Eli, the young man at the parts counter, told me he'd noticed that he hadn't felt strongly about anything in a year, and that this had frightened him more than any temptation. The takeaway for him is that numbness at nineteen isn't peace; it's a warning light, and the fix is a brother and a real thing to care about.

At forty-five the drift and the itch travel together. A man's marriage has gone quiet, the work is the only place that claps, and a woman at the office laughs at his jokes. Walt was three years into that pattern before his wife said her sentence in the kitchen. The takeaway for him is that the affair he's certain he'd never have starts with the drift he's certain isn't happening.

## The Man who took none of them

Jesus got angry, and He turned over tables in the temple, and He looked at hard-hearted men with something the Gospels are willing to call anger, and He never once sinned in it. He knew what it was for and He aimed it and He put it down.

Jesus was tempted, in every way we are, and Hebrews says it flatly: He did not sin. Every itch a man has ever felt, He felt the pull of, alone, in a desert, for forty days, and He did not go down.

Jesus never drifted, not once. When the time came to go to Jerusalem He set His face and went. When His men fell asleep, He stayed awake. When everything in Him wanted another way, He said "not as I will," and He stayed where He was.

He took none of the exits, and He did it so that when you take one, there would be a Man who could find you in the ditch and bring you back, without shame, and show you the way He walked instead.

You have a home exit. Now you know its name. Next chapter, we go to the Father.

## Before you turn the page

You know your exit now, and if you're like most men you feel a little exposed by knowing it. I'd like you to hear that the exposure is a mercy. The exit was running your house from the dark, and now it has a name, and a thing with a name can be brought to the Father. He isn't disgusted by your anger or your itch or your drift. He knows the boy underneath every one of them, and He's been waiting for the man to bring the boy home. May He meet you in the truck, at the screen, and in the recliner, and may He give you one small refusal this week that becomes a habit.
