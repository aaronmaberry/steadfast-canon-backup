# 8. The Empty Chair

The permit in the boy's pocket was a few hours old, and the lot behind the high school was empty on a Saturday afternoon. Curtis sat down in the passenger seat of his own car. His hands had nothing useful to do. The boy had the wheel.

He drove well. A year of waiting for this showed up in the way he braked, early and smooth. Two slow laps past the light poles. Curtis had his right hand clamped on the grab handle above the door, and his foot kept pressing the floor mat for a brake that wasn't there. Every time the boy touched the gas, "Easy" came out of Curtis in a voice he wouldn't have claimed. On the third lap the boy put the car in park. "Dad. You're doing the thing."

I know the moment when a man finally sees his own reflex. For Curtis it came that night at the kitchen sink, after the house had gone to sleep. No man had ever sat in that seat for him. When he was learning to drive, his mother took the passenger side, both palms flat on the dashboard, the two of them scared together. His father had been gone for years by then, and there was no other man to ask. Curtis had never once seen what calm looks like in the seat next to a boy learning the wheel. He had a handle to grab and one word to say, and the word came out sharp.

## Reading the chair

The chair at the head of the table is a boy's first school of manhood. The sermons come later, and so do the books. First comes whoever sat in that chair, or the hole they left. You were reading him before you could read words. What time he walked in the door. What his silence meant. Whether his hands were safe. Whether your mother's words reached his face or sailed past it. Whether the plans he made with you on Monday still had him in them by Saturday. That course was finished before anyone handed you a house key.

This part of the book is a sorting. Some of what you carry was laid on you. Some of it is still yours to use.

Some of you were raised by an empty chair. Divorce, death, prison, or a leaving nobody ever explained. The outline where a man should have been did the teaching. You learned that men leave. Your mother's exhaustion became your repair job. You learned to figure out life with nobody to ask.

Some of you had a man in the chair who was missing anyway. The job took him, or the bottle, or a television that got the attention you never got. Ask what he thought of you and you come up blank. Family stories excuse a father like that, and he didn't earn the excuse. That wound can run as deep as the one left by a father who packed a suitcase and walked out, and it hides itself, because the neighbors saw him in the house and decided your trouble was made up.

Some of you were raised by danger. The sound of his keys hitting the counter told you what kind of night it would be. You got small. Or you got loud on purpose, hoping the storm would spend itself on you and leave your mother alone.

And some of you were raised by a faithful man. I'm glad. Hold on to that. Even the best fathers have an edge, and the edge came with you when you left home. Every man in this book arrived with a mark from that chair. For some of you, it may take a while before you can see yours.

## The chair never stays empty

A boy can't live around a hole that size. Something sits down in it, and whatever sits down finishes raising him.

Often it's the boy himself. He becomes his own father. He writes his own rules and talks his own fear down to a size he can carry. Some real strength comes out of that. So does a man who can't be fathered by anyone anymore, not by other men and not by God. He's held the job alone for so long that another pair of hands on the weight feels like drowning.

For a lot of boys, a lesson takes the chair. Need someone and you'll get hurt. So he stopped needing. The man he became asks for nothing. His wife could live with him for twenty years and never learn one thing he wants. He says he doesn't want anything, and he believes it.

Other boys hand the chair to the whole room. They can read everyone's mood in a second. Their own mood is a closed book.

Or a crowd takes the chair. He buys thin approval with grades, with jokes, with a fastball. The gray comes into his beard and the performance keeps going, and he couldn't tell you who's still holding the scorecard.

Sometimes the only thing in the chair is the man he swore he'd never become. Curtis made that vow long before he was old enough to drive. His children would never be left. They haven't been, and he's right to be glad about that. But the vow is also his whole picture of a father. He'd memorized the turns he would never take. Nobody ever taught him how to sit quietly in the passenger seat while a boy learned the wheel. The grab handle was the only tool he had.

## Inherited, and still yours

You inherited whoever sat in that chair. Nobody asks a grade-school boy to vote on whether his father stays. It happened to you, and it shaped you. God keeps the record of it, and it matters to Him.

From today on, what you do with it is yours. Curtis meets his own kids with a short fuse, cut in the shape of a father who wasn't there. The fuse belongs to Curtis now. His wife meets it in the kitchen she actually lives in. She never had an appointment with the decade he grew up in. I've treated a clear explanation of my own wound as if it were a changed life. Plenty of men can explain themselves with precision and then go right on living inside the explanation, because explaining starts to feel like moving.

Scripture is plain about men in this condition. Joseph's father loved him and still couldn't keep him out of the pit. David was out with the sheep on the day a prophet came to look over his father's sons, and nobody thought to call him in. Even Jesus grew up with an earthly father who drops out of the story before the years we know best. Scripture doesn't hide any of it, and none of those men were excused from what came next. Take that as dignity. God speaks to you as a man who can act on what was done to him. He's talking to the right man.

## Where the chair goes

Three jobs belong to the man who was handed this chair.

First, he says it. I've watched men big enough to fill a doorway go completely still when I asked about their fathers. "I don't really think about it." That sentence admits the thinking. Say yours once, out loud, where you can hear it. My father left. My father was in the house and I never knew him. My father scared me. My father was a good man, and part of me is still sitting just out of his reach.

Second, he grieves, and that's the job men will trade almost anything to skip. Grief lets the loss be its real size. A chair nobody sat in. A shoulder your hand never found. Words a teenage boy needed and a grown man somehow still waits to hear. Give it the weight you'd give a death. A management plan won't do your grieving for you. I keep coming back to that boy left out in the field with the sheep.

:::margin
Psalm 27:10 (NIV)
"Though my father and mother forsake me, the LORD will receive me."
:::

David doesn't pretend the leaving didn't happen. He names the wound, and he names where he took it.

The third job has a chapter of its own, "The Father Who Does Not Leave," so I'll only point to it here. There's a Father who stays. The empty chair can be the first thing you say about fathers. If you let it be the last thing, you've believed a lie, and this part of the book exists to put the truth in the seat that lie has been holding.

:::margin
Psalm 68:5-6 (NIV)
"A father to the fatherless, a defender of widows, is God in his holy dwelling. God sets the lonely in families, he leads out the prisoners with singing; but the rebellious live in a sun-scorched land."
:::

A father to the fatherless. That's His name.

## The chair follows you

No single room can hold that chair, and no new season retires it.

Your father may be alive and still gone. Quit waiting for his manhood to sign the permit for yours. Put your weight on the Father who's already here.

The first year you hold a son, the chair comes back as a catch in your breath. What if this passes through my hands to him? That fear means your eyes are open. The men who repeat the chair are the ones who never turned around to look at it.

If you're divorced, I won't dress this up. You're somebody's empty chair now. Showing up is what starts to answer it. The handoff of the kids. The game. The call you keep making. Hold on through the months when the only reply you get is "nothing."

Maybe death ended the conversation before it ever started. I'm sorry. Say the words anyway, to God, who knew your father better than you ever could, and to a brother. Your father can't hear them now. They still need to be said.

## The next Saturday

The next weekend they were back in the same lot. Before the car moved, Curtis told his son the truth. "Nobody ever sat in this seat for me. I'm learning it while you learn this." His hand let go of the handle, settled on his knee, and stayed there. The boy took the first corner too wide. Curtis said, "You've got it." It was the first time in his life he'd chosen a father's sentence on purpose.

The boy won't remember the laps. He'll remember the man who stayed in the seat.

You didn't choose who sat at the head of your childhood table. The chairs you sit in now are a choice. Sit in them and stay, so the children watching you learn a different man from the one you had to study. Come down from being your own father long enough for fathering to reach you. There's been more in the seat beside you than you were ever trained to see.

## Before you turn the page

Bring Him the chair the way it actually was. May the Father who sets the lonely in families take you in, the way David said He would. And tonight, at the table that's yours, sit down and stay.
