# 3. The Four Marks

Those maps came apart in your hands. The question that remains is sharper than the one you arrived with. What, then, is a man?

I want you holding the answer as one pocket sentence. We will open it afterward, one mark at a time, on the scale of an ordinary week. Picture first. A sentence with no picture climbs back onto the shelf.

## What a mark is

Twenty years in the same shop and the room testifies before the man does. Repeated cuts have grooved the bench until the wood knows the job. Coffee has stained a circle where the cup belongs. Hooks for the wrenches follow the path his hand already takes. The ring on my own bench is from a mug I reheated twice and still walked off without. Wanting the name craftsman never cut his grooves. A Saturday class on tools never cut them. Work did, on a run of days that blurred into each other.

Call that a mark. Self-regard leaves nothing you can see. A printed title leaves nothing you can see. Repeated work, kept up for years, presses a mark into a man. Dawn enthusiasm fades by lunch. The hour you walk back into the house is the hour that cuts.

Robert Lewis is the one who formed the sentence this way. It did not start with me. Men keep passing it on because it survives contact with a real week. I have laid it over my own record. I have laid it over men from the youngest season to the oldest I know. It has not come up false.

**A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.**

Go back over it and take your time. The line holds four marks. Four actions. Stay at them and they become four things visible on a man, as visible as those grooves.

"In your relationships with one another, have the same mindset as Christ Jesus."

On a man, that mind takes the shape of the four marks. Under his roof. With his crew. On a week with nothing special in it.

The men ahead in this book, Roy and Tomás among them, are composites. Real tables sat under those conversations. Names were blended so the face you recognize is your own, and your neighbor stays out of it. Nobody blended the sentence. It stays.

## He rejects passivity

Find the tailgate in any town. A decent man is on it. Weather, the school board, the city's water, he will give you a full account of all three while the task he drove there to finish waits at his back. The right hour has not come. Or the right price. Or the right mood. The wait has gone on so long that he has started to trust it.

Leave a man alone and passivity is where he settles. The look of it is seldom lazy. It dresses as patience, or as peacekeeping. The line it prefers is "I didn't want to overstep," spoken by a man convinced he is being humble. I have said that line under my own roof. Scripture's first man did the same kind of nothing. The serpent entered the garden and began to twist the single command God had spoken, and the man stood beside the woman the whole time. His hand stayed off the fruit. His mouth stayed off the lie. The world broke on his silence.

Volume is a poor substitute for this mark. So is pushing people around. The mark is movement. The job is clear. You are the one on site. You get down off the tailgate. You open the hard conversation while it is still yours to open. You start with the feeling still absent. **Stop Waiting** stays with this until you can spot it in the week you are actually having.

## He accepts responsibility

The second mark is weight. August, a friend's gun safe, four hundred pounds, a hallway that barely cleared our shoulders, air thick enough to lean on. Lift a load like that and a moment arrives when retreat is gone. Both of you have it up. The hall offers nowhere to park it. Halfway along, you lose the right to announce that the safe is somebody else's problem. You finish the carry. Then you set it down.

A boy handles weight by waiting for an assignment and then arguing that the assignment was unfair. A man looks at the thing in front of him and says "that's mine." Fault can belong elsewhere. Blame could pass him by. He takes it anyway. Unopened bills. The child no one is speaking to. The apology still unsaid. A young shoulder under a yoke is a gift. Carrying forms the man who carries. **Pick Up the Weight** is how to take the yoke and how to refuse the loads that were never issued to you.

## He leads courageously

Picture a stock tank in cold weather and boys stalled along the bank. The water outruns every guess they made, and the whole line is waiting on some other boy. The one who goes in is the leader, because now the rest have a direction. Office doors and titles can wait in the building.

Houses, crews, and churches all stall until somebody moves. After three quiet days in a marriage, somebody has to speak "I was wrong" before anyone else will. Somebody has to pray aloud where the table has never heard prayer. A drifting brother needs the first call. A meeting full of nods needs the first true sentence. I have held back on every one of those and labeled the hold caution. Fear can be in the man and the boots can still go on. That walk is courage. God charged Joshua to be strong and courageous, and the charge told him how to act inside the fear, and why the acting was possible. **Go First** opens that charge.

## He expects the greater reward

Good men and godly men divide at the last mark. I set it at the end because men step over it.

A pecan tree keeps its own calendar. Set one in the ground this fall. Years of watering stand between you and a nut you would bother to crack. Orchards of them have always been gifts to people who arrive later. The man planting spends strength on a harvest his own hands will miss.

There is the fourth mark. A real man expects the greater reward, God's reward, and that hope governs ordinary days more than any other line in this book. Unseen obedience becomes possible. A hard decade of marriage becomes possible. Money that never returns, and hours nobody thanks, become possible, because a Father sees the hidden thing. Coming to God includes believing He is real and believing He pays the one who seeks Him. God is willing to say the word reward. Expect nothing from Him and you will shop elsewhere. A crowd's approval. Cash. A woman. A newer truck. A son who thinks well of you. A father who finally does. **Live for a Reward That Lasts** follows that search. The other three marks survive a costly Tuesday, I now believe, only because of this one.

## Why four, and why these

Men come apart in four familiar ways, and the marks answer them one for one. Doing nothing is the break called passivity. Refusing weight is the break called blame, or evasion. Hanging back under a polite name is the break called cowardice. Aiming a whole career at the wrong prize, while the town calls you a success, is the slow break. Trouble, in the men I have sat with, has traced home to one of the four every time.

Any decent man might try the first three, with Christian faith or with none. The fourth is what ties the other three to Christ. Cost arrives, the room stays silent, and the fourth is why he continues. Drop it and you are holding a self-help list. Keep it and you are holding a life that goes on after you.

Seasons change under a man's feet. The four marks do not. Seriousness can start in a young man now, with no wife on hand to force it. In the noise, a young father can turn his face toward a wife who is used up. A man in his middle years can notice the drift toward applause and point the truck home. An older man can rise with nothing left to pin on his chest and still locate the work. Four marks. Another Tuesday.

## Before you turn the page

The sentence is yours. This week, leave three marks on the shelf. Stay with the faint one. Change, in the men I have watched, arrived as one mark on one plain day, and then the same mark again. May the Father who formed you in His image point to the mark He means to press in first, and may His patience run longer than your slow beginning, as it has run longer than mine.
