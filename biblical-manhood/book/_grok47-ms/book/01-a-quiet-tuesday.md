# 1. A Quiet Tuesday

If you had asked me at twenty-five which days would decide the man I became, I would have given you the big ones. The wedding. The morning the first child came. A funeral. The job I took and the job I turned down. The Saturday a pastor put me under in a river and I came up sure.

I have had those days now. They are stones on a bank I can point at and say, that was the year. The deciding happened somewhere else, in a long run of days I could not tell apart if you paid me.

A man's life is decided on Tuesdays. Not on the Sunday when he knows how to stand. Not on the Saturday when he takes his son somewhere and feels like a father for an afternoon. On the day in the middle, with nothing special on it. The day nobody writes down. He comes home tired. The house is already loud. There is a sentence he could say or swallow, a call he could return or let die, a battery he could change or step under one more week. That is where the marks get made.

## What a Tuesday looks like

So here is one.

A man I will call Anthony left the house at 6:10 on a Tuesday in October. He had slept badly. His wife had said something the night before about money that was not quite fair, and he had let it sit. It rode in the truck with him.

At the shop a customer's truck was promised by lunch. The parts ticket was still on the bench where he had set it, meaning to call it in. He had not. The fuel pump was not coming. He had to push the job while the shop lead listened from two bays over and said nothing. At lunch he sat in the cab with his phone for forty minutes. Afterward he could not have named one thing he had read.

His father called in the afternoon. The man calls maybe twice a year. The name on the screen still makes Anthony feel nine for a second. He let it go to voicemail and told himself later.

He finished the work. He drove home with the radio off, pulled into the garage, and stood in the doorway the way he does most nights. Then he walked in. He kissed her on the side of the head without looking at her. He ate. He got the kids down. By 8:40 he was in the recliner with the game on and the phone in his hand. She was in the kitchen. The money stayed where he had left it.

The thing he let sit was a decision. The part he did not order was a decision. The phone at 8:40, while his wife stood alone in the kitchen, was a decision. None of them felt like one. They felt like a day. Give a man ten thousand days like that and he becomes a particular man without ever standing up and choosing it.

Anthony is a good man. He did not set out to wound anybody on that Tuesday. He worked, he provided, he came home, he was gentle enough. He is also becoming something, one ordinary day at a time. Ask his wife what, and she could tell you.

## Why the big days can't do it

I have stopped trusting the big days to make men. A big day is a burst. It gathers what you feel and what you hope and sets it on a table in front of witnesses. God builds days like that on purpose. Later in this book I will tell you to make one for your son. A burst is still not a shape. A mark is what repeated work leaves behind, the way a callus comes from a thousand mornings nobody photographed.

Men come home from a conference on fire and are back in the recliner inside two weeks. They spent the burst and skipped the shape.

On the big days you are watched. A wedding, a baptism, a funeral. You know the part, and most men play it. What tells you about a man is what he does when the room is empty, when nobody would know if he made the call or left it, opened the drawer or left it shut, said the true thing or let it sit. Tuesday is when nobody is watching. That is why it counts.

## What God does with Tuesdays

If you have been in church a while, you may have filed the spiritual life under retreats and altars. God does meet men there. I am not arguing with that. Look at how the Bible actually talks about the way a man is formed.

:::margin
Luke 16:10 (NIV)
"Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with very little will also be dishonest with much."
:::

Very little is a Tuesday phrase. The man who can be trusted in the small unwatched things is the man who can be trusted when the room fills up. The recliner, the voicemail, the kiss that does not look her in the face. Those are the shop where the man who will one day stand in a hard room gets made, or does not.

When Jesus tells about a house that held, notice what He says the man did.

:::margin
Matthew 7:24 (NIV)
"Therefore everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man who built his house on the rock."
:::

Puts them into practice. Practice is what you do again until it is how you do things. The wise man laid courses on days nobody drove by to admire. When the rain came, the house held because of those days.

Most of a man's walk with God is Tuesday. Most of his marriage is Tuesday. Most of his fathering is Tuesday. If you want to know what you are becoming, leave the retreat out of it. Look at last Tuesday.

## The other Tuesday

Here is Anthony's day again, a year later. I am not describing a trap. I am describing a place where a man has more say than he thinks.

He left at 6:10 again. He had slept badly again. Somewhere on the drive he said, out loud, "Father, I don't want to haul this all day." He pulled into a gas station and sent his wife a text. He was sorry he had gone quiet. He wanted ten minutes after the kids were down. Ninety seconds. He was still tired. The thing was no longer riding with him.

The part for the lunch job was on the shelf, because he had called it in the day before. When a different job went sideways anyway, he told the shop lead the truth and called the customer before anybody had to overhear a scramble. His father called. Anthony felt nine for a second. Then he answered. Six awkward minutes about nothing. The first time in two years he had not let it die in the voicemail.

At home he caught himself standing in the doorway and walked through it. He put the phone on the counter, face down. He looked at his wife when he kissed her. After the kids were down they sat at the counter for ten minutes and talked about the money. He wanted to win a point and he laid the point down. He did the dishes. He prayed, badly and briefly, with his hand on her back. Then he slept.

His daughter is seven. She watches his face when her mother walks in. She could not have told you what changed. Something had, in about six places, each of them under two minutes. Give that man ten thousand of those Tuesdays and you get a different girl learning what a husband's face does.

That is the claim of this book. Know what a man does on a plain day, then do it again.

## The same Tuesday at every age

A Tuesday does not look the same at nineteen as it does at seventy. It decides the same kind of thing. Find yourself in one of these four before we go on.

Eli is nineteen. He works the counter at a parts store and lives with two roommates. Around ten the apartment goes quiet and the phone is in his hand. What he does in that pocket of quiet is writing the man he will be at thirty. He told me once he was waiting for his real life to start. His real life is the ten o'clock.

Tomás is twenty-nine. You will meet him again. The baby does not sleep. At two in the morning he hears the crying and hears his wife start to move, and lying still feels like the only sane choice a tired man has. Whether he gets up is the marriage. Not the trip they cannot afford. The two o'clock.

Walt is fifty-five. He built a business from one truck to eleven. At 6:15 he can leave the shop and make dinner, or stay another hour because the shop is quieter than the house. He stayed for twenty years, an hour here and an hour there, and never once held a meeting about it. The staying decided itself.

Roy is seventy-two. He turned in a state trooper's badge after thirty-one years. At 5:30 the alarm goes off and there is nothing he has to pin on. Nobody would notice if he slept until nine. What he does at 5:30 is deciding whether the last stretch of his life is a long exit or the most useful years he has had. He gets up and makes the coffee. You will see why that matters.

Four men. Four hours that do not feel like decisions. That is why the book starts here.

## What comes next

Soon you will get a sentence with four marks in it. The rest of the book shows what those marks look like on a Tuesday. Before that, one word for the man who suspects everything he has been told about being a man is thin or contradictory. He is not crazy. **You Are Not Crazy** is next.

## Before you turn the page

I do not know which of those four you are, or whether you are somewhere between them. I know your week has a Tuesday in it, and that Tuesday has a moment, probably several, where you will choose without feeling the choice. I am not asking you to be a different man by Sunday. I am asking you to see the moment once, and to do one small thing in it on purpose. The Father who made you sees that moment. He is glad when you meet it. May He give you eyes for a plain day, and the small strength it takes to walk through a doorway instead of standing in it.
