# 28. Give Your Son a Hill

Lyle puts it at eleven, maybe twelve. A fall morning. The kitchen tap coughed, pulled air, and died.

Water for that house came off the hill. A spring a quarter mile up the pasture, his grandfather's concrete box, pipe to the kitchen and on out to the stock trough. A dead tap meant a climb.

The boy had handed the bucket up a dozen times while his father cleaned the box. This morning the man stood at the dry sink, eyed the hill, and said, "Spring's quit. Go see to it." Go. Company was not on offer.

Years later, hair white, the farm already sold, Lyle told me he froze at the yard gate and looked up. He knew he could not do it. The lid outweighed him. He turned. His father had taken what was left of the coffee onto the back step and sat. The man was staying there.

Morning went to the job. The lid came up and dropped once across his fingers. A drowned possum lay on the screen. He lifted it with his hands. Silt came out of water cold enough to ache his arms to the shoulder. The pipe went in wrong. He pulled it and set it right. Three looks down the hill. Three times the man was still on the step, coffee long past warm.

Water hit the trough before Lyle was halfway home. He came through the yard soaked to the collar, mud at the knees, two knuckles bleeding. His father held the look a long moment. "Well. I figured you could."

The step never lost him. Lyle worked the meaning out later. A father on the hill would have taught the boy he was unable alone. A father who stayed put taught him he was able.

You gave him a name. Give him a hill, or the name is a compliment with nowhere to land. He turns into the thing you called him by climbing something that proves the word.

## Boys need difficulty

Difficulty a little past a boy, with a man watching and slow to rescue, is how boys come into manhood. Wrap him against every hard thing and you starve the growth you meant to save. Isaac carried wood up a mountain.

:::margin
Genesis 22:6 (NIV)
"Abraham took the wood for the burnt offering and placed it on his son Isaac, and he himself carried the fire and the knife. As the two of them went on together,"
:::

The wood went on the son. Abraham kept fire and knife. They went up together, one under the load, one with his eyes on the boy. Sheep were what David's father handed over.

:::margin
1 Samuel 17:34-35 (NIV)
"But David said to Saul, 'Your servant has been keeping his father's sheep. When a lion or a bear came and carried off a sheep from the flock, I went after it, struck it and rescued the sheep from its mouth.'"
:::

No one shipped David a lion. A father sent him into hills with a real flock to keep, and the lion arrived there. Goliath met a boy who had already climbed. Jesus came up in a shop under a father's eye. Luke's line is wisdom and stature. That is a boy with somewhere to climb.

Love and comfort, with no hill, leave him starving. He will locate one. The ones he locates run worse. A fight. A dare. Headlights off on a back road. Or the quiet version, a basement he never exits, because climbing was never demonstrated and the world looks like a slope with an empty bottom.

## What a hill is

Real is the first test. Somebody besides the boy needs the thing done. No water in the house until he finishes. He can smell a fake job across the grass. A swept garage that was already clean, assigned so he will learn character, is a lie he will not bother believing.

A little past him is the second. Beyond yesterday's skill. Short of a man's full strength. The next piece. Easy teaches nothing. Impossible teaches I cannot.

Risk, inside a fence, is the third. Failure allowed. A small hurt allowed. A dry tap when he comes down, allowed. Remove the chance of failure and you removed the hill. Fathers pick with care. Lyle's knew the box, knew the copperheads had gone down for the year, and kept the step.

An outcome he owns is the fourth. I tried belongs to boys. Done, or not yet and here is my next move, belongs to men. He learns the second sentence where I tried will not be accepted as a finish. Water in the trough, or none.

A name after it is the fifth. Coming down, win or mess, the father speaks. Capable was the word beforehand. The climb happened. Now the word is true in his body.

## The hills, by age

Small years, a job the house needs. Every grocery bag, four trips if that is the cost, and tell him he is working. School years, ownership. A garden bed. A pet's whole life. Trash out with no reminder. Just before thirteen, wages from a man who is not you, one scare that stays safe, one climb he takes alone while you stay in sight. High school, a boss who can fire him, money he may lose, a project he finishes, a decision you do not overrule. Then launch. His life is the hill. You are the man who answers when he calls.

Leave trophies off when the sport is the father's pride and the referee takes the blame. Leave screens off. They fake the climb and skip the weight. A thousand levels, and the tap still dry.

## The father's part

Give the hill away. You are faster, and finishing it yourself steals the climb.

Watch him struggle. The lid can hit his fingers. Mothers feel this harder, so you lead the explanation, and you lead it gently. Rescue a boy who is safe, and you teach a need for rescue. A watched boy who is safe learns he can do without it. Sitting with empty hands is the job, even on the day it feels like you walked off. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. Passivity is the father who climbs it for him. Everybody is more comfortable. Nobody is formed.

Small failure now is mercy. Forty dollars gone because he failed to plan can spare him forty thousand when the numbers are large. Forbid every fall and the first one arrives with a wife, a mortgage, and an empty step.

On the way down, questions. What happened. What did you learn. What changes next time. Then quiet. Then the name. I figured you could. You stayed with it. A hard day with no sentence at the bottom was only weather.

Raise the next one. A little. Year after year of a slightly higher hill, and the boy who leaves is a man. The hands will show it.

:::margin
Hebrews 12:11 (NIV)
"No discipline seems pleasant at the time, but painful. Later on, however, it produces a harvest of righteousness and peace for those who have been trained by it."
:::

Trained. Pleasant is the wrong hope, for him and for the man on the step. The harvest is the later word.

## How fathers get it wrong

A mountain on day one crushes him. Failure becomes the meaning of hills, and he stops. Keep it a little past, and stop there. Some men assign their own unfinished dream. The sport they missed. The shop they want continued. Some men rescue at midnight, phone the coach, pay the debt. Each rescue is one sentence. You cannot. Some men shame the fall until every hill is a fear he will route around until he dies. The lid hit Lyle. The pipe went in wrong. His father let both pass without a word.

## Hills in every season

Diapers are early enough. Let him fight the toy before your hands fix it. Do every hard thing for the baby because the cry undoes you, and you will do every hard thing for the teenager because the sulk undoes you. Divorce adds its own trap. Weekends with the hill edited out, guilt in the driver's seat, a hope that fun will make him want the driveway. He needs work that belongs to him at your place, and a project left unfinished so the unfinished part calls him back. Grandfathers may be the best hill-givers alive. Time, tools, patience a father has not grown. No son of your own still leaves boys at church who have never climbed, because the bottom of their hill was empty. Point. Say go. Stay. Daughters are owed real work too, and real ownership, and a father who says strong when they come down. Different hills. Same method.

## Well, I figured you could

Hills have gone out from Lyle to sons, grandsons, and boys in a Sunday class. I asked for the secret. "Pick the right hill. Stay on the step. Say the right thing when he comes down." He laughed after. "And don't tell his mother what was in the box."

Paul expected his son in the faith to meet hills for the length of the road.

:::margin
2 Timothy 2:3 (NIV)
"Join with me in suffering, like a good soldier of Christ Jesus."
:::

A soldier, trained on climbs, able to clear a spring alone.

He is at the gate, checking the house.

Take the step. Stay there.

## Before you turn the page

The step is the hard obedience. Years of getting up off it have not finished him. Boys bend and recover, and a late hill still counts. The Father who loaded Isaac with wood and walked beside him understands a watched son under weight. May He give you nerve for the right hill this week, and the sterner nerve to sit through it, and may the line waiting for you at the bottom be the one Lyle heard.
