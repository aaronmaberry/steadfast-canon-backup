# 8. The Empty Chair

Saturday afternoon the school lot was bare, and the permit in the boy's pocket was only hours old. Curtis took the passenger seat of his own car. His hands had nowhere useful to go. The boy had the wheel.

He drove well. A year of private rehearsal showed up in the brake, early and smooth. Two slow laps past the poles. Curtis locked his right hand on the door. His left foot searched the carpet for a pedal that belonged to the driver. Each time the boy touched the gas, "Easy" came out of Curtis in a voice he would not have claimed. On the third lap the boy set the shifter in park. "Dad. You're doing the thing."

I know the hour a man finally sees his own reflex. For Curtis it arrived at the sink, after the house had gone to sleep. He had never been the adult in that seat. When the wheel had been his, his mother filled it, palms flat on the dash, the pair of them frightened, his father already years gone and no other man available. Calm, in the seat beside a learning boy, was a sight nobody had given him. He had a handle. He had one word. The word showed up already bent.

## Reading the chair

A chair is the first school of manhood. The sermon arrives later. The book arrives later. First comes whoever took the head of the table, or the gap that person left. You were reading him while letters were still a mystery. The hour he walked in. The job his silence was doing. Whether those hands were safe. Whether your mother's sentence found his face or sailed past her. Whether the plan you had named on two days still had him in it. That course was finished before anyone handed you a house key.

The work of these pages is a sorting. Part of what you carry was laid on you. Part of it is still yours to use.

Vacancy raised some of you. Divorce, death, prison, or a departure that never explained itself. The outline where a man should have been did the teaching. Men go. A mother's exhaustion becomes the son's repair job. You learn to solve a life with nobody to ask.

A body in the chair raised others, and the man was missing anyway. The job took him, or drink, or a television that received a focus you never received. Ask what he thought of you and the answer is a blank. Family stories excuse that father. He did not earn the excuse. The wound goes as deep as a suitcase by the door, and it conceals itself, because neighbors saw him in the house and decided your trouble was invented.

Danger raised others. Keys on the counter were the forecast. You got small. Or you got loud on purpose, hoping the storm would spend itself on you and spare your mother.

A faithful man raised some of you. I am glad. Keep that. Even the best of them had an edge, and the edge left the house with you. Every man in this book arrived with a mark. A few of you will need more time before the mark will show.

## The chair never stays empty

A boy cannot keep house around a hole that size. Something sits down, and the sitter finishes the raising.

Often he sits down himself. He runs his own life as its father. He writes rules. He talks fear down to a size he can carry. Strength comes out of some of that labor. So does a man no longer able to be fathered, by people or by God. He has held the office alone so long that another set of hands on the weight feels like water over his head.

A lesson sits down for many boys. Need someone, and you get hurt. So he quit. The adult he became requests nothing. Years with him will not teach a wife a single want. He says he has none, and he believes the report.

Other boys give the seat to the whole room. Moods around him are easy to read. His own mood is a closed book.

Or a crowd takes it. Thin approval gets purchased with grades, with a joke, with a fastball. Gray arrives in the beard and the performance continues, and he could not name the person still holding the scorecard.

Sometimes the only thing in the seat is a man he has sworn to avoid becoming. Curtis swore before driving was even a thought. His children would not be abandoned. They have not been, and the gladness is right. The oath is also his whole picture of a father. He had catalogued the turns he would refuse. Nobody had trained him to be quiet in the passenger seat while a boy learned the wheel. The handle was the tool he owned.

## Inherited, and still yours

You inherited the occupant. A grade-school boy is never asked to vote on a father's staying. The event arrived. It formed him. God keeps the record, and the record is not a matter of indifference to Him.

The handling, from this day, is yours. Curtis meets his own children with a short fuse cut in the shape of a father who was not there. The fuse is his. His wife meets it in the kitchen she actually lives in. She has no appointment with the decade that left. I have treated a clear map of a wound as if the map were a changed man. Plenty of men can explain themselves with precision and then live inside the explanation, because explaining had begun to feel like motion.

Scripture stays plain about men in this condition. Joseph's father loved him and still could not stop the pit. David was with the sheep on the day a prophet looked over the other sons. Jesus grew up under a man who was not His father, and that man drops from the story before the years we know. The chair marked each one. Pardon was not included. Take it as dignity. God speaks to you as a man able to act on what was done to him. He has the right man.

## Where the chair goes

Three labors belong to the man who received this chair.

He says it. I have put the question to men big enough to block a doorway, and I have seen them go still. "I don't really think about it." The sentence admits the thinking. Say yours once, in earshot of yourself. My father left. My father was in the house and I never knew him. My father scared me. My father was a good man, and a part of me still sits outside his reach.

Then he mourns, which is the labor men will trade away. Mourning lets the loss have its real dimensions. A seat unused. A shoulder his hand never found. Lines a teenager required, lines you are somehow still waiting to hear. Give it the weight you would give a death. A management plan will not grieve in your place. I come back, again and again, to the son who was left out in the field.

:::margin
Psalm 27:10 (NIV)
"Though my father and mother forsake me, the LORD will receive me."
:::

David will not describe the leaving as if it failed to happen. He names the wound. He names where he carried it.

The third labor has a chapter of its own, so a gesture is enough here. A Father stays. The empty chair can be the opening word you speak about fathers. Leave it as the final word, and you have chosen a lie. This part of the book exists to seat the truth in the place that lie has occupied.

:::margin
Psalm 68:5-6 (NIV)
"A father to the fatherless, a defender of widows, is God in his holy dwelling. God sets the lonely in families, he leads out the prisoners with singing; but the rebellious live in a sun-scorched land."
:::

A father to the fatherless. That is His name.

## The chair follows you

A single room cannot hold it. A change of season will not retire it.

Your father may be living and still gone. Stop waiting for his manhood to sign the permit for yours. Put the weight on the Father who is already present.

The first year you hold a son, the chair returns as a caught breath. What if this passes through my hands into his? Fear of that sort means your eyes are open. The men who repeat the chair are the ones who never turned toward it.

Divorce receives the sentence I will leave undecorated. You are somebody's empty chair now. Presence is what begins to answer it. The exchange of the children. The game. The call you keep making. Hold on through the months whose only reply is "nothing."

Death can end the talk before it begins. I am sorry. Say the words to God, who knew the man past your knowing, and say them to a brother. Your father cannot hear them now.

## The next Saturday

The next weekend the lot had them again. Before the car moved, Curtis said the true thing to his boy. Nobody ever sat in this seat for me. I am learning it while you learn that. His hand left the door, settled on his knee, and remained. The boy opened the first corner too wide. Curtis said, "You've got it." Until that moment he had never selected a fatherly sentence on purpose.

Memory will release the laps. It will keep the man who stayed.

Your old table's end was assigned to you. The chairs you hold now are a decision. Occupy them and remain, so the children who study you meet a different man from the one you had to study. Come down from running your own life as its father, long enough for fathering to reach you. More was in the seat beside you than your eyes had been trained to register.

## Before you turn the page

Bring Him the chair as it actually was. May the Father who places the lonely in families take you in, the way David said. Tonight, at the table that is yours, sit down and stay.
