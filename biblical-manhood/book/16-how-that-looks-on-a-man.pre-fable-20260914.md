# 16. How That Looks on a Man

I knew a man whose garage was the cleanest room in his house. There was pegboard with every tool outlined in marker, and labeled bins, and a swept floor, and you could have eaten off the workbench. And in the bottom drawer of the red toolbox, the one that locked, was a life his wife didn't know about.

Nothing dramatic at first: a second phone, a few receipts, and later, more. But the drawer had been there for years before it held anything worse than a lie, because the drawer wasn't really about what was in it. The drawer was a habit. It was a place in the man that was his alone, that nobody got to see, that he'd built long before he had anything to hide in it. The clean garage was the surface. The locked drawer was the man.

In the last chapter we named the problem inside. This chapter is about what it looks like from the outside, on a man in particular, because sin wears different clothes on us than it does on anyone else, and if you don't know what it looks like on a man you'll walk right past it in the mirror every morning.

## The shapes it takes

Here are the shapes I've seen most often. Not all of them will be yours. One or two will be.

The first is the locked drawer. Every man is tempted to keep a second set of books, a part of his life that's his alone, managed and hidden and curated. For some it's money: an account she doesn't know about, a debt he hasn't mentioned. For some it's the screen. For some it's a friendship that isn't what he says it is. For some it's simply his inner life, his real fears and real thoughts, which he's decided nobody gets to see. The drawer starts small, as a boy's reasonable wish for a little privacy, and it grows into a man's hidden room, and everything bad that ever happens to a man happens in that room.

The second is the fortress. This is the man who has no needs. Ask him how he's doing and he's fine. Ask what he needs and he needs nothing. He's the corner post for everybody and he leans on no one, and he calls that strength, and it isn't. It's pride in a work shirt, the same pride that kept Walt in a bank parking lot with three hundred names and nobody to call. The fortress looks a great deal like the second mark, accepting responsibility, and that's what makes it hard to spot. But a man who accepts responsibility can still be helped. The fortress can't be. It was built to keep out exactly the thing the man needs.

The third is the scoreboard. Comparison, envy, the quiet ranking of every man in the room by his truck, his job, his wife, his kids, his body, his salary. Most men do this without noticing, the way you'd notice the temperature, and it poisons everything, because a man who's always measuring can never be glad for another man's win and can never rest in his own, since somebody always has more. Saul had this, and it drove him to try to kill the young man who'd just won a battle for him.

The fourth is the podium. This is sin dressed as righteousness, and Jesus hated it more than any other kind. It's the man who's generous when people are watching, spiritual when the pastor's in the room, patient with his kids in the church parking lot and a storm in the truck on the way home. He's performing goodness for a human audience, and Jesus said such men have already been paid in full, which is the cruelest thing that can happen to a man: to receive all the reward he's ever going to get from people who don't really know him.

The fifth is the throne. Control, harshness, the man who has to win every conversation in his house and makes sure he does, by volume or by silence or by the kind of sarcasm that leaves marks. He tells himself he's leading. His wife would tell you she's managing him, and his kids would tell you they've learned to read the truck in the driveway. Scripture speaks directly to this one and doesn't soften it.

:::margin
Colossians 3:19 (NIV)
"Husbands, love your wives and do not be harsh with them."
:::

God saw it coming. He knew what a man with a little authority would be tempted to do with it, and He put a fence around it in a single sentence. The sixth is contempt, which is the throne's quieter brother. The sneer, the eye roll at his wife's story, the disdain for the softer man at church or the weaker man on the crew or the son who cries. Contempt is a man deciding he's above someone, and it's the fastest way I know to shrivel a soul, because a man who looks down at people can't look up at God. It's the same neck.

And the seventh is the recliner, which we've covered: sloth dressed as rest, the drift. I'll only say that on a man it doesn't look like laziness. It looks like a fellow who's earned his evening, and it costs his house the rafters.

## The man on the roof

There's a man in the Bible who wore about half of those shapes at once, in the span of a single spring, and he was the best man of his generation. I want to walk through it slowly, because it's the clearest picture in Scripture of how sin looks on a man, and because if it could take David it can take you. It starts with passivity.

:::margin
2 Samuel 11:1 (NIV)
"In the spring, at the time when kings go off to war, David sent Joab out with the king's men and the whole Israelite army. They destroyed the Ammonites and besieged Rabbah. But David remained in Jerusalem."
:::

At the time when kings go off to war. That's what kings did, and it's what David had always done. But that year he sent Joab and stayed home, and nobody would have called it sin. He was the king, and he'd earned a spring off. But he was a man on the tailgate with nothing in his hands, and the next verse has him walking around on his roof in the evening with nothing to do, and you know what happens on a roof in the evening to a man with nothing to do.

He sees her, and here's the part men skip: he asks about her, and someone tells him she's married, and he sends for her anyway. Every mark inverted in a single afternoon. He didn't reject passivity; he'd been drifting since spring. He didn't accept responsibility; he'd handed the war to Joab and now he handed his conscience to his appetite. He didn't lead courageously; he used power, which is the coward's substitute for courage, and took what he wanted from a woman who couldn't say no to a king. And he wasn't living for God's reward. He was living for the next hour.

Then comes the locked drawer, because she's pregnant. He calls her husband home from the front, gets him drunk, and tries to get him to sleep with his wife so the child can be passed off. The man won't do it; he's too honorable to sleep in a bed while his brothers are in the field. So David sends him back to the front with a sealed order to put him where the fighting is worst and pull back, and Uriah dies, and David marries the widow, and everything is covered. And then comes the sentence that ought to keep every competent man awake at night.

:::margin
2 Samuel 11:27 (NIV)
"After the time of mourning was over, David had her brought to his house, and she became his wife and bore him a son. But the thing David had done displeased the LORD."
:::

The whole kingdom kept running and the wars kept getting won and nobody knew. David ran Israel for the better part of a year with a murdered man in his drawer and a stolen wife in his house, and he was good at it. Competence is not righteousness. A man can run an entire kingdom while he rots. He can run a business, a church, a household, a crew. The surfaces stay clean while the block is cracked.

Psalm 32, the one we read in the last chapter, is David describing that year from the inside: bones wasting away, strength sapped, a hand heavy on him. That is what a locked drawer does to a man even when the drawer is working.

## What exposed him

It wasn't his conscience, and that's the hard truth. David didn't wake up one morning and decide to come clean. God sent a man.

:::margin
2 Samuel 12:7 (NIV)
"Then Nathan said to David, 'You are the man! This is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: I anointed you king over Israel, and I delivered you from the hand of Saul.'"
:::

You are the man. Nathan told him a story about a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb, and David got furious, and Nathan said: that's you. A brother, or in this case a prophet, walked into the throne room and named it to his face. That's what it took, and it's almost always what it takes. Sin on a man is rarely exposed by the man. It's exposed by a wife who finally says it, or a brother who asks the question in the twenty minutes and won't accept "fine," or a doctor, or a bank, or the hand of God getting so heavy the man can't stand up under it.

Which is why you need a Nathan, and why you need to be one. The whole company of brothers and older men we built in chapters 13 and 14 is, among other things, a way of making sure somebody can walk into your throne room and say four words.

And look at what David did. Not what he said first; he said a great deal. Look at the sentence that mattered.

:::margin
2 Samuel 12:13 (NIV)
"Then David said to Nathan, 'I have sinned against the LORD.' Nathan replied, 'The LORD has taken away your sin. You are not going to die.'"
:::

I have sinned against the LORD. Five words, and no "but." No "she came out on the roof." No "Uriah should have gone home when I told him." No "I'm the king and I've carried a lot." The sentence stops at the period, which is what we said in chapter 5 a man's confession does, and the forgiveness comes in the same breath. There were consequences, terrible ones, for years afterward. But his standing was restored the moment the drawer was opened.

## Checking the mirror

You're not going to see these shapes on yourself by looking harder, because sin is deceitful and that's its nature, so you'll need tests, and here are the ones I use.

The drawer test asks what in your life you wouldn't want your brother to see. Your phone, your browser history, your bank accounts, your calendar, the friendship, the account. If there's a drawer, there's a problem, whatever happens to be in it, because the drawer itself is the problem. A man with nothing hidden has nothing to defend, and a man with nothing to defend is free.

The "no" test asks when you last let somebody tell you no and accepted it. Your wife, about a purchase. A brother, about a plan. An elder, about a decision. A man on a throne doesn't get told no, because he's arranged his life so that nobody can. If you can't remember the last time, you're on a throne.

The wife test is the bravest one. If you're married, ask her, sincerely and in a quiet moment, "Where do you see me hiding? Where am I harsh?" And then don't argue. Listen, and thank her. She has been watching you for years and she knows the shapes better than you do. If you're single, ask your closest brother or your mother the same thing. Somebody has been watching.

The podium test asks what you do only when someone's watching. Where's the gap between the parking lot and the truck, between Sunday morning and Tuesday night? Whatever's in that gap is what you're performing, and whoever's watching is who you're living for.

And the throne test asks who in your house is afraid of you, not who respects you but who is afraid. If the answer isn't "no one," you've got work to do, and the work begins with getting off the throne and onto your knees in front of the people who've been reading your truck in the driveway.

## The shapes in different houses

If you're single, the drawer and the podium are your largest risks. You have a lot of unwatched hours and a lot of incentive to look good at church. Get a brother with the passwords and a habit of asking, and kill the drawer before it has anything worth hiding in it.

If there's a baby in your house, watch the throne. Exhaustion makes a man harsh, and harshness gets rationalized fast when you've slept four hours. Your wife is more tired than you are, and she's watching what you do with your tiredness. Apologize the same night, every time.

If you're divorced, the scoreboard is going to call to you. Comparing your house to hers, your new life to her new life, your Christmas to theirs. Every comparison is a small theft of your own peace. Put the scoreboard down. You've got a Father who sees in secret, and He isn't keeping that score.

And if you're older, look hard at the fortress. Men past sixty have usually become very good at needing nothing, and it has cost them. Roy said the hardest question at his Thursday table was "what do you need from us?" because he'd never once in his life been asked, and he honestly didn't know. It took him three weeks to come back with an answer. When he did, the table changed.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A young man of twenty is most at risk of the drawer and the podium: unwatched hours on one hand, and a strong incentive to look good at church on the other. Eli's drawer was a second browser he'd never told anyone about, and the day he gave a brother the passwords he said he felt lighter than he had in three years. The takeaway for him is to kill the drawer before it holds anything worth hiding, because the drawer itself is the habit.

A man of fifty is most at risk of the scoreboard and the fortress together: measuring himself against every man at church while needing nothing from any of them. Walt had both for twenty years and didn't know it until the bank parking lot. The takeaway for him is that a man who can't remember the last time he let anyone tell him no is on a throne, and a man who can't name what he needs is in a fortress, and both are pride in a work shirt.

## The Man with no drawer

Jesus had no drawer. There was nothing in Him that He hid from the Father, nothing He managed, nothing He performed. When they came to arrest Him, He said He had spoken openly to the world. When they searched for a charge, they had to invent one. He was fully known, all the way down, and unashamed, because there was nothing there that needed hiding.

That's what He's making you into. Not a man with a cleaner garage, but a man with no locked drawer, whose inside and outside match, who can be fully seen by his wife and his brothers and his God without flinching, because the thing that used to need hiding has been confessed and forgiven and is being replaced.

The man with the red toolbox opened the drawer eventually. His wife found the phone, which is how these things usually end when a man won't end them himself. There were two years of wreckage. And then, slowly, there was a marriage with no drawers in it, which he told me was the first time in his adult life he'd ever felt clean. "I spent twenty years keeping that garage spotless," he said. "It was the drawer that was killing me."

Open the drawer, let a Nathan in, and say the five words.

## Before you turn the page

You've probably found your shape by now, and it isn't a comfortable thing to find. I'd ask you to remember that David found his only when a man walked into the room and said four words, and that the five words David said back restored his standing in a single breath. The Man you're following had no drawer at all, and He isn't asking you to have a cleaner garage; He's asking you to let a Nathan in. May the Father give you a brother who's willing to say "you are the man," and the plain honesty to say "I have sinned" without a "but," and may you find that a man with nothing to defend is free.
