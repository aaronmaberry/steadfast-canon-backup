# 11. When Home Never Handed You Off

Micah asked me a question at his grandfather's funeral that I've never forgotten. He was twenty-six, standing at the back of a fellowship hall with a paper plate of somebody's casserole, watching the older men from the church talk in a loose circle by the coffee.

"When did you know you were a man?"

I asked him what he meant. He said he honestly couldn't tell me when it had happened for him, or whether it had happened at all. He had a job, he paid his rent, and he'd voted in two presidential elections, and he still felt, standing in that fellowship hall, like a kid who had wandered into the men's section and was waiting for somebody to notice and either send him out or tell him he could stay.

"Nobody ever said it," he told me. "Nobody ever said, 'You're a man now.' I think I've been waiting for somebody to say it."

He isn't unusual; he's only more honest than most, because most men in this country were never handed off. They went from boy to man the way a river crosses from one county into the next: at some point they were across the line, and there was no sign, and nobody was standing there to say so.

## What a handoff is

For most of human history, boys didn't become men by accident. They became men because men made them men, on purpose, at a certain point, with words and a weight and a welcome. The details changed from one people to the next, but the shape was the same everywhere. The older men took the boy from the women, put him through something, told him what a man was, gave him a man's responsibility, and brought him into their company. He went to sleep a boy and woke up a man, not because his body had changed overnight but because the men had said so and meant it.

The Bible does this too. It isn't just something anthropologists write about. Watch David, dying, hand off to Solomon.

:::margin
1 Kings 2:2-3 (NIV)
"'I am about to go the way of all the earth,' he said. 'So be strong, act like a man, and observe what the LORD your God requires: Walk in obedience to him, and keep his decrees and commands, his laws and regulations, as written in the Law of Moses. Do this so that you may prosper in all you do and wherever you go'"
:::

Look at what's in that passage. A man, older, about to leave. A word: act like a man, and here is what that means. A weight: a kingdom, and God's law to keep inside it. Moses did the same thing with Joshua in front of the whole people. Paul did it with Timothy, whose own father barely rates a mention in Scripture, and he did it with his hands.

:::margin
2 Timothy 1:6 (NIV)
"For this reason I remind you to fan into flame the gift of God, which is in you through the laying on of my hands."
:::

Through the laying on of my hands. Paul had put his hands on the young man and said, in effect, "You. You're one of us. Here's your work." And years later, when Timothy was frightened and tired and thinking about quitting, Paul reminded him of the moment. Remember when I handed you off. Fan it back into flame.

That's what a handoff is, and it has four things in it: a man, a word, a weight, and a welcome. Most of you got none of them. Or you got the weight without the word, from a father who piled responsibility on you at twelve and never once told you that you were doing a man's job. Or you got words without weight, from a dad who said "you're the man of the house now" on his way out the door and never came back to see whether you were.

## The counterfeits

A boy who isn't handed off doesn't stop wanting to be. He goes looking for it somewhere else, and the world has counterfeits ready for him.

The first is sex. For a lot of boys, the first time is the closest thing to a rite of passage they'll ever get, and they treat it that way, and it doesn't work, because there's no older man in the room and no word and no weight. There's only a girl who didn't sign up to make him a man and couldn't have if she'd wanted to.

The second is a paycheck. A job feels like manhood, and it's part of manhood, but a boy with a paycheck is still a boy. Any foreman can tell you that.

The third is a fight, or a truck, or a drink, or turning eighteen or twenty-one. Every one of them is a boy trying to hand himself off, and the trouble is that you can't hand yourself off. That's the whole point of the thing. A handoff is something a man receives from men.

The fourth counterfeit is the quietest, and it's the one Micah was living inside, and it's simply waiting. A man who was never handed off can spend his entire adult life waiting for someone to tell him he's allowed in the circle by the coffee. He defers to men his own age. He shrinks around older men. He's forty-five and still feels like he's auditioning for a room he has been standing in for twenty years.

And here's what that costs. A man who doesn't know he's a man doesn't take a man's weight, and why would he, when he's still waiting for someone to tell him it's his? He doesn't go first, and he doesn't reject passivity, and he can't, really, because underneath everything he's still a boy waiting for permission. Every one of the four marks depends on this. You can't act like a man for very long if nobody, including you, has ever said that you are one.

## Jacob's night

There's a man in Genesis who spent his whole life trying to get a handoff through the wrong door.

Jacob wanted his father's blessing so badly that he stole it. He dressed up as his brother, lied to a blind old man, and got the words. And it didn't work. He spent the next twenty years running and scheming, working for a man who cheated him, still trying to get somewhere. The stolen blessing didn't make him a man. It made him a fugitive.

Then one night, alone, on the road home to face everything he'd run from, a Man came and wrestled him until dawn. And Jacob did the one thing he had never done in his life. He stopped scheming and simply held on.

:::margin
Genesis 32:26 (NIV)
"Then the man said, 'Let me go, for it is daybreak.' But Jacob replied, 'I will not let you go unless you bless me.'"
:::

I will not let you go unless you bless me. That's a boy who has finally stopped stealing what he needs and started asking for it, from the only One who could actually give it. And he got it. He got a new name, Israel, which meant he wasn't Jacob the grabber anymore. He got a limp, which meant he'd never walk like a boy again. And he walked toward his brother the next morning like a man.

The handoff Jacob had spent his life trying to get from his father, he finally received from God, on the ground, in the dark, somewhere in his forties. If you're reading this and you're somewhere in your forties and it's dark, I want you to hear that. It is not too late for you to be handed off.

## How a man receives it now

So how does a grown man get what should have been given to him at fourteen? Here's what I've watched work.

Hear it from the Father first. We said in the last chapter that the Father speaks identity before performance, and He has already said the words over you. "This is my son, whom I love." If you're in Christ, that isn't a hope; it's a settled fact. The handoff you never got from your dad, you have from your Father, and it outranks anything a man could say to you. Start there or nothing else will hold.

Then ask an older man to say it. This is going to feel like the most awkward thing in this book, and I'm asking you to do it anyway. Find a man older than you who's living the four marks, and tell him the truth: nobody ever told me I was a man, and would you? And then let him do it. Let him put a hand on your shoulder and say it. Chapter 14 is about finding that man. You'd be surprised how many older men have spent their whole lives waiting for a younger man to ask them for something real.

Be welcomed by brothers. A handoff has a welcome in it, and you need a circle of men who know you and count you as one of them. Not a crowd; a few. Micah's entire question at that funeral was really "am I allowed in the circle," and the honest answer is that the circle should have pulled him in years before, and if it didn't, he needed to go find one that would. Chapter 13 is about that.

Take the weight before you feel ready. There's something in the David and Solomon handoff that I missed for years. David says "act like a man," and it's a command. Solomon didn't feel like a man; he was young and scared and said so to God a chapter later. David told him to act like one anyway, and the acting was part of how he became one. Some of you are waiting to feel handed off before you pick up the weight, and I'd ask you to reverse the order. Pick up the weight and carry it for a year. The men around you will begin treating you like a man because you're doing a man's work, and one day you'll notice that the feeling has caught up.

And mark it by writing down the date: the day you decided, the day the older man said it, the day you asked God on the ground in the dark. Men need markers, and Jacob got a limp; you may have to settle for a date on the inside cover of your Bible. But have something you can point to and say, that's when. Not because the date is magic, but because a man who can't point to when he became a man will always half-wonder whether he did.

## When the man in the chair won't hand you off

Cole's father is alive and present and has never once, in thirty-three years, told his son he did a good job. Cole rebuilt a transmission at seventeen, and his father found the one bolt he'd torqued wrong. Cole got a promotion at twenty-nine, and his father asked why it had taken so long. Cole is still trying; he calls every Sunday and fixes things at the old man's place, and he's waiting for a sentence that isn't coming.

If that's you, I have to tell you something hard. Some fathers will never hand you off, not because you haven't earned it but because they never received it themselves and don't know how, or because keeping you a boy is the only way they know to stay a man. You can honor that father, and you should, and chapter 12 is about how. But you cannot wait on him. Every year you spend waiting for his sentence is a year you don't spend being the man God has already said you are.

Cole finally heard it from a seventy-year-old deacon named Hollis who had watched him serve for six years and one Sunday pulled him aside by his truck and said, "Son, I've been watching you. You're a good man. I'd trust you with anything I've got." Cole sat in the cab of his truck and cried for twenty minutes. He was thirty-three years old, and it was the first time.

That was a handoff, and it didn't come from the chair. It came from a man who was willing to stand in for the chair, and God arranges men like that for those who ask.

## The other side of this

If you're the older man reading this, I need you to hear the rest, because you're the other half of the problem and the other half of the solution.

You probably never got handed off either. Most men your age didn't. And you may be thinking that means you can't give what you didn't get, and that's wrong. You've lived the four marks, or enough of them to know what they look like. You have watched younger men and thought, that one's got it, and you never said so. Saying so is the whole job.

Roy began doing this at his Thursday table almost by accident. One of the men in his thirties told him about a hard call he'd made at work, standing up to a boss over something dishonest, and Roy said, "That's what a man does. You did right." The young man didn't say anything. But he came back the next week early, and the week after that, and a year later he told Roy that sentence was the first time any man over fifty had ever told him he'd done right about anything.

You don't need a ceremony for this. Chapter 29 is about ceremonies, and they're good, and I'll tell you how to hold one. But most of the handoffs in Scripture were a man, a word, a hand on a shoulder, and a "go." You can do that at a truck window this week.

## Handed off in every season

If you're divorced, the divorce may have un-manned you in your own eyes. You had a handoff of a kind, a wedding and a house and a role, and it came apart, and now you're not sure what you are. Hear this, then: the Father's word over you didn't get revoked in a courtroom. You're still His son, and you're still a man, and the weight you're carrying now, children across two houses and a life rebuilt from a one-bedroom apartment, is a man's weight, and you're carrying it. Let someone older say so.

If you're a young father, you're about to be the one who hands off, and you'll be tempted to think you can't because you never got it. You can, and Part 4 will show you how, and you'll find that giving the handoff heals something in the giving.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A young man of eighteen is the one the handoff was designed for, and he's the one least likely to get it. Eli, who works the parts counter, was handed off by accident: an older man on the church softball team watched him handle a bad call from an umpire without losing his head, and said afterward, "That's how a man does it," and Eli told me he thought about that sentence for a month. The takeaway for him is that the handoff can come in pieces, from any man who's paying attention, and that he should go find men who pay attention.

A man of forty-four is Cole, waiting on a father who will never say it, and the takeaway for him is the deacon by the truck: the handoff can come from a man who stands in for the chair, and God arranges those men for those who ask.

## Micah, five years on

Micah is thirty-one now. A man named Dale, twenty years older, took him to breakfast every other Tuesday for two years and one morning told him, plainly, that he was a man and that it was time to stop waiting for permission to act like one. Micah wrote the date inside his Bible. He got married last spring. And at his own wedding, at the back of a different fellowship hall, he told me he'd caught himself looking around at the older men by the coffee and realizing, for the first time, that he wasn't waiting to be let in.

He was one of them. You're allowed in the circle, and you always were, because the Father already said so. Now go find a man who'll say it out loud, and then go be a man who does.

## Before you turn the page

If nobody ever told you that you were a man, I'd like to be one voice that does, though I know a page isn't a hand on the shoulder. You've carried weight and gone first and kept getting up, and that's what a man does, and the Father who spoke over His Son at the Jordan has spoken the same words over you. Go find a man who'll say it out loud, and then go be a man who says it. May God give you the awkward courage to ask, and the plain courage to tell, and may you write a date inside your Bible this year that you can point to for the rest of your life.
