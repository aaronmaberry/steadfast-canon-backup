# 5. Pick Up the Weight

There's a wall in your house you can't take out. It's painted the same color as the others, and it has the same nail holes from the years the school pictures hung there. But the joists overhead end on that wall, and every bed and dresser and sleeping kid upstairs is resting on it, whether anyone thinks about it or not. A contractor can find it in about a minute. He knocks, looks up, follows the direction of the floor above, and tells you which wall is holding up the second floor.

You can still open up the kitchen. You just have to put a new beam under that weight first. Take the wall out with nothing to replace it and the upstairs floor starts to sag. A year later, a bedroom door won't latch.

Every family, every job, and every life has weight that has to land somewhere. Whether that's fair doesn't change where it lands.

I've argued with where it lands. In my own kitchen I've slid a weight onto someone else with a tidy sentence and called it being honest. The second mark says a real man accepts responsibility. He finds the part of the weight that belongs to him and says, "That lands on me." He may know he didn't cause all of it. He may know the arrangement isn't fair. He says it anyway, because in that house he's the wall. When he steps aside, everyone upstairs feels it.

## Not mine

After the fall, the first words a man said to God became a pattern for the rest of us. God asks Adam what happened, and Adam points. The woman You put here with me. She gave me the fruit. One breath, two accusations. His wife gets one and God gets the other, and Adam keeps none for himself. I've spoken that same grammar in more than one room. Cain picked it up whole. God asked him where his brother was, and Cain answered with a question.

:::margin
Genesis 4:9 (NIV)
"Then the LORD said to Cain, 'Where is your brother Abel?' 'I don't know,' he replied. 'Am I my brother's keeper?'"
:::

The answer is yes, and it reaches a long way. It reaches the man who says the department isn't his, the kid isn't his, she agreed to this, and nobody ever told him.

Both men were protecting the same thing, a story where the weight belonged to somebody else. That story is called blame. It makes the afternoon easier.

Good men get stuck here because they mix up fault and responsibility. Fault asks who caused this. Responsibility asks who owns it now. A layoff, a diagnosis, a daughter's anxiety, a father who left you nothing but his debts, none of those may be your fault. Your name can still be on every one of them. Fairness belongs in a courtroom. A wall doesn't get to use it.

## Five things with your name on them

The list of what's yours is short. The weight that isn't yours can wait. These five can't.

Your own life comes first. Your body, your money, your word, your habits, your walk with God. Nobody is going to book your doctor's appointment, pay down your credit card, or open your Bible for you. Those moved out of your mother's hands a long time ago.

Second is your house, if you share one. A marriage has a temperature, and you answer for whether it's warming or cooling. The children under your roof are being formed, and the forming is yours. Teachers help, and a church helps. But the voice your boy grows into will sound like whoever actually lived with him.

Third is the work in front of you today. Do it the way you would if the owner were standing at your elbow, because One is.

Fourth come the people tied to you. A brother in trouble. The man at church whose wife walked out this month. The boy down the street whose father is only a rumor. Am I my brother's keeper? Yes. Your job is keeper. Only one Man gets the title Savior.

Fifth is the weight men drop first, and it's the heaviest. Your own wrong. What was done to you belongs on a different page. This page is for the sentence you said at dinner that you can't take back, the years your body was home and you weren't, and the thing she still hasn't been told. Set that down and it doesn't get lighter. It moves onto everyone else in the house, and it grows.

The seasons change what's in the pack. They don't change whose name is on it. A man living alone is mostly carrying himself, and that's heavier than he thinks, because an empty apartment never reports the hour he quit. A new husband finds out that somebody else's peace now rests on his back. A newborn means he's also carrying a wife who can't sleep. A teenage son behind the wheel means a father has to carry his own temper. Divorce doesn't end a man's share. His kids live in two houses now, and he's still holding up his part of both. Grown kids mostly leave a man with the fifth weight, the wrongs from when they were small. He can still pick that one up. The tool is a phone.

## Two words for weight

Paul writes two commands a few lines apart, and at first they sound like they collide.

:::margin
Galatians 6:2 (NIV)
"Carry each other's burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ."
:::

:::margin
Galatians 6:5 (NIV)
"for each one should carry their own load."
:::

Carry theirs. Carry yours. Paul can say both because he uses two different words. The first means a crushing weight, more than one back can hold. The second means a soldier's pack, the gear issued to you and sized for your shoulders. When Jesus said His burden is light, He used that second word.

Once you can tell the two apart, you're practicing the mark. Your own pack goes on without any announcement. When a brother is flattened by the crushing kind, you get under it with him, whether he asked or not. The mark takes both.

## The weight that isn't yours

Pride will load a man up until he staggers, because an empty hand feels like failure to him. He takes on all of her happiness. Every choice his grown kids make. The health of the whole church. He calls the overload devotion.

Moses reached that point. He was in a desert with a whole nation complaining about the food, and he said something to God that pride usually keeps a man from saying.

:::margin
Numbers 11:14 (NIV)
"I cannot carry all these people by myself; the burden is too heavy for me."
:::

God answered by sending seventy elders to share the weight, so that Moses wouldn't carry it alone. God leaves certain weights off your list on purpose, and grabbing them anyway is pride. Your wife's joy belongs to her and to God. Your grown son's choices belong to him. Carry what has your name on it. For the rest, ask where your seventy are.

## Providing without disappearing

One verse about providing is famous, and a lot of men were told it meant a paycheck and nothing more.

:::margin
1 Timothy 5:8 (NIV)
"Anyone who does not provide for their relatives, and especially for their own household, has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever."
:::

Household is the word that matters. It means everyone under your roof. Money is a thin slice of what a father owes his kids. A man can pour money into a house he's hardly ever in. He can pay for years of dance lessons while the folding chair at his daughter's recital sits empty, and the empty chair cancels out the lessons.

Food on the table is the bottom layer of providing, and it's only the bottom layer. Being there is part of what provision means. Trade your presence for a bigger paycheck and you've bought an expensive absence.

A man who holds up a household owes it a floor with no give in it. When a teenager shoves, he's testing whether the floor will hold. When a wife says something hard, she's counting on a man who'll stay put while she says it. You can feel weak and still stay. When the voices get louder, stay again. Nobody in the house thinks about the beam over their heads. They cross the floor without testing it. That's how you know the beam is doing its job.

## How to pick it up

Look at the person involved and say, "That's mine." Leave the polished version alone, the one that starts with "I can see how you might feel." Just say, "That's mine."

Then stop at the period. The minute you add "but," you've started a second speech, and everyone in the room hears the turn. Adam's excuse came out in the same breath as his answer. Say the words and stop.

Own the results. Rain coming through the roof is a result. The hours you meant to spend on the roof are a story. Tell your kids you tried to make it home for dinner and you've described a plan. They'll describe dinner without you.

Pack first, then the crushing weight. The order matters. Running around helping everybody else while your own pack sits in the driveway is a way of hiding, and home is where it shows.

Then ask. Name the weight God never put on your list, and hand it back to Him. Let two or three men take the part that's flattening you. You're still responsible. Handing off the crushing part may be the most responsible hour you spend all year.

## The one who took it up

The language of carrying was used about another Man before it was ever used about us.

:::margin
Isaiah 53:4 (NIV)
"Surely he took up our pain and bore our suffering, yet we considered him punished by God, stricken by him, and afflicted."
:::

Took up. Bore. He hadn't earned that weight, and the wreck wasn't His doing. He never once reached for Adam's excuse. On the cross He said, in effect, "That's mine," and He was the one Man who could have refused it and been right.

Joy was set in front of Him while He carried it. The fourth mark is already alive inside the second one. The Father sees the beam nobody upstairs ever thinks about, and the Father is the one who pays. That's why you carry it.

## Before you turn the page

If you feel a weight settling on you as you read, that's what it feels like to be the wall. You were built for it. God sizes a man for the pack he's been given, and He puts seventy more around him for the rest. Hold up the corner with your name on it. When it gets heavy, keep your hand from pointing. May He set you under your share, keep you honest about the share He gave to someone else, and teach your house that the floor will hold.
