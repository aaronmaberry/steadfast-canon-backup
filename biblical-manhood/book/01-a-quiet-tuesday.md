# 1. A Quiet Tuesday

If you'd asked me at twenty-five which days would decide the man I became, I'd have given you the big ones. The wedding. The morning our first child came. A funeral. The job I took and the job I turned down. The Saturday a pastor put me under in a river and I came up sure.

I've had those days now. They're stones on a riverbank, and I can point at each one and say, that was the year. The deciding happened somewhere else, in a long run of days I couldn't tell apart if you paid me.

A man's life is decided on Tuesdays. On Sunday he knows how to stand. On Saturday he can take his son somewhere and feel like a father for an afternoon. Tuesday is the day in the middle with nothing special on it, the day nobody writes down. He comes home tired to a house that's already loud. There's a sentence he could say or swallow. There's a call he could return or let die. Down the hall a smoke detector chirps for a new battery, and he can change it tonight or walk under it for one more week. That's where the marks get made.

## What a Tuesday looks like

So here's one.

A man I'll call Anthony left the house at 6:10 on a Tuesday in October. He'd slept badly. The night before, his wife had said something about money that wasn't quite fair, and he'd let it sit. It rode in the truck with him.

At the shop, a customer's truck was promised by lunch. The parts ticket was still on the bench where he'd set it the day before, meaning to call it in. He hadn't. The fuel pump wasn't coming. He had to push the job back while the shop lead listened from two bays over and said nothing. At lunch he sat in his cab with his phone for forty minutes. Afterward he couldn't have named one thing he'd read.

His father called that afternoon. The man calls maybe twice a year, and his name on the screen still makes Anthony feel nine years old for a second. He let it go to voicemail and told himself he'd call back later.

He finished the work. He drove home with the radio off, pulled into the garage, and stood in the doorway to the kitchen the way he does most nights. Then he walked in. He kissed his wife on the side of the head without looking at her. He ate. He got the kids down. By 8:40 he was in the recliner with the game on and the phone in his hand. She was in the kitchen. The money stayed where he'd left it.

The money he let sit was a decision. So was the part he never ordered, and the call he let go to voicemail, and the phone at 8:40 while his wife stood alone in the kitchen. None of them felt like decisions. They felt like a day. Give a man ten thousand days like that and he becomes a particular kind of man without ever standing up and choosing it.

Anthony is a good man. He didn't set out to hurt anybody that day. He worked, he provided, he came home, and he was gentle enough. He's also becoming something, one ordinary day at a time. Ask his wife what, and she could tell you.

## Why the big days can't do it

I've stopped trusting the big days to make men. A big day gathers up what you feel and what you hope and sets it on a table in front of witnesses. God builds days like that on purpose, and later in this book I'll tell you to build one for your son. But a big day can't give a man a habit. A mark is what repeated work leaves behind, the way a callus comes from a thousand mornings nobody photographed.

Men come home from a conference on fire and are back in the recliner inside two weeks. The fire was real. They just never did the ordinary thing the next morning, and the morning after that.

On the big days, people are watching. At a wedding or a baptism or a funeral, you know your part, and most men play it well. What tells you about a man is what he does when the room is empty, when nobody would know if he made the call or let it ring, and nobody would see whether the drawer stayed shut. Tuesday is when nobody is watching. That's why it counts.

## What God does with ordinary days

If you've been in church a while, you may have filed the spiritual life under retreats and altar calls. God does meet men there. But look at how the Bible talks about the way a man gets formed.

:::margin
Luke 16:10 (NIV)
"Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with very little will also be dishonest with much."
:::

"Very little" is a Tuesday phrase. The man who can be trusted with the small things nobody sees is the man who can be trusted when the room fills up. The parts ticket, the voicemail, the recliner, the kiss that doesn't look her in the face. That's where the man who will someday have to stand up in a hard room either gets built or doesn't.

When Jesus told the story of the house that held, notice what He said the builder did.

:::margin
Matthew 7:24 (NIV)
"Therefore everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man who built his house on the rock."
:::

Puts them into practice. Practice is what you do over and over until it's how you do things. The wise man dug down and laid his foundation on days when nobody drove by to watch. When the storm came, the house held because of those days.

Most of a man's walk with God happens on plain days. So does most of his marriage, and most of his fathering. If you want to know what you're becoming, leave the retreat out of it. Look at last Tuesday.

## The other Tuesday

Here's Anthony's day again, a year later. I'm telling it twice because a man has more say over a day like this than he thinks.

He left at 6:10 again. He'd slept badly again. Somewhere on the drive he said out loud, "Father, I don't want to haul this around all day." He pulled into a gas station and texted his wife. He was sorry he'd gone quiet the night before, and he wanted ten minutes with her after the kids were down. It took ninety seconds. He was still tired, but the thing wasn't riding with him anymore.

The part for the lunch job was on the shelf, because he'd called it in the day before. When a different job went sideways anyway, he told the shop lead the truth and called the customer before anybody had to overhear him scrambling. His father called. Anthony felt nine for a second. Then he answered. They talked for six awkward minutes about nothing much. It was the first time in two years he hadn't let the call die in voicemail.

At home he caught himself standing in the doorway, and he walked on through. He put his phone face down on the counter. He looked at his wife when he kissed her. After the kids were down, the two of them sat at the counter for ten minutes and talked about the money. He wanted to win a point, and he let it go. He did the dishes. He prayed with his hand on her back, badly and briefly. Then he slept.

His daughter is seven. She watches his face when her mother walks into a room. She couldn't tell you what changed, but something had, in about six places, and none of them took more than two minutes. Give that man ten thousand days like that one, and a different little girl grows up learning what a husband's face does when his wife walks in.

That's what this book is about. Learn what a man does on a plain day. Then do it again.

## The same day at every age

An ordinary day doesn't look the same at nineteen as it does at seventy-two, but it decides the same kind of thing. See if you can find yourself in one of these four men before we go on.

Eli is nineteen. He works the counter at a parts store and shares an apartment with two roommates. Around ten at night the place goes quiet and the phone is in his hand. What he does in that pocket of quiet is writing the man he'll be at thirty. He told me once he was waiting for his real life to start. His real life starts at ten o'clock every night.

Tomás is twenty-nine. You'll see him again. The baby doesn't sleep. At two in the morning he hears her crying and feels his wife start to move, and lying still feels like the only sane choice a tired man has. Whether he gets up is the marriage, far more than the anniversary trip they can't afford.

Walt is fifty-five. He built a business from one truck to eleven. At 6:15 he can leave the shop and go home to dinner, or he can stay another hour because the shop is quieter than the house. He stayed for twenty years, an hour here and an hour there. He never once decided to. He just kept doing it.

Roy is seventy-two. He turned in a state trooper's badge after thirty-one years. At 5:30 the alarm goes off, and there's nothing for him to pin on. Nobody would notice if he slept until nine. What he does at 5:30 decides whether the last stretch of his life is a long exit or the most useful years he's had. He gets up and makes the coffee. You'll see why that matters.

Four men, and four hours that don't feel like decisions. That's why the book starts here.

## What comes next

Soon you'll get a sentence with four marks in it. First, though, a word for the man who suspects that everything he's been told about being a man is thin or contradicts itself. The next chapter is for him.

## Before you turn the page

I don't know which of those four men you are, or whether you're somewhere in between. I do know your week has a Tuesday in it, and that day has a moment, probably several, where you'll choose without feeling the choice. I'm not asking you to be a different man by Sunday. I'm asking you to notice the moment once, and to do one small thing in it on purpose. The Father who made you sees that moment, and He's glad when you meet it. May He give you eyes for a plain day, and the small strength it takes to walk through a doorway instead of standing in it.
