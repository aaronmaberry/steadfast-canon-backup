# 29. Give Your Son a Day He Will Not Forget

Roy's grandson turned thirteen in October, and on a Saturday two weeks later four men picked him up before dawn.

He knew something was coming. His father had told him to be dressed and ready at 5:30 and hadn't said why. Roy drove, and Hollis sat in the back with the boy's father and an uncle who had come in from Amarillo the night before, and nobody said much. They drove out past town to a stretch of pasture where Roy and the boy had rebuilt a fence line together over the summer, and they parked, and they walked the fence to the far corner post as the sky started to go gray, and they stood there.

Roy read Genesis 2:15 out loud. To work it and take care of it. Then each man spoke. The uncle told the boy about the worst mistake he'd ever made and what a man does afterward. Hollis told him about the caprock and the calf. His father told him the three names, the ones he'd been saying weekly for years, but this time he said them in front of other men with his hand on the boy's shoulder, and then he said the fourth one. "You're becoming a man. That's what today is. And here's what that means." And he said the sentence. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. He handed the boy an index card with it written in his own hand.

Then Roy reached into his coat and pulled out a brass plumb bob, about four inches long and heavy for its size, with the boy's name and the date engraved on it, and handed it to him on its string. And he told him what it was for.

The boy has carried it every day since. His father says he's seen him take it out of his pocket in the school parking lot and simply hold it.

That's what this chapter is about. Chapter 11 told you most men never got handed off, and spent their lives waiting for a sentence that never came. Don't let your son wait. Give him a day. Not a party, but a marker, with men, and a word, and a weight, and a welcome. A day he can point to for the rest of his life and say: that's when.

## Why a day

God builds markers into His people, and He always has. When Israel crossed the Jordan into the land, Joshua had twelve men carry twelve stones out of the riverbed and stack them on the bank, and he told them why.

:::margin
Joshua 4:6-7 (NIV)
"to serve as a sign among you. In the future, when your children ask you, 'What do these stones mean?' tell them that the flow of the Jordan was cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD. When it crossed the Jordan, the waters of the Jordan were cut off. These stones are to be a memorial to the people of Israel forever."
:::

When your children ask you what these stones mean. God wanted a pile of rocks on a riverbank so that a boy fifty years later would ask his father a question and get a story. Jacob set up a stone at Bethel. Samuel raised one after a battle and named it. Every Passover, a father answered a child's question about why that night was different. Jesus stood in a river and heard His Father's voice out loud in front of witnesses before He started His work. Markers, days, stones a man can point to.

Some traditions still do this for boys. Most American Christian families don't. We have birthdays, which mark time, and graduations, which mark school, and we have no day at all that marks the thing that matters most: the day a boy's father and the men around him say, on purpose and out loud, you are a man now, and here is what that means, and you're one of us.

So the boy guesses. He picks his own marker from whatever is available: the first time with a girl, the first drunk, the first fight, the license, the truck. Chapter 11 called those counterfeits, and they are, and the reason he reaches for them is that nobody gave him the real one. Give him the real one. Give him a stone he can point to.

## The design

I'm going to lay out the shape of a day. It isn't the only shape. It's one that works, that I've watched work, and that you can bend to your own family. Don't make it more complicated than this, and don't make it less.

The when is somewhere between twelve and fourteen, as he's starting to cross. You'll know it: his voice, his shoulders, the way he has begun to watch men. If you missed that window, do it anyway, because seventeen is fine and twenty-five is fine. A man of forty who never got a day can be given one by his father, and it will still do something, and I've seen it.

The who is the father and two to four men the boy knows and respects and who will be in his life going forward: a grandfather, an uncle, a coach who's a real man, your brother from the twenty minutes, a man from church who has watched him grow. Not a crowd, because a crowd makes it a performance. Four or five men who'll still be around in ten years to say "remember the fence line." If you have no men, this is why chapters 13 and 14 came first, and you have a year to go get them.

The where is outside, early, somewhere with weight: a hill he has climbed, a fence you built together, the stock tank at first light, the deer lease, the church before anyone else arrives. Not a restaurant, and not a living room. The place should ask something of him just to get there, even if it's only a walk in the cold and dark.

Before the day, he should know something's coming but not what. The father meets with the men beforehand, once, and asks each one to prepare one true thing: a story about what a man is from their own life, one failure and what they did afterward, and one charge for the boy. Five minutes each and not more. The father prepares the three names and the fourth word. If you've been writing the birthday letters from chapter 27, this is when the box gets handed over.

And the shape of the morning goes like this. You arrive by going somewhere. Up something, along something. A small hill to echo all the hills you've given him, walked in quiet, so that the weight of it settles on him.

One man reads the Word. Genesis 2:15 for the work and the keeping. Joshua 1:9 for courage. 1 Kings 2:2-3 for the handoff, David to Solomon. Or this, which I've heard read at more of these mornings than any other verse.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 16:13 (NIV)
"Be on your guard; stand firm in the faith; be courageous; be strong."
:::

Four commands and four marks in a single breath, and it should be read slowly. The men speak, each in turn. The story, the failure, the charge. The boy stands there and takes it. He'll remember every word for the rest of his life; men have told me at sixty what a man said to them at thirteen on a morning like this.

The father gives the four marks. He says the sentence, the locked one, and walks through each mark in a sentence or two, and hands the boy the card in his own handwriting. He tells him what each mark looks like on a Tuesday. He tells him which one he himself is weakest at. He tells him they'll be working on them together.

The father gives the name. He puts his hand on his son's shoulder and gives the three names in front of the men, mine and what I see in you, and then the fourth: you are becoming a man. He says what that means. He says that he'll walk him across. If you've never said it before, you'll probably cry. Let them see it. A boy who watches his father cry while naming him learns something about men that no dry ceremony could teach.

Then comes the token. Give him something he'll carry for sixty years, not expensive but weighted: a knife, a Bible with the date inside, his great-grandfather's watch, a tool. Roy chose a plumb bob, and I'll tell you why in a moment, but the point is that it's a physical thing he can hold in a parking lot at fifteen when he has forgotten everything else about the morning. The stones on the Jordan were only stones. Their whole job was to be there when he needed to remember.

Then comes the prayer. Every man puts a hand on him and every man prays, short and out loud. The father prays last, and asks the Father who does not leave to be this boy's Father in every place his earthly one can't reach.

And then the welcome. Somebody says it plainly: "You're one of us now." Then you go eat, and at breakfast, for the first time, the men talk to him like a man. They ask his opinion, and they tell him things, and he notices.

## The plumb bob

Roy chose the token because of chapter 17, and here is roughly what he told his grandson at the corner post with the sun coming up. I asked him afterward to say it again so that I'd get it right.

"This is a plumb bob. Your great-grandfather used one like it to build the barn that's still standing. You hang it on this string next to whatever you're building, and you wait for it to stop swinging, and when it settles it points straight down. Every time. It doesn't care what the wall looks like. It doesn't care what you think is straight. Gravity doesn't have opinions.

"You're going to build a lot of things in your life. Some of them are going to be crooked, and you won't be able to tell, because everything around them is crooked too. When that happens, you hang this and you wait. God's Word is your plumb line. It doesn't move. When you can't tell what's straight anymore, you hold your life up next to it and let it settle and see where you're off.

"And here's the other thing. This is heavy for its size, and that's on purpose, because a light bob swings forever. A heavy one settles fast. A man's supposed to be like that, steady and weighted and hard to knock off true. Your name's on this so you remember who you're supposed to be when you're not sure."

The boy's father told me his son has put that plumb bob next to more than one decision already. Whether to go along with something at school. Whether to tell the truth about a grade. He takes it out, holds it, and watches it settle. It's a piece of brass. It's also the whole book, in his pocket.

Choose your own token. But choose one that says something, and say the something out loud when you hand it over.

## The day after

Now the part that matters more than the day, and it's the reason this chapter sits in a book with a Tuesday spine.

The ceremony changes nothing by itself. I've watched fathers put everything into the morning and then go back on Monday to treating their son like a boy, and the son learns that the day was a nice thing his dad said once, like a birthday card. What makes the day real is that starting Tuesday, the men treat him differently. He's given more hill. He's asked his opinion at the table and it's actually weighed. He's handed a man's jobs. He's invited, eventually, into the twenty minutes with his father and a brother, and he's expected to answer "what's heavy" like a man. He's corrected like a man, privately and with respect. He's trusted with things, and when he fails he's held to a man's standard and given a man's grace.

That's what a handoff is. The day is the marker. The Tuesdays are the handoff. If you give him the day and not the Tuesdays, you've given him a stone with no story.

And there's another day-after I need to say something about, because this book has men in it who have turned in an actual badge.

Roy retired from thirty-one years of wearing one, and he told me the day of the retirement ceremony was the best day of his career and the Tuesday after it was the worst day of his life. Because the ceremony said "well done" and then Tuesday said nothing at all, because nobody needed him and there was no assignment. He sat by the phone like a rookie, as he put it, until he got up and went to find the work.

Every man who has had a marker knows something about this. The wedding is a marker; the Tuesday after is the marriage. Baptism is a marker; the Tuesday after is the walk. The retreat where you finally said yes to God is a marker; the Tuesday after is when you find out whether you meant it. Markers matter, and God builds them in. But they're stones on a riverbank, not the land. The land is Tuesday. The land is always Tuesday. Roy knew that when he handed his grandson the plumb bob, and that's why the first thing he did the following week was call the boy and ask him to come help rebuild the north gate. The day said "you're a man." Tuesday made him one.

## If you never had a day

Some of you are reading this with a stone in your throat because nobody did this for you, and you're not sure you can give what you didn't get. Hear me on this: you can, and giving it will heal something in you that receiving it would have.

I've watched fathers who were never named stand at a corner post and name their sons, and break down halfway through, and finish anyway, and drive home different men, which is what chapter 11 promised. The handoff you give your son hands something off to you too. You get to say the sentence you never heard, and hearing it in your own voice does something the Father has been waiting to do in you for forty years.

If you want it to be complete, do this: ask one of the older men at your son's day to say something to you afterward, privately. Let him put a hand on your shoulder and tell you that you're a man and you did right by your boy. It will take him thirty seconds. It isn't too late for you either.

## The day in every season

If you're divorced, you can still do this, and you should. Coordinate with his mother respectfully; tell her what it is and why, and let her know it's about the boy and not about the two of you. If there's a stepfather in his life who's a good man, consider inviting him. I know how hard that is, and it's also one of the most honorable things a divorced father can do, and your son will never forget that you did it. If his mother resists, do it on your time, with your men, and keep the tone clean. Kenny gave his son a day at fourteen at a cabin on Lake Buchanan with three men from his church and his own father. His ex-wife wasn't sure about it beforehand. Afterward the boy came home, and she told Kenny, "Whatever you did, do more of it."

If you're a single man, you may be one of the men at somebody else's day, and you should be ready for it. Have your one true thing. And there may be a fatherless boy in your life whose mother would weep with gratitude if you and three other men gave her son a day, so ask her, and do it with her blessing and in the light. You may give a boy a day he'll never forget before you have a son of your own.

If you're an older man, grandfathers often lead these because fathers aren't sure how, and you're the one who should offer. You have the standing. You have the token; there's something in a drawer of yours right now that a boy would carry for sixty years. And you have the time to make the Tuesdays after mean something. Roy's day for his grandson was Roy's idea. His son told me he'd wanted to do it for two years and hadn't known where to start, and his father said, "I do. Let me help."

If your son is an infant, the day is thirteen years off, and the letters start now, and so do the Tuesdays. A boy who has been named weekly and given hills yearly walks up to his day already knowing most of what he'll hear. The day just makes it official.

And a word for fathers of daughters. She needs a day too. It's a different shape: her mother and grandmother should be there, it's often a dinner rather than a dawn, the token is often a ring or a necklace, and the word is about who she is and what she's worth and what she should expect from a man. But the bones are the same: a marker, people who love her, a word spoken on purpose, a weight she can hold. Don't give your son a day and leave your daughter guessing.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A boy of thirteen is the one the day was designed for, and Roy's grandson is standing at the corner post with a plumb bob in his hand. The takeaway for the father of a boy that age is that the window is now, that the men need to be asked a year out, and that the Tuesdays after are what make the day real.

A man of forty-five who never got a day can still give one, and Kenny's cabin at Lake Buchanan with three men from church and his own father is the picture. The takeaway for him is that giving it will heal something in him that receiving it would have, and that asking one of the older men to say a sentence to him afterward is not too much to ask.

## What do these stones mean

Roy's grandson will ask his own son someday what the plumb bob means. He'll tell him about a fence line before dawn, and four men, and a card in his father's handwriting, and a great-grandfather who used a tool like it to build a barn that's still standing. And then, if the men did their job, he'll give the boy a day.

That's how it's supposed to work. A pile of stones on a riverbank, and a father with a story, and a boy who knows where he came from and what he's for and who's watching.

Give your son a day he will not forget. Then give him the Tuesday after.

## Before you turn the page

If you never got a day, and I know a great many of you didn't, I want to say to you what I'd want said to me: it isn't too late, and the day you give your son will hand something back to you that you've been waiting forty years for. Stones on a riverbank are only rocks without a story, and you're the one who gets to tell it. May the Father who set stones in the Jordan give you the men and the morning and the nerve to say the fourth name out loud, and may your son carry something heavy in his pocket for sixty years that reminds him who he's supposed to be.
