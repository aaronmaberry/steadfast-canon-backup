# 21. If There Is No Wife Yet

Every wedding has a table for the single men. It's usually near the back, next to the cousins nobody could quite place, and the men at it are various ages, and every one of them has been told some version of the same sentence by some well-meaning aunt on the way to his seat. Your time will come.

Micah sat at that table for about six years. He told me that at one wedding, when he was twenty-eight, he counted: five men at the table, three of whom had been at the same table at the last four weddings. A married friend came over between the toasts, put a hand on Micah's shoulder, and said, "Hang in there, man. God's got somebody for you." And Micah said he sat there thinking, what if He doesn't, and what if that isn't actually the point?

That's the right question, and it's the one this chapter is about. Because I'm about to spend nine chapters on the house, on wives and beds and daughters and sons, and if you're a single man you could reasonably wonder whether you should skip ahead to Part 5. I'd ask you not to, because this chapter is for you first, and then I'm going to ask you to stay for the other eight, and I'll tell you why.

But first I have to say the thing the church mostly doesn't.

## You are not in a waiting room

The way most churches treat a single man, whether they intend to or not, is as a man-in-waiting. Half a man, whose real life starts at the altar. The singles group is a holding pen. The sermons about manhood assume a wife. The men's retreat is about being a better husband and father. And the single man sits there, twenty-six or thirty-four or forty-one, and absorbs the message that he's in the lobby of his own life.

Scripture doesn't talk like that. Jesus was single, and the fullest man who ever lived, the second Man in the second garden, never married, and nobody in the New Testament seems to think that made Him less of a man. Paul was single for the years we know about. John the Baptist was single. Some of the most useful men in the Bible had no wife and no children and served God's purpose in their generation with everything they had.

And go back to chapter 18. Nearly the whole blueprint was handed to Adam while he was alone: the image, the blessing, the commission to make the world more than it was, the place, the work, the guarding, the word, the naming. He was a full man with a full assignment before there was a bride. The bride was God's remedy for a specific not-good, and it was a good remedy, and I'm going to spend four chapters on it. But she wasn't what made him a man. He was one before she woke up.

So here's the truth I want to put under your feet before anything else. If there is no wife yet, you are not waiting to start. You're a man with a garden, and it needs working today, and a wife, if she comes, will walk into a house you've already built rather than a lot you've been meaning to clear.

## Three men at the table

I said "single man" as if it were one man. It's three, and they have very different griefs, and I want to look each of them in the eye.

The first is the man who has never married. You're somewhere between twenty-two and forty-five. You may want a wife badly, or you may be ambivalent, or you may have given up. What you share with the other two men at the table is that the culture has told you your life is on hold. What you don't share is that you've never had the thing everyone assumes you're waiting for, so you don't fully know what you're waiting for, and that makes it easy to turn her into something no real woman is.

The second is the man who's single again because a marriage ended, which is Kenny's seat at the table. You aren't waiting for something you've never had. You're grieving something you lost, or something you broke, or both, and you're doing it while managing a custody schedule and a one-bedroom apartment and a church that isn't sure what to do with you. Everything I say to the first man is true of you too, and you have a whole extra layer on top of it, and I'm going to speak to that layer.

The third is the man who's single again because his wife died, which is Mr. Pruitt's seat. You built the house and lived in it for forty or fifty years, and then the other chair emptied, and you're a single man in your seventies with a garden you don't know how to work alone. Nobody's telling you your time will come. Mostly they aren't telling you anything, because they don't know what to say. I do, so stay in this chapter.

And there's a fourth chair at that table that's easy to miss, because the man in it doesn't think of himself as waiting for anything yet.

At nineteen, Eli is single and expects to be for a while, and the danger for him isn't the back table; it's that he'll treat the next ten years as a rehearsal. He decided this year to treat his apartment like a house, which meant a table he actually eats at and a budget he actually keeps, and he started coaching a nine-year-old whose father left. The takeaway is that a nineteen-year-old who lives like a man is already doing most of what a husband does, minus the wife.

## What a single man builds

The blueprint says work it and keep it. Here's what that looks like without a wife.

He builds a house. Not a mortgage; a home. A single man's apartment should be a place where a man lives and not a place where a boy crashes. It should be clean enough for company, with a table people can eat at and food in the refrigerator that you cooked, with the money handled and the bills paid on the day. I've watched too many single men live as though their real house were somewhere in the future and this one were a tent, and it isn't a tent; it's your garden. And if a wife comes, the state of this house is the first thing she'll read about you, and she'll read it correctly.

He sets a table. Hospitality is not a married gift. Some of the best hospitality I've ever received was from single men who had decided their table was for other people. Have the young guys over, feed the widower, and host the twenty minutes at your place with coffee on. A single man who can set a table is doing what Adam was doing before Eve: making a place more than it was.

He builds a company, which was chapter 13. You need brothers more than a married man does, because you don't have the built-in person at home who notices when you go quiet. Get your twenty minutes, get a man ahead of you, and don't let the drift or the itch get a year's head start because nobody was watching. He builds a devotion. Paul says something about single men that usually gets read as a consolation prize and isn't one.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 7:35 (NIV)
"I am saying this for your own good, not to restrict you, but that you may live in a right way in undivided devotion to the Lord."
:::

Undivided devotion is what he calls it, and that isn't a lesser thing. It's a thing a married man with three kids honestly can't have in the same measure, and Paul knows it, and he's telling you it's a gift. You have hours, and you have attention, and you have a Tuesday evening with nobody needing you. You can pray longer and read deeper and serve harder and give more than any married man at your church, and if you spend those hours waiting instead, you've thrown away the one advantage your season gives you.

He builds a fatherhood. This is the one nobody tells single men, and it's the one that would change a church if they heard it. There are fatherless boys in your congregation, dozens of them, and their mothers are exhausted, and the married men are stretched thin with their own children. You have time and a truck, and you can coach, and you can show up, and you can be the man who teaches a nine-year-old with an empty chair how to throw, or change a tire, or pray. Paul was a single man who fathered Timothy so well that he called the younger man his true son. A single man can do fatherhood. It simply has a different shape.

And he builds a purity. Chapter 25 is called "The Bed Is a Covenant," and a single man might assume it's for the married men, and it isn't, because the covenant starts before the bed. Every choice you make now about what you look at and where you put your hands is either building a covenant for a woman you haven't met or spending it before she arrives. Paul's word to Timothy about women was one word long at the end of the sentence.

:::margin
1 Timothy 5:1-2 (NIV)
"Do not rebuke an older man harshly, but exhort him as if he were your father. Treat younger men as brothers, older women as mothers, and younger women as sisters, with absolute purity."
:::

As sisters, with absolute purity. That's the standard for how a single man treats every woman who isn't his wife, including the one who might become his wife. Absolute, and not mostly.

## If you want a wife

Most of you do, and it's a good thing to want, and Scripture says so.

:::margin
Proverbs 18:22 (NIV)
"He who finds a wife finds what is good and receives favor from the LORD."
:::

Finds is an active verb. A man who wants a wife doesn't wait to be found. He looks, with intention and with honor, and here is what I'd tell my own sons about how. Look at the right thing first. The culture will train you to look at her face and her body first and her character eventually, and you'll need to reverse that order, because Proverbs is blunt about it.

:::margin
Proverbs 31:30 (NIV)
"Charm is deceptive, and beauty is fleeting; but a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised."
:::

Fleeting is the word, because everything you're tempted to look at first is going to change. What won't change is whether she fears God, whether she tells the truth, whether she's kind when nobody's watching, how she treats her mother and the waitress, and those are the things to watch. Attraction matters, and I'm not telling you to marry a woman you aren't drawn to. I'm telling you that attraction is the least reliable thing you'll feel about her, and it should be the last thing you weigh rather than the first.

Go first, and go clearly, which we said in chapter 6. Ask her out plainly, for a specific evening, with your intention visible. Don't hang out for a year. Don't text for three months and never ask. Women can tell the difference between a man pursuing and a boy hovering, and they should never be made to guess which one you are. If you aren't sure you're interested enough to ask, you aren't ready to ask, and that's fine; just don't hover.

Don't linger, either, because once you're dating, a man decides. Not in a week, but not in four years either. If you've been with a woman long enough to know her character and you're still not deciding, you aren't being careful. You're being passive, and you're spending her years. Either move toward the covenant or release her honorably so that somebody else can.

Bring older men in. Let your man ahead of you meet her. Let your brothers meet her. Ask them what they see. You're in no condition to see clearly, because nobody in love is; borrow their eyes. And meet her people. How she is with her father and her friends is how she'll be with you in year eight.

Keep your hands off her. I'm going to say it because nobody else will. Sexual involvement before the covenant does two things at once. It clouds your judgment about whether this is the right woman, and it teaches both of you that the covenant isn't necessary for the very thing it was made to protect. Every man I know who did it wishes he hadn't, and every man I know who didn't is glad. That's a hundred percent in both directions, and I've asked a lot of men.

And marry when you can carry the weight, not when you feel ready, because nobody ever feels ready. What you need isn't a feeling but a garden that's being worked: a job you show up to, money that's handled, a house that's a home, brothers, a purity that's been held, a Father you talk to. If you have that, and you've found a woman who fears God, and the older men who know you both say go, then go. Paul had a plain word for men who could carry the weight and were stalling anyway.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 7:9 (NIV)
"But if they cannot control themselves, they should marry, for it is better to marry than to burn with passion."
:::

Better to marry, he says, and not "wait until you're thirty-two and own a house." If you're a man who can carry it, carry it.

## If you're single again

Kenny, I'm turning to you now, and to every man in your seat. Everything above is true for you. You still build a house, a table, a company, a devotion, a fatherhood (your own this time, across two addresses), and a purity. Single again is single. The standard doesn't have an asterisk for men who used to be married.

But you have things the never-married man doesn't, and I want to name them. You have grief, whether you wanted the divorce or fought it with everything you had, because something died, and it needs a funeral. Men skip this step and go straight to rebuilding, and the grief comes out sideways as anger or the itch or the drift, and then the rebuilding is on sand. Cry about it, tell a brother, and take a year to be honest about what was lost.

You have your part, which was chapter 15. You can't heal until you've said "I did this" about the piece that's yours without attaching her piece to it. Not for her sake, but for yours, and for God's, and for the man you're going to be next.

You have children, probably, and they come before any new woman. I'll be blunt about this. A divorced father who's dating hard while his kids are adjusting has his priorities upside down. Your children need to see a father who's stable and present and not distracted by a new relationship for a good long while. Kenny's older man told him two years, minimum, before he even thought about it. Kenny said it felt like a prison sentence at the time and turned out to be the best advice he ever took. "I got to be their dad without splitting my attention," he said. "They needed that more than I needed a girlfriend."

You have a church that may not know what to do with you. Some churches handle divorced men beautifully, and some don't, and the men drift off in shame. Stay anyway, and reject the passivity of leaving. Find the men who'll have you, and become the man who eventually welcomes the next divorced fellow who walks in looking for the exit.

And you have the question of remarriage, which I'm not going to settle in a paragraph, because Scripture handles it with more care than a men's book can. Sit down with your pastor. Read what Jesus and Paul said. Be honest about your circumstances. And don't let anyone, including yourself, rush you, because the man who remarries in year one to stop hurting is a man about to hurt somebody else.

## If she died

Mr. Pruitt, this is for you, and for every man in your chair. The blueprint still says it isn't good for a man to be alone, and you know that now in a way the young men at the table can't. You had the remedy for fifty years and it's gone, and nobody has told you what a man does with that.

Here is what I've watched. You grieve, out loud, for as long as it takes, and you don't let anyone tell you it should be faster. You keep working the garden, whatever's left of it: the trees, the grandkids, the Wednesday coffee. You find a brother, one man, because the couple friends fade and your children are busy and God said not alone. You stay in the house of the Father, in the Word and in the church, because the drift comes for widowers harder than for anyone, and it comes disguised as rest.

And whether you marry again is between you and God and your grown children and the woman, if there is one. Some widowers do, and it's a gift. Some don't, and their last years are full anyway. What you cannot do is decide that your life ended when hers did. Mr. Pruitt planted forty trees the spring after. That's a man who knows the garden is still his.

## Why you should stay for the next eight chapters

Now for the ask I promised. I'm about to write about knowing a wife, laying your life down for her, what she actually needs, the bed, daughters, and sons. Here's why a single man should read every word of it.

Read chapters 22 through 25 as a man building the house before she moves in. Everything you learn about knowing a woman and laying down your life and covenant faithfulness is a tool you'll want in your hand on the wedding night, not something you pick up in year three after the damage is done. The married men reading those chapters are mostly repairing. You get to build it right the first time.

Read them, too, as a man who can serve his married brothers. You're going to sit in the twenty minutes with a man whose marriage is struggling, and if you've read what a wife needs, you'll be able to ask him better questions than "how's it going."

And read chapters 26 through 29 as a man who can father. The daughter and son chapters are about what a child needs from a man, and there are children in your church who don't have one. A single man who knows how to give a boy a name and a hill and a day he won't forget is worth more to a fatherless kid than you can imagine. You may end up giving a son a day he'll never forget before you have one of your own. Some of the best fathers I know did exactly that.

## Not yet is not never, and it is not nothing

Micah got married at thirty-one, as you know. But the thing I want you to hear is what he told me about the six years at the back table. "I wasted the first three of them," he said, "just waiting. Then Dale told me to stop treating my apartment like a tent and my Tuesdays like a lobby, and I started building. I got the twenty minutes going, and started coaching a kid whose dad was gone, and fixed my money, and learned to cook, and had guys over. And the strange thing is, the day I stopped waiting for a wife was about the day I became a man a wife would want."

Not yet is honest. Most of you will marry. But not yet isn't never, and it certainly isn't nothing. It's a garden, with your name on it, and a work to do, and a Father who's watching you work it, and a reward that doesn't depend on whether anybody ever moves in.

Get up from the table. There's a house to build.

## Before you turn the page

If you're a man without a wife, I'd like you to close this chapter without the hum of being half a man, because you aren't one. The fullest man who ever lived never married, and nearly everything Adam was given, he was given alone. Your garden is real and it needs working today, and a wife, if she comes, will walk into a house you've built. If you're single again by divorce or death, the grief is real and God knows its size, and the garden is still yours. May the Father who said it isn't good for a man to be alone give you brothers this year, and a man ahead of you, and a church that knows your name, and may He handle the question of a bride in His time while you get up from the table and build.
