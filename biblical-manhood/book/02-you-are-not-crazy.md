# 2. You Are Not Crazy

For years I tried to steer my life by a list of slogans about manhood. Be strong. Be soft. Take charge. Get out of the way. The drive in me was dangerous, right up until the day I didn't use it, and then my lack of drive was the reason the house had gone cold. I could recite the whole list. I couldn't run a single week by it.

Then a man in the middle of his working years slid into the booth across from me at a diner and recited the same list back to me. He counted the items off on his fingers. When he got to six, he stopped. "Six manuals," he said. "Every one of them cancels the other five, and I'm supposed to follow all of them at once."

Mostly he was tired. The target kept moving, and somebody was still keeping score. One voice had told him manhood was a costume he could take off. Another told him it was the most important thing about him. One said he had to invent the meaning for himself, and another said the meaning had been settled for centuries and he was simply bad at it. At church he'd been treated like a problem the room had to manage. In locker rooms he'd been treated like an appetite the room had to feed. Live in that long enough and a man starts to wonder whether the fog rolled in from outside or whether he's been carrying it around in his own coat.

I told him what I'm telling you. The confusion is out there, in the world. The gap you feel between what you were told and what actually works is real.

## The picture you were handed

The picture of manhood men are handed right now won't hold together. We've spent years trying to clamp the pieces into one frame, and when they spring apart we blame our own hands. I want to lay the pieces out where you can see them, because you shouldn't keep blaming yourself for directions that never worked.

Start at the table where you grew up. Later in this book, "The Empty Chair" takes that room apart. For now the point is smaller. Whoever sat at the head of that table taught you manhood before you could read. Most of us got that lesson in broken pieces. A father who was there and never spoke. A father who was already gone. A father who came in like a storm. Even a faithful father left gaps. You left home holding one piece of the picture, and you went looking for the rest.

Plenty of people were glad to sell you the rest, and none of their pieces matched. The culture around you filed manhood under problems. Building, competing, protecting, taking charge of a room, all of it got described as an old instinct men should have outgrown by now. Then the same culture mocked the men who stayed boys, who wouldn't commit to anything and wouldn't grow up. It never admitted it had given both orders.

Movies and television offered a fool, a brute, or a hero who answers to nobody. The fool is the sitcom dad, lost in his own kitchen. The brute is the villain, and on some nights the man a boy gets coached to admire. The hero is the private movie every boy plays in his head. In that movie nobody's wife is worn out and nobody's father calls twice a year. His son never goes quiet on him. He has no church, no brothers, and no dishes in the sink on a weeknight. He lives a life with no address, and boys were taught to want that life while they lived in actual houses.

The job said a man is his paycheck. The internet said a man is whatever he watches. Some churches handed out the phrase "spiritual leader" so vaguely that a man still didn't know what to do on a Wednesday. Other churches shouted "warrior" and "king" so loudly that there was no room left for a car payment, a mother in a hospital bed, or the third shift at the plant. And a lot of books for men start in a man's feelings and never leave. Feelings matter. They just can't tell you whether you lived well. What you did can.

Set the piece from home next to all those instructions and you have the kit most of us have been steering by. One clear picture was never in the box. Six maps were, and no two of them point the same way. Carrying all six is easy. Following all six is impossible, because every new heading erases the last one. A man who keeps trying gets dizzy, and then he decides the dizziness is his own fault.

## What crossed maps do to a man

Those maps do three kinds of damage. I have scars from all three, and you may recognize them.

First, they freeze a man. If somebody has condemned every direction, the safest-looking choice is to stay put. In a lot of men, what gets called passivity is really that freeze. I've sat on a decision for months because one voice said lead and another said step back, and doing nothing felt like the only move that wouldn't break a rule. It passed for patience. The decision never got made.

Second, they turn a man into a set of performances. I learned the rotation early. One face for church, another for work, another for the guys after work, another for my wife. The man who wakes up in the morning keeps getting replaced by whichever one the room needs. A week of costume changes will empty you out. Underneath all the changes is a man who never got a picture steady enough to keep, so he borrows one from whoever's watching.

Third, they teach a man shame, and shame teaches him to keep quiet. Give a man orders he can't meet, orders that fight each other, and he'll convict himself. A low hum starts up under his days. He doesn't talk about it, because he's sure that saying it out loud would make the verdict final. I had that hum for years before the man in the booth told me about his. Most men who sit across from me have it. It's the sound a good man makes while he tries to satisfy six maps by Friday.

## What's actually true

Here's what's true, and I'll say it plainly.

Your sense that manhood is real is right. God made it. He created man and woman in His own image, and He called His work good before either of them had done a thing.

:::margin
Genesis 1:27 (NIV)
"So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them."
:::

Manhood is one of the ways that image gets carried. It was His plan from the start.

Your other sense, that the instructions don't fit together, is right too. You were handed maps that cross. Getting lost on them says more about the maps than about you.

And your hunger to hear, in plain words, what a man actually does is a good hunger. Older men were supposed to hand you a picture. Many of us never got one, and we filled the years after we left home with guesses.

The answer ahead is simpler than a shelf of books, and it will cost you more. It fits in one sentence. You live it on ordinary days, including the days you feel nothing useful. A rush of feeling won't carry it, and neither will the biggest Sunday of your year.

## One pass through the room

Where you are in life changes where the contradiction bruises you. The room is full of men with the same bruise.

A young man hears from school that his restlessness is a disorder. His phone tells him manhood is a matter of how many people are watching. His youth group tells him to be a servant leader, and the older guys tell him to keep count of girls. He tries the whole menu, crashes, and blames himself for a wreck the menu caused.

In a single week, a young father is told to be home more and to earn more, to lead and to defer, to be a rock for a newborn and to keep close track of his own feelings. He misses most of those orders, because they can't all fit on one calendar. The hum under his tiredness comes from demands that were never going to fit together.

A man further along quits listening. He decides manhood is the job plus the tired drive home, and he's tuned out the voices because they cancel each other. It looks like peace. Underneath, he folded up the maps and sat down on the stack.

An older man has lived his whole life on a picture he was handed decades ago. His grandchildren hold phones that mock that picture, and he wonders if the foundation was a mistake. Some of what he was handed was true, and some of it wasn't. The true parts didn't expire. He needs someone to sit with him while he sorts it out, and to help him leave the true pieces standing.

Same maps, every age. You're not the only one carrying them.

## Before you turn the page

Carrying a contradiction will wear a man out. That's the tiredness you feel. You've spent your strength hauling cargo with the wrong label on it. If the hum has been running under your life for a long time, let it go quiet tonight. You can see the maps now, so set them down. A line that can bear your weight is close, and you can drop the false pictures before the rest of your life is figured out. May God lift off your shoulders what He never gave you to carry, and strengthen you for what He did.
