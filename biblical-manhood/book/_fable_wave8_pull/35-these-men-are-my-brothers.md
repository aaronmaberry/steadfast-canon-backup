# These Men Are My Brothers

It's an ordinary day as I write this. Gray sky, a little wind, the kind of day nobody remembers. I've been thinking about where the men in this book are today, and I want to tell you before I let you go.

Not because they're impressive. None of them is famous, and if you passed any of them at a gas station you wouldn't look twice. I want to tell you because they're the argument of the whole book: the four marks are not for exceptional men. They're for ordinary men, in every season, on ordinary days, and I've watched them hold.

## Where they are

Roy is seventy-two. He got up at 5:30 this morning, made the coffee, and sat on the porch while his wife told him about a dream. Then he read Proverbs 12 out loud and drove to the church to look at a roof leak nobody had asked him about. He turned in a badge five years ago and thought his work was over, and he'll tell you the last five years have been the most important of his life, and nobody has clapped once.

Tomás's daughter is two. He walked her around the block at six this evening. His wife got five hours of sleep in a row last night because he took the first shift, and she learned in the worst year that she could trust him. He's twenty-nine, and most days he doesn't feel like a man. He's more of one than he knows.

The man whose children are with their mother this week called his son at 7:30 tonight, and the boy talked for twenty minutes about a smoker they built over eleven weekends. Three years ago he was a ghost in a one-bedroom apartment. An older man called him every week for a year and asked three questions. Now the men at his church whose marriages have ended talk to him. He's serving God's purpose with the thing he thought disqualified him.

Micah is married now, and he and his wife had a younger couple over tonight. He still meets Dale for breakfast every other week, and he's started meeting a twenty-four-year-old on Thursdays. He wrote a date inside his Bible the day a man told him to stop waiting for permission.

Walt sold off part of the business. He was home for dinner tonight, and he and his wife are planning a fall trip she picked. He has a brother now. He still thinks about the bank parking lot, the day he found out he had three hundred contacts and nobody.

Anthony walked into his kitchen tonight. He didn't stand in the doorway. His daughter looked up from her homework to see what his face would do when her mother came in, and it did the right thing. He says "Father" in the truck every morning, and it still feels a little foolish.

Cole names his son every night, three sentences, hand on the boy's head. He drove out to his father's place on Sunday and they looked at a pump together and didn't say much, which is the closest thing to an embrace his father has, and Cole took it. He's thirty-seven, and he isn't waiting anymore.

Hollis, seventy-four, wrote tomorrow's three before bed. One of them is "Call Roy re: roof."

Mr. Pruitt's pecan trees are past knee-high. He drank coffee with Ed this morning, as he has every Wednesday for four years, and it was the most important twenty minutes of his week. He's seventy-six, he won't eat one of those pecans, and he's the most settled man in the county.

Mr. Alvarez bathed his wife this morning, and she didn't know his name, and he knew hers. He said "in sickness" in 1968 and meant the whole thing.

## What they have in common

Look at that list. Single, married, single again, widowed, a newborn in the house, an empty nest, retired, sick. Not one of them is remarkable, and that's the point. They're the men I know, and every one of them, in his own season, on his own ordinary days, is doing the four things.

Every one of them got off the tailgate: Roy went and found the work, Tomás turned toward his wife inside the chaos. Every one of them picked up weight that was his, and some that wasn't: Walt sold trucks to be home, Mr. Alvarez picks up a washcloth every morning. Every one of them went first: Anthony into the apology, Hollis across a parking lot to a man by a truck. And every one of them is doing it for a Father who sees in secret. They know who's watching, and they expect God's reward, and that's the only reason the other three have held.

:::margin
Psalm 112:7 (NIV)
"They will have no fear of bad news; their hearts are steadfast, trusting in the LORD."
:::

Steadfast is the word this book is named for, and this is what it looks like: not a man who never fails, but a man whose heart is fixed to something that doesn't move, so that when the bad news comes he doesn't come apart. He reads the gauge, says the true thing to God and a brother, and puts his hands back on the wheel.

## These men are my brothers

I've called them brothers throughout, and I want to say what I mean by it. I mean we're in something together. We've said the true thing to each other at tables and in trucks and on phones at nine at night. We've stood at fence lines before dawn and put our hands on each other's sons. We've buried each other's wives and held each other's babies and called each other when there was nothing to say. Solomon said it in one line.

:::margin
Proverbs 17:17 (NIV)
"A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for a time of adversity."
:::

A brother is born for a time of adversity. Not the hunting buddy, and not the three hundred contacts. The one who picks up. Every man in these pages has one now. Most of them didn't when I met them, and the difference between the man they were and the man they are is mostly that.

And the company isn't closed, and it isn't a club for men who've got it together. Every one of them walked in as a man who didn't: Walt from a parking lot, Roy from a recliner, Anthony from a doorway, one from a ghost's apartment. They walked in because somebody asked them to, or because they finally asked somebody. The door was open, and it still is.

:::margin
Hebrews 10:24 (NIV)
"And let us consider how we may spur one another on toward love and good deeds,"
:::

Spur one another on. Not a crowd; a company. If you've read this far, if you've named the things and done the things and told a brother, then whether we ever meet or not, you're one of them. That isn't sentiment; it's what the four marks make. A man who lives them is in a company that stretches back to a garden and forward to a table that hasn't been set yet, and every man in it is ordinary and steadfast.

## The last time

The sentence once more, and then I'll get out of your way. Reject passivity, accept responsibility, lead courageously, and expect God's reward.

Four marks, not four moods. Four things you can see on a man the way you could see the fence stretcher on my grandfather's palm. They're the portrait of a Man in a garden on the worst night of His life, who got up off the ground, went out to meet what was coming, covered His men, and finished the work, for the joy set before Him. Every time you live one of them on an ordinary day, you're wearing His face.

You're going to fail at them. The question is whether you're in the first man or the second, and if you're in the second, whether you'll get up tomorrow and turn back toward the Father who does not leave and stand in your garden again. That's the whole life: get up, turn, stand, repeat until He says well done.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 15:58 (NIV)
"Therefore, my dear brothers and sisters, stand firm. Let nothing move you. Always give yourselves fully to the work of the Lord, because you know that your labor in the Lord is not in vain."
:::

Let nothing move you. Your labor is not in vain. Not the slab, not the coffee, not the call, not the pecans. He saw all of it, and the reward is with Him, and it lasts.

## An ordinary day

A man's life is decided on ordinary days. Not at the altar, not at the fence line, not on the last page of a book. On a gray day with a little wind, when nobody's watching and nothing's at stake except everything.

Tomorrow is one of those days. There's a smoke detector chirping in a hallway near you. There's a woman who needs you to know her, a child who needs a name and a hill, a father who needs a call, a brother who needs to be asked, a Father who's watching the road for you. You know what a man does. There's only the getting up. So I'll leave you the way the old priests left the people, with the words God gave them.

:::margin
Numbers 6:24-26 (NIV)
"The LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face shine on you and be gracious to you; the LORD turn his face toward you and give you peace."
:::

The LORD bless you and keep you. Keep is the second verb of Genesis 2:15, the one you were told to do for the garden, said back over you by the One who does it for you. So go work it, and go keep it. These men are my brothers, and there's a chair open at the table. Come be one.

## Before you close the book

If you've read this far you have a page written and a brother asked and a mark you're working on, and the Father who sees in secret has counted every line of it. May He keep you, the way He asked Adam to keep the garden, and may He make His face shine on you on the ordinary days, and may you find one day, at a table that hasn't been set yet, that these men were your brothers all along.
