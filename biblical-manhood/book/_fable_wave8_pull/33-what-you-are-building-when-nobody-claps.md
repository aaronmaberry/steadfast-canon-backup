# What You Are Building When Nobody Claps

In high summer, foundation crews pour at four in the morning. The concrete has to set before the heat gets to it, so the men work under portable lights, and by sunup the slab is down and the crew is gone.

Nobody photographs a slab. The family will live there thirty years and never think about what's under their feet, and every room where a child learns to walk and a couple fights and makes up sits on what those men did in the dark. Most of what a man builds, he builds when nobody is watching, and the part nobody sees is the part everything else stands on.

## Most of your life is unwitnessed

Do the math on your own week: the two o'clock feeding, the temptation you turned away from in a hotel room, the coffee you've made for your wife every morning for eleven years, the lie you didn't tell at work when the true answer cost you. Nobody clapped for any of it, and nobody is going to.

The culture's answer is poison: if nobody saw it, it doesn't count. That's the whole logic of the scoreboard, why men perform, why they drift toward the place that claps. Jesus said the exact opposite, over and over, in one sermon.

:::margin
Matthew 6:6 (NIV)
"But when you pray, go into your room, close the door and pray to your Father, who is unseen. Then your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you."
:::

Who sees what is done in secret. The unwitnessed thing isn't the wasted thing. It's the one with the highest return, because it's the only kind you can be sure you didn't do for the crowd. That's the fourth mark in its purest form: a man who expects God's reward can do a lifetime of unwitnessed good and not feel cheated, because he knows who saw.

## What you're actually building

He's building a floor for his children. The thousand dinners nobody remembers become, in a child, the settled sense that somebody's in charge. Your kids won't remember a single ordinary night. They'll remember, in their bones, whether the ordinary nights were there.

He's building a marriage that holds at seventy. Roy's has lasted forty-four years on coffee, not anniversaries, and it's better now because of the coffee he started making after he retired. When a couple in their seventies still likes each other, you're looking at a slab poured in the dark for fifty years.

He's building a name that arrives before he does. A reputation is built by the things people found out later that you did when they weren't looking. A man's last name is the first weight his son carries, and the feed store credit two counties over comes from forty years of paying bills nobody knew about on time.

He's building a church. Every church runs on men nobody thanks: the ones who stack chairs, sit with the old man in the hospital, teach the fourth-grade boys for twenty-two years. The pastor gets the platform. The men who built the floor under it are in the parking lot afterward with their hands in their pockets, and they're fine.

He's building a self, which is the one men forget. Jesus said whoever can be trusted with very little can be trusted with much, and the little is the unwitnessed. Eli's unwitnessed work this year was telling a customer the truth when a lie was free, and putting the phone in the kitchen when nobody would have known.

And he's building treasure in heaven, which isn't a metaphor for feeling good about yourself. It's a claim about where your work went: into an account kept by a Father who doesn't lose track.

## The fire

Paul had a picture for this.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 3:13 (NIV)
"It will be revealed with fire, and the fire will test the quality of each person's work."
:::

Every man is building; the only question is what with. What's built for applause is straw. It may even look better than gold, because straw is showy and gold is heavy, but when the fire comes, the work done for the crowd burns off in a second, and what's left is what was done for the Father in the dark. The slab poured at four in the morning is gold. The two o'clock feeding is gold. The sermon preached for the preacher, the gift given so the giver's name would go on the wall, the fatherhood performed at the ballpark and abandoned in the truck: straw.

## The day after the marker

You've had markers: a wedding, a baptism, the day you gave your son at the fence line, a retirement plaque and a handshake. The marker is the stone on the riverbank, and the Tuesdays are the land. The day after is where it gets decided, and the day after is exactly the day nobody's clapping.

Here's a rhythm for the man after the marker, the unwitnessed structure that holds the witnessed moments up. Roy's is the model; he built it after the hardest marker of his life.

The alarm doesn't move; Roy still rises at 5:30. The first hour is the plumb line: the Word, a chapter, and a prayer that starts with Father, before the phone. The floor, the family things that don't move, written down. The twenty minutes with your brother, same time every week. One secret good thing a week. A job by noon that somebody is counting on. The blessing, a hand on the head and three sentences if the children are home, the call if they're grown. The notebook, a line a day of what she told you. Sundown, nothing carried to bed: the apology made, the anger handed to God. And Sunday in the house of the Father, as a man with a job there.

Now watch it scale. Tomás, with a four-month-old and a ten-hour shift, takes the first fifteen minutes of the two-to-five feeding for a Psalm on his phone. His floor is the twenty minutes after his boots come off and the prayer over the crib. His brother gets the phone at nine while he does the dishes. His secret good thing is letting his wife sleep an extra hour Saturday and not mentioning it. His sundown is "I'm so tired I'm scared of myself, and I'm sorry, and I'm not going anywhere." It fits in the cracks of a life with no room, and it's gold.

The man with the every-other-weekend schedule builds his around the first hour before the kids wake, the midweek call, and the weekend that never gets traded away.

Build yours, ten things or five, on a card. It's the slab, and everything visible sits on it.

## When you're weary

There's a stretch in every unwitnessed work when nothing grows. You pour the slab and there's no house, only a flat gray thing in the dirt, for months. You make the coffee for a year and she's still distant. You call your son every week for eight months and he says "nothing." And the thought comes: I have labored for nothing.

:::margin
Isaiah 49:4 (NIV)
"But I said, 'I have labored in vain; I have spent my strength for nothing at all. Yet what is due me is in the LORD's hand, and my reward is with my God.'"
:::

Yet what is due me is in the LORD's hand. The servant felt what you've felt and didn't argue himself out of it. He moved the accounting. My reward is with my God, not with the results or the son who said nothing, but with God, who saw every one of the thirty-four calls before the one when the boy talked. Paul says the harvest comes at the proper time if we don't give up. Not your time; the pecan tree's time. Mr. Pruitt's trees are knee-high and he won't see them bear, and he isn't weary, because he never believed the reward was the pecans.

## The barn nobody remembers

Roy's grandson carries a plumb bob like the one his great-grandfather used to build a barn. The barn is still standing, and nobody in the county remembers the man's name.

That's what legacy is: not being remembered, but something standing. The town will forget you. The church will get a new pastor. Your grandchildren will move away. If you were building for the remembering, you'll be disappointed. If you were building for the standing, you'll have a barn.

Roy told me the hardest thing after the badge was realizing thirty-one years of work had already been forgotten by the people he'd done it for. "And then I realized that was fine. I didn't do it for them. I thought I did. I didn't. I did it because it was mine to do and God was watching." That's a man who found the fourth mark at seventy. His Thursday table won't be remembered either. The two men at it will stand.

## Thirty years in a shop

Jesus spent thirty years unwitnessed, and Luke gives the whole stretch one sentence.

:::margin
Luke 2:51 (NIV)
"Then he went down to Nazareth with them and was obedient to them. But his mother treasured all these things in her heart."
:::

The Son of God, planing boards in a town nobody thought anything of, obedient to a carpenter and his wife, and no one clapped, and it was the foundation everything else stood on. When He walked into the Jordan at thirty, the Father spoke out loud: this is my Son, whom I love. One sentence of applause, then three years of being doubted and finally killed, and He never went looking for the crowd's version. The Father had seen the thirty years. That was enough.

It's enough for you too. Nobody is going to clap for your slab. The Father watched you pour it in the dark.

:::margin
Hebrews 6:10 (NIV)
"God is not unjust; he will not forget your work and the love you have shown him as you have helped his people and continue to help them."
:::

He will not forget your work, so build. Pour the slab and make the coffee and make the call. Nobody's watching except the only One who counts.

## Before you turn the page

If the silence has started to wear on you, hear the servant's sentence once more: what is due me is in the LORD's hand. May the Father who watched His Son plane boards in Nazareth watch you pour your slab this week, and may you run your rhythm seven days without telling anyone and find that He kept every one.
