# 4. Stop Waiting

There's a smoke detector in a hallway somewhere near you that's chirping right now, one chirp every forty seconds or so, because the battery's low. The man of the house has walked under it two hundred times in the last three weeks. He knows exactly what it is and exactly where the nine-volt batteries are kept. He is a capable man who has rebuilt a carburetor and negotiated a raise and coached a ten-year-old's baseball team to a losing record with real grace, and he cannot bring himself to spend the ninety seconds it would take to make that chirp stop.

If you asked him why, he'd tell you he's going to get to it. That's the honest answer, and it's also the whole problem.

I don't care about the smoke detector. I care about what it's standing in for, because that same man has a conversation with his wife that he's going to get to. He has a call to his brother he's going to get to, a habit he's going to deal with, a debt he's going to face, a son who's gone quiet that he's going to sit down with, a church he's going to start showing up at again. He's going to get to all of it. He's been going to get to it for years, and the years are the point.

The first mark of a man is that he rejects passivity. This chapter is about what that looks like on a Tuesday, because the word "passivity" makes most men picture somebody lying on a couch, and that isn't the man I'm worried about. The man I'm worried about is busy and tired, and he is working hard on nearly everything except the handful of things that matter most, and he's waiting, and he doesn't know it.

## What passivity wears

Passivity almost never shows up in its own clothes. If it did, we'd recognize it and be ashamed, and shame would move us. Instead it borrows the wardrobe of the virtues, and it wears them convincingly.

It wears patience, and says "I'm giving it time." Sometimes that's true, and time really is the right tool. More often time is doing what the man is refusing to do, and it's doing it badly, because time has no judgment.

It wears peace, and says "I don't want to start something." So the thing that needs starting never starts, and the peace he's keeping is the peace of a house where nobody talks about anything that matters.

It wears humility, and says "Who am I to say anything?" The answer is that you're the husband, or the father, or simply the one who's standing there, and that is exactly who.

It wears spirituality, and says "I'm waiting on the Lord." I'll come back to this one at length, because there is a real kind of waiting on God, and there is a counterfeit that borrows His name as a doctor's note, and the difference between them decides a great deal.

It wears research, which is another book and another podcast and another conversation about the thing instead of the thing itself. I've known men who read six books on marriage and never once sat down across from their wife, asked her a real question, and listened to the entire answer.

And it wears busyness, which is the cleverest disguise of all. A man who's out the door at 5:40 and home at 7:15 and coaching on Saturdays and fixing the church van on Sunday afternoon does not look passive to anyone, least of all to himself. But ask his wife what he's avoiding and she'll tell you without taking a breath. Motion is not the opposite of passivity. Motion is very often how a man hides from the one thing standing still in the middle of his life. The first man did this too. When God came walking in the garden after the fruit, Adam was hiding in the trees, and God asked him a question that wasn't really a question.

:::margin
Genesis 3:9 (NIV)
"But the LORD God called to the man, 'Where are you?'"
:::

God knew exactly where he was. The question was for Adam's benefit, and it wasn't about his location. It meant: where are you, the man I put here to work this place and to guard it, the man who was standing right there when it all went wrong and said nothing? That question is still walking through gardens. It walks through your house, and it will walk through it again tonight, and the man under the chirping smoke detector needs to hear it as a question addressed to him.

## Why we wait

I've asked a lot of men why they didn't move when they knew they should have, and nobody has ever said "because I'm passive." Here's what they actually say, and you'll recognize your own voice in at least one of them.

They say they didn't want to make it worse. This is fear of conflict, and it's the most common reason of all. A man figures the current quiet is better than the noise he'd make by speaking, and what he doesn't figure is that quiet has a cost too, and that the cost compounds like interest on a debt nobody's paying down.

They say they weren't sure they were right. This is fear of failure, and it sounds responsible. He'd rather not act than act wrong. But not acting is also acting. It's a decision with consequences, and he will have to answer for it just as surely as he'd have answered for a wrong move.

They say nobody asked them. This one runs deep in a lot of men, and it almost always traces back to a boyhood where initiative got punished. Cole, who you'll meet properly later in this book, rebuilt a transmission at seventeen and had his father find the one bolt he'd torqued wrong, and that was the pattern of his whole childhood: every time he started something, a man showed him what was wrong with it. By ten he had learned that starting things meant being corrected, and by thirty he was a capable man who waited to be told, at work and at church and in his own marriage, because waiting was the only thing that had ever been safe. Maybe your father ran everything and there was no room for a boy to start anything. Maybe your father was gone and there was nobody to show you how a man begins a thing. Either way you learned to wait to be invited, and now you're forty-three and still waiting for an invitation that isn't coming, because grown men don't get invited to lead their own lives. If you're a young man and this is you, hear it early: nobody is going to send you an invitation. The garden's yours the day you're standing in it.

They say it'll work itself out, and it won't, because fields don't weed themselves, and Proverbs has a picture of this that's almost gentle in the way it's told, which is exactly what makes it land.

:::margin
Proverbs 24:30-31 (NIV)
"I went past the field of a sluggard, past the vineyard of someone who has no sense; thorns had come up everywhere, the ground was covered with weeds, and the stone wall was in ruins."
:::

Notice what the sluggard didn't do. He didn't burn the field, and he didn't tear down the wall. He just did nothing, for long enough, and the world did the rest, because thorns don't need a man's permission. Neither does distance in a marriage, or debt, or a teenager's silence, or a father's last years passing without the conversation.

And underneath all of the reasons men give, there's one they don't say out loud, which is that they're comfortable. The tailgate is comfortable. The hallway with the chirping detector is comfortable enough. Comfort is the most persuasive voice in a man's life, because it never has to raise itself to be heard.

## What waiting on God actually looks like

Now, about the doctor's note. The Bible does tell men to wait on the Lord, and I don't want to take that away from you, because there are things in your life you genuinely cannot control and you'll wreck yourself trying. But look at how Scripture says it.

:::margin
Psalm 27:14 (NIV)
"Wait for the LORD; be strong and take heart and wait for the LORD."
:::

Be strong and take heart. That isn't a man on a tailgate. That's a man on his feet with his jaw set, doing everything that's his to do while he waits on the parts that aren't. Abraham waited twenty-five years for a son, and while he waited he moved his whole household across a continent, dug wells, fought a battle, and negotiated with kings. Waiting on God never once meant sitting still on the parts that were his.

So here's the test I use on myself, and I'd ask you to use it too. If you say you're waiting on God, ask what you're doing while you wait. If the answer is "the next thing I already know is right," you're waiting on God. If the answer is "nothing, until He makes it obvious," you're waiting on comfort and calling it faith, and God rarely makes the next step obvious to a man who won't take the one already in front of him. Jesus never waited that way. When the time came for Him to go to Jerusalem, knowing exactly what waited for Him there, Luke reaches for a particular word.

:::margin
Luke 9:51 (NIV)
"As the time approached for him to be taken up to heaven, Jesus resolutely set out for Jerusalem."
:::

He set His face and He went. He didn't feel ready in any sense a man would recognize; we know that from the garden a few chapters later. He went anyway, and that is the Man we're following.

## How this looks in the different rooms

I want to turn toward a few of you specifically now, because passivity puts on different clothes in different houses, and you may not recognize it in yours.

If you're single, the most dangerous sentence in your life right now is "when I get married." When I get married I'll get serious about church. When I get married I'll get my money straight, and deal with what I watch, and learn to cook, and call my dad. But a wife is not a starting gun. The man you are on the Tuesday before you meet her is the man she'll get, and if you wait for her to make you start, you'll hand her a project instead of a husband. Start now, all of it, while there's nobody to blame you for not.

If there's a baby in your house, you're being tempted every day to wait for "when things calm down" before you turn back toward your wife. I have to tell you plainly that things aren't going to calm down. They're going to change shape, and then change shape again, and the turn toward her has to happen inside the chaos or it won't happen. Ten minutes at the kitchen counter after the baby's down, with your phone face down on the other side of the room, isn't waiting for a better season to arrive. It is the season, and it's the whole liturgy of this year of your marriage.

If you're divorced, there's probably a call you've been waiting to make until the anger cools. A conversation with your kids' mother about the schedule, or the school, or the thing your daughter said in the truck. The anger isn't going to cool on its own; anger doesn't work like that. It cools when a man acts like a man in spite of it. Make the call, keep it short and kind and about the children, and notice afterward that the anger is a little smaller than it was, because you did the thing it was telling you not to do.

And if you're retired, you may be waiting to be asked. Waiting for the church to call, for a younger man to seek you out, for your grandkids to want your time. Roy, who you'll meet again and again in this book, turned in a state trooper's badge after thirty-one years, and he told me the hardest part of the first year was realizing that nobody was ever going to hand him an assignment again. "I sat by the phone like a rookie," he said. "Then one morning I got up and went to find the work." He was sixty-eight when he stopped waiting, so don't tell me you can't.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A young man of twenty is waiting for his life to start. He has a phrase for it, which is "once I," as in once I finish school, once I get the job, once I meet her, once I move out. He's a good kid, and he's been on the tailgate for three years without knowing it, because every real thing he could start has been filed under "once I." The takeaway for him is that there is no starting gun. The garden is the apartment he's standing in.

A man of fifty is waiting for a conversation with his grown son to happen on its own. The son has gone quiet, and the father has told himself that the boy will come around, and it has been three years. The takeaway for him is the sluggard's field: nothing burned it down. It simply went untended for long enough. He needs to make the call this week, badly, and then again next week.

## How to get off the tailgate

Rejecting passivity is a habit, not a moment, and I want to give you the habit in a form you can actually run.

Start by naming the thing. Most men carry three or four things they've been going to get to, and they've never once said them out loud in a single sentence. Say them, or write them down if saying them is too much at first. "I need to talk to my wife about money. I need to call my brother. I need to deal with what I've been watching. I need to fix the smoke detector." Passivity lives in vagueness, and it can't survive a list.

Then ask, for each one, what you're actually waiting for. There's an honest reason to wait and a comfortable one, and they sound different if you listen. An honest reason sounds like "I need one more fact" or "it's better to talk after the kids are asleep, which is tonight." A comfortable reason sounds like "when things settle down" or "when I feel ready." Feeling ready is not a prerequisite for anything a man has to do. Nobody has ever felt ready to bury a parent or hold a newborn or tell his wife the truth about the debt.

Then find the smallest first move. Not the whole conversation, but the sentence that starts it. Not the whole financial overhaul, but the one account you log into tonight. Not a new life, but the battery. Passivity is beaten by small real motion and not by big resolutions, because a big resolution is one more way to feel like you did something without doing anything.

Then move before noon. This is a Tuesday rule and I'd ask you to keep it strictly. If you've named the thing and found the first move, do it before lunch. Waiting for the evening is waiting. Waiting for the weekend is waiting. Men who have learned to reject passivity have learned to hate the gap between knowing and doing, because that gap is where whole lives disappear.

And expect it to feel wrong. Almost every right move a man makes against his own passivity feels awkward the first time. The conversation will start badly. Your voice will sound strange to you. She may not respond the way you hoped, and your brother may not call back. None of that means you shouldn't have moved. It's the sound of a man doing something he's out of practice at, and the only cure for being out of practice is practice.

## The reward for moving

I told you last chapter that the fourth mark makes the other three possible, so let me show you how it touches this one. There's a line in James that most men read as a warning, and it is one, but there's a promise hidden inside it if you look.

:::margin
James 4:17 (NIV)
"If anyone, then, knows the good they ought to do and doesn't do it, it is sin for them."
:::

The warning is plain enough: knowing and not doing is sin. God takes your passivity personally, because He put you where you are on purpose, the way He put Adam in the garden, and He's asking where you are. But the promise is this: God sees the good you ought to do. He knows about the smoke detector and the conversation and the call. Which means He also sees when you move. Nobody else may notice. Your wife may not thank you for finally bringing up the money, and your brother may let it go to voicemail. But the Father who sees what's done in secret watches a man get off the tailgate, and He is not indifferent to it. That's a reward worth working for, and it's the reason you can move on a Tuesday when nothing in you feels like moving.

So stop waiting. Go change the battery, and then, while you're standing there with the old one in your hand, go make the call.

## Before you turn the page

If you've been on the tailgate a long time, I want to say something to you before you get off it. You aren't lazy, and you probably aren't a coward. You're a man who learned somewhere that waiting was safer than moving, and the lesson was true once and isn't anymore. The Father who asked Adam where he was is asking you the same thing tonight, and He isn't asking in anger. He's asking the way a father asks a boy who's been hiding in the barn: come on out, there's work to do, and I'll do it with you. May He give you the small strength to change the battery, and the larger strength to make the call, and may you find that the first one makes the second one possible.


## YOUR TURN

---

**Name it.** Because passivity can't survive being said out loud: name the one thing you've been going to get to the longest, and say honestly how many months or years it's been.

**Do this week.** Because the gap between knowing and doing is where a life disappears: do the smallest first move on that one thing before noon on a workday. The first sentence, the first login, the first call.

**Tell a brother.** Because a man is far more likely to move when another man knows he said he would: tell him what you've been waiting on and what you did about it, and give him permission to ask again next week.

**Carry this.** Where are you? God knew where Adam was. The question was for Adam, and it's for you.
