# 12. Leave Without Slamming the Door

A porch will show you a man and his parents.

One man skips the knock. Gray in the beard, a mortgage in another part of town, and he has the refrigerator open before a hello gets said. His wife is still outside on the step. Another man has kept his feet off that porch for years. He can recite the fight that closed it. Freedom is what he claims. The place still has him. A third man may keep a key. He knocks. He waits. The door opens, and what he says is, "Hey, Dad."

Home has been left by the third man. The first two are still in the doorway. A hand rests on the knob for one of them. A back is turned for the other. Neither man knows his own position. The knock is what God designed for a son.

:::margin
Genesis 2:24 (NIV)
"That is why a man leaves his father and mother and is united to his wife, and they become one flesh."
:::

Leaving is assigned to a man. God is the one speaking, and the subject is a grown son. The line stands ahead of "is united to his wife," so the single man is inside the command. The same God set the other half where missing it would take work, inside the Ten Commandments.

:::margin
Exodus 20:12 (NIV)
"Honor your father and your mother, so that you may live long in the land the LORD your God is giving you."
:::

Leave, and honor. One motion holds both. A slammed door manages the leaving and fails the honor. A hand that will not release the knob manages the honor and fails the leaving. God is after the son who carries both. Two messages ride on a knock. My address is elsewhere now. The house remains yours, and I am glad the door opened.

## The man who never left

Start with the hand on the knob. He is confident the chapter was written for a louder man.

Your mother's opinion still outranking your wife's, inside a kitchen that is yours, means you have not left. One remark from your father still able to return you to twelve, with your decisions arranged to prevent the remark, means you have not left. Single, well into your thirties, every large choice still cleared at their table, means you have not left. Furniture in a different building was a separate event.

Counsel remains a gift across a whole life. A wise man keeps receiving it. A life whose center stays in someone else's kitchen never grows a center of its own. Children need the father in the house to be the father, a man whose directions begin with him.

A good leaving skips the fight. Decide, then tell them. Ask first, and the decision returns to their hands. Say, "I love you, and we're going to do it this way." Stand between your wife and your mother's criticism, including the days your mother is right. Keep the voice low and the back straight.

## The man who slammed it

Face the man who turned his back. Some of you left houses no boy should have had to live in. I will not send you back, and I will not send you toward a man who is still dangerous. The aftermath of a slam, I have watched too often to leave quiet.

A slam does not stay behind you. Fury is attention, so the father you cut off occupies every room you enter, and your days arrange themselves around him. The bond is tighter than the Sunday phone call, and the rope stays invisible because anger made it.

Children study how a son treats a father. That is the manual they will use. Contempt gets stored for the year you are the old man. Honor, given to a man who did not earn it, teaches what a sermon cannot install.

## Honor when he wasn't honorable

Honor is the treatment of an office. Scripture withholds any assignment to call a bad father good, and it withholds any assignment to lie about him.

A soldier can salute the rank when the man in the uniform has lost his respect. The salute stays clean.

With a hard father, honor is practical. Tell the truth and skip the mockery. Provide for him in old age, even if he never provided for you. Pray, even if the whole prayer is, "Father, I don't know how to pray for this man, but You know him."

Forgive him inside the same honor. The harm mattered. God knows the size. Trust is a separate choice. Forgive him and still refuse to leave your children alone with him. Some tables are unsafe, and a restored one may never come. You decide that God will settle the debt. You stop collecting. Peace quits waiting on an apology that may never arrive.

:::margin
Romans 12:19 (NIV)
"Do not take revenge, my dear friends, but leave room for God's wrath, for it is written: 'It is mine to avenge; I will repay,' says the Lord."
:::

Hear relief in "leave room for God's wrath." Repayment was never placed on your list of duties.

A pit, then a sale. That is what Joseph's brothers did to him. Years on, he held every advantage, and he spoke the clearest line I know about forgiveness of this kind.

:::margin
Genesis 50:20 (NIV)
"You intended to harm me, but God intended it for good to accomplish what is now being done, the saving of many lives."
:::

He named their intent. You intended to harm me. Then he named what God accomplished with the harm. Honest about the wound. Trusting about the use God made of it. No invoice laid on the table.

## The knock

A father alive, and an approach that is safe, may mean a conversation is waiting on a porch. Some men should leave that conversation shut. Certain talks only tear open a wound God is already closing. Plenty of men have occupied the porch for years without ever knocking. Four parts make the knock.

What you needed and failed to receive. One sentence carries it. A list will not. "Dad, I needed to hear you were proud of me, and I never did." One sentence can land. Ten will bounce.

What you can thank him for. Even a hard father usually left a particular thing. A way of working. A roof. A single afternoon on the water. Gratitude, here, is honor doing its work.

What you have decided. "I'm going to raise my kids differently, and I'm done carrying this against you." A decision has one sound. A threat has another.

The door, held open by you. "I'd like to know you as a man, if you're willing." His apology is not the fee for that offer. Whatever he answers stays on his shoulders.

Grant walked it out with a father who had never spent a kind word on him. The exit from that house, years earlier, had been a slam. He sat in the truck a long while before the door. His father opened, said nothing, and Grant delivered the four parts on the porch, then stopped. No apology came. He never said he was proud. The old man's eyes went past Grant to the field across the road and stayed there. Then he asked, "You eat yet?" and turned for the kitchen. Behind him the door remained open. Grant followed. Supper, he told me later, was as near an embrace as the old man possessed. Grant took it. He had quit demanding more than his father had the power to give.

A dead father, or a father too dangerous to approach, still gets the four parts. God hears them. A brother hears them. The words have to leave you even when they have no way to reach him.

## You'll leave more than once

The verse names father and mother. A mother who raised you alone is in it. In your house, your wife comes first. Men leave more than once, and both failures repeat.

A single man wants to cut his parents off, or to call them before every large choice. Decide, then tell them, and let love ride in the telling. A young father leaves again when the baby comes with grandparents attached. In his mother's hearing he learns to say, "We're doing it my wife's way." After a divorce the pull backward is strongest, into a mother's kitchen, where love will make her his confidante and his excuse to skip a life of his own. She will make room. He still has to build the life. Middle years, an aging father, and every visit sends him home a boy again. The four-part talk is not late. An older man watches from the other side of the door, a son who slammed it or drifted. Be the father Jesus described, who went out to both sons. Make the unanswered call. If you failed him, say it once. "I wasn't the father you needed, and I'm sorry." If he knocks, set another plate.

## Knock, and wait

The four marks Passivity is what keeps a hand on the knob for years, and that passivity has to be rejected. Honor still owed is what the slammed door left behind, and responsibility means he accepts the debt of it. Both men have a walk to lead, up to the door. Both are done treating a father's apology as the prize. A better prize is in God's hand.

Whichever kind of father he was, he was yours, and the treatment he receives now shows on you. Leave gently. Draw the door shut behind you and let it make no sound. On the return, knock. Build a house that has a door your own son will be able to knock on when his turn arrives.

## Before you turn the page

Tell God the truth about your father. Give Him the debt. Honor the office on your way out, and let the door shut without a sound. May the Father who stayed with you make that exit steady, give you grace for a later knock, and teach your children, by your example, how to leave you well.
