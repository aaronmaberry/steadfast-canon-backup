# 35. These Men Are My Brothers

Gray sky, a little wind, an ordinary day to be writing. Journals skip days like this. Before I let you go, here is where the men in these pages are.

I am not telling it because they impress anyone. Fame missed all of them. A gas station, and you would walk past without a second look. They are the argument. Ordinary days, every season, ordinary men. The four marks were made for that. I have watched the marks hold.

## Where they are

Roy is seventy-two. Coffee at 5:30, then the porch, his wife telling a dream. Proverbs 12 read aloud. A roof leak at the church after that, though nobody had asked him to look. The badge went in five years back, and he thought the work had closed. He will tell you these five mattered more than the years before them. Clapping has not happened once.

Tomás walked his daughter around the block at six. She is two. Five hours of sleep in a row for his wife last night, because the first shift was his. Trust got learned in the worst year. He is twenty-nine. The feeling of manhood is missing most days. The manhood is not.

Their mother has the children this week. At 7:30 their father called his son, and for twenty minutes the boy talked about a smoker they built across eleven weekends. Three years ago the father was a ghost in a one-bedroom apartment. An older man phoned every week for a year and asked three questions. Men at his church whose marriages have ended now come to him. God's purpose is being served by the very thing he thought had ruled him out.

Micah is married. A younger couple ate at their table tonight. Dale still gets breakfast every other week. A twenty-four-year-old gets Thursdays. The day a man told him to quit waiting on permission, Micah wrote the date in his Bible.

Part of the business is sold. Walt was home for dinner, and a fall trip is being planned, her choice. He has a brother. The bank parking lot is still in his head, three hundred contacts and nobody.

Anthony entered the kitchen tonight. The doorway did not keep him. His daughter, homework in front of her, watched his face for what it would do when her mother walked in. The face did the right thing. "Father," in the truck each morning, still feels a little foolish in his mouth.

Cole's hand is on his boy's head every night. Three sentences, and the name. Sunday they stood over a pump at his father's place and said little. That quiet is as close as his father comes to an embrace. Cole received it. Thirty-seven, and the waiting has stopped.

Hollis is seventy-four. Tomorrow's three were written before bed. One of them reads "Call Roy re: roof."

Mr. Pruitt's pecans are past his knee. Ed had coffee with him this morning, Wednesday, as it has been for four years, the most important twenty minutes he will spend all week. Seventy-six. Those pecans will not be his to eat. Settle the county and he is the most settled man in it.

Mr. Alvarez bathed his wife this morning. Her memory had lost his name. His had kept hers. The year was 1968 when he said "in sickness," and he meant every word that followed.

## What they have in common

Single. Married. Single again. Widowed. A baby in the house. An emptied nest. Retired. Sick. Run the list. Remarkable fits none of them, and that is the whole point. I know these men. Each one, his season, his ordinary days, is doing the four things.

Off the tailgate, all of them. Roy went looking for the work. Tomás turned toward his wife while the chaos was still in the room. Weight was picked up, theirs and some that was not. Trucks got sold so Walt could be home for dinner. A washcloth comes up in Mr. Alvarez's hand every morning. Someone went first. Anthony went into the apology. Hollis crossed a lot to a man standing by a truck. A Father who sees in secret is who they are doing it for. They know the audience. God's reward is the one they are counting on. The other three marks have held for that reason.

:::margin
Psalm 112:7 (NIV)
"They will have no fear of bad news; their hearts are steadfast, trusting in the LORD."
:::

The book takes its name from steadfast. On a man it looks like a heart fastened to what does not shift. Bad news arrives and he stays in one piece. Gauge read. Truth said to God and to a brother. Hands returned to the wheel.

## These men are my brothers

I have used the word the whole way through, and I want it to mean a real thing. We are in the same work. Truth has been said at tables, in trucks, on the phone at nine. Fence lines before daylight, hands on one another's sons. Wives buried. Babies held. Calls made when no useful sentence existed. Solomon got it into a line.

:::margin
Proverbs 17:17 (NIV)
"A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for a time of adversity."
:::

Born for adversity. A hunting partner is a different category. So is a name among three hundred contacts. The brother is the one who answers. Every man on these pages has one. Most had none the day I met them. That gap, more than any other, is what changed them.

The company has not closed, and it is no club for men who already have it handled. They walked in unfinished. A parking lot for Walt. A recliner for Roy. A doorway for Anthony. An apartment that felt like a ghost's for one of them. Asked, or finally asking. The door stood open. It stands open.

:::margin
Hebrews 10:24 (NIV)
"And let us consider how we may spur one another on toward love and good deeds,"
:::

Spur one another on. Company, where a crowd would have been easier. This far into the book, the naming done, the doing done, a brother told, you belong with them whether our paths cross or not. A garden behind us. A table not set yet, ahead. The four marks make that company. Ordinary. Steadfast. Same men.

## The last time

Once more, and then I am out of the way. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

You can see the four on a man the way the fence stretcher showed on my grandfather's palm. They portrait a Man in a garden on the worst night He lived. Up off the ground. Out to meet what was coming. His men covered. The work finished, for the joy set before Him. An ordinary day, one of the marks lived, and His face is what you are wearing.

Failure is coming. The question is the man you are standing in, the first or the second. In the second, the next question is tomorrow. Get up. Turn toward the Father who does not leave. Stand in the garden again. The life is that repetition, until the word is well done.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 15:58 (NIV)
"Therefore, my dear brothers and sisters, stand firm. Let nothing move you. Always give yourselves fully to the work of the Lord, because you know that your labor in the Lord is not in vain."
:::

Let nothing move you. The labor is not wasted. Slab, coffee, call, pecans, He saw the set of them. The reward sits with Him. It lasts.

## An ordinary day

Ordinary days decide a life. Altars do not. Fence lines do not. Last pages do not. A gray day, a little wind, nobody looking, and the stake seems to be nothing, which means it is everything.

Tomorrow is one. A smoke detector chirps in a hallway near you. A woman needs to be known. A child needs a name and a hill. A father needs the phone to ring. A brother needs the ask. A Father is on the road, watching for you. You already know the move. What remains is getting up. The old priests left the people with words God had given them. I will do the same.

:::margin
Numbers 6:24-26 (NIV)
"The LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face shine on you and be gracious to you; the LORD turn his face toward you and give you peace."
:::

The LORD bless you and keep you. Keep is the garden's second verb, the charge laid on a man for his ground, now said back over him by the One who keeps him. Work it. Keep it. My brothers are these men. The table has an open chair. Come take it.

## Before you close the book

A page is written if you have come this far, a brother has been asked, and a mark is in your hands. Lines have been counted by the Father who sees what is hidden. May He keep you as He told Adam to keep a garden. May His face shine on the ordinary days. At a table still unset, may you learn that the brotherhood was already yours.
