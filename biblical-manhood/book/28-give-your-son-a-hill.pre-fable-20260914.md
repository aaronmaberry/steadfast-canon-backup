# 28. Give Your Son a Hill

Hollis was twelve the day his father sent him up the caprock alone after a stray calf.

They'd been checking fence in the truck when they spotted it, way up in the rocks where the pasture broke off into a wall of caliche and cedar. His father stopped the truck, looked at the hill for a minute, and said, "Go get him." Not "let's go get him." Go. Hollis remembers standing at the bottom looking up and being certain he couldn't. The calf was scared and half wild, the rocks were loose, there were rattlesnakes in that country, and he was twelve years old.

"I looked back at the truck," he told me, sixty years later. "He was just sitting there with the window down, watching. He didn't get out."

It took Hollis an hour and a half, and he fell twice, and he got the calf cornered against a ledge and got a rope on it and got kicked in the thigh and got it down the hill anyway, and when he walked up to the truck with the calf on a lead and his jeans torn and blood on his shin, his father looked at him for a long moment and said, "Well. I figured you could."

"I knew he was watching the whole time," Hollis said. "He never got out of the truck. That was the point. If he'd come, I'd have learned I couldn't do it without him. He stayed in the truck, and I learned I could."

Give your son a hill. That's this chapter, because the last one was about the name you give him, and a name without a hill is only a compliment. A boy becomes what you called him by climbing something that proves it.

## Boys need difficulty

Here's a thing about boys that the culture has mostly forgotten and every father knows in his bones. Boys don't become men by being protected from difficulty. They become men by being handed difficulty that's slightly beyond them, with a man watching who doesn't rescue too soon. That's how it has always worked. Isaac carried the wood up the mountain.

:::margin
Genesis 22:6 (NIV)
"Abraham took the wood for the burnt offering and placed it on his son Isaac, and he himself carried the fire and the knife. As the two of them went on together,"
:::

Placed it on his son. There's a father giving a boy a weight to carry up a hill. Abraham carried the fire and the knife, the dangerous things, and gave his son the load, and the two of them went on together: a father and a son, one carrying and one watching, going up. David's father gave him the sheep. That doesn't sound like much until you read what David did with them.

:::margin
1 Samuel 17:34-35 (NIV)
"But David said to Saul, 'Your servant has been keeping his father's sheep. When a lion or a bear came and carried off a sheep from the flock, I went after it, struck it and rescued the sheep from its mouth.'"
:::

I went after it. A teenage boy alone in the hills with his father's flock, and a lion comes, and he goes after it. Nobody sent him a lion. But his father sent him to the hills with something real to keep, and the lion found him there, and by the time Goliath showed up David had already been up a few hills. He told Saul as much. The lion and the bear were the training, and the sheep were the hill his father gave him.

And Jesus grew up in a carpenter's shop. We don't have the details, but we have the word: the boy who spoke the world into being spent His growing years with tools in His hands under His father's eye, and Luke says He grew in wisdom and stature. That's a boy with a hill.

Now look at what happens to a boy without one. He's comfortable and he's protected, and every difficulty gets removed before it reaches him, every failure gets cushioned, every risk gets managed by adults who love him, and he's starving, because boys are built for hills, and a boy who isn't given one will go find one, and the hills he finds on his own are almost always worse: the fight, the dare, the video game with ten thousand hills in it that cost nothing and build nothing, the drugs, the recklessness in a truck at seventeen. Or he'll do the other thing, which is quieter and which I see more of now. He'll never leave the basement, because nobody ever taught him he could climb anything, and the whole world looks like caprock with no truck at the bottom.

## What a hill is

Let me define it, because a hill isn't a chore chart and it isn't a travel ball schedule.

A hill is real. It matters to somebody besides him. The family depends on its getting done, or a person is actually helped, or something actually gets built. Boys can smell a made-up job from across the yard. Feeding the dog is real if the dog doesn't eat otherwise. Sweeping a clean garage to "learn responsibility" is a lie, and he knows it.

A hill is slightly beyond him. Not what he can already do, and not what a man can do; the next thing. The calf was beyond a twelve-year-old and only barely not, and that's the zone. Too easy and he learns nothing. Too hard and he learns that he can't. The father's whole skill is finding the next hill, and it's a skill you acquire by watching your son closely, which is chapter 22 applied to a boy.

A hill has real risk, bounded. He can fail, and he can get hurt a little, and he can lose money, break the thing, come down without the calf. If there's no way to fail, it isn't a hill; it's a ride. But the risk is bounded by a father who has chosen the hill with care. Hollis's father knew that hill. He knew the snakes were mostly gone that time of year. He was in the truck. Bounded risk is how a boy learns courage without learning recklessness.

A hill has an outcome he owns, and I mean outcome rather than effort. "I tried" is what a boy says. "It's done," or "it isn't done yet, and here's what I'm doing," is what a man says, and he learns to say it on hills where nobody accepts "I tried." The calf came down or it didn't.

And a hill is followed by a name. When he comes down, whatever happened, the father speaks. "I figured you could." That's the name from chapter 27 landing on the hill from chapter 28, and together they make something a compliment alone never can. He was called capable, and then he climbed, and now he knows it's true.

## The hills, by age

Fathers ask me what hills look like at what age, so here's the range I've seen work. Your son is his own boy; adjust.

From four to six, real jobs a small boy can do that actually matter. Carrying the groceries in, all of them, even when it takes four trips. Feeding the animal that depends on it. Handing you tools and learning their names. Picking up the whole yard before the mower. He isn't helping; he's working, and you should tell him so.

From seven to ten, ownership. Something that's his to keep alive or keep working: a garden bed, a pet's whole life, the trash on trash day without reminders, a small tool set he's responsible for. First real tools, with instruction and then with trust. First fire, built himself, with you a step back. First time doing a whole task alone from start to finish while you're somewhere else. The hill at this age is "yours, without me standing over you."

From eleven to thirteen, a real job with real money: mowing yards, hauling hay, a summer working for a man who isn't you. A physical challenge that scares him a little: the long hike, the first hunt, the build he isn't sure he can finish. Caring for someone weaker: a younger sibling for an afternoon, the widow's yard without pay. And at least one hill he climbs alone with you in the truck, which is his caprock.

From fourteen to seventeen, work for someone else who'll fire him if he's lazy. Money he earns and manages and loses some of. A project he owns from the idea to the finish: rebuild the engine, build the deer stand, run the fundraiser. Hardship he chooses and endures: the mission trip somewhere hard, the cold camp, the fast. A decision with consequences that he makes and you don't override. The hill at this age is "a man's work with a man watching from farther off."

And from eighteen onward, the launch, which was chapter 12. He leaves, without slamming the door, and the hill is his own life, and you're a man he can call rather than a man who climbs it for him.

Notice what isn't on that list. There's nothing about trophies. A boy can play sports for a decade and never climb a hill if the sport is his father's ego and every failure gets explained away by the coach's bad call. And there's nothing about screens, because a screen is a machine for simulating hills without the weight. Ten thousand levels beaten, and not one calf brought down.

## The father's part

Give it, and don't do it. Your instinct will be to do the job yourself because you're faster and better and it's easier. Every time you do, you've stolen a hill. Hand it over and walk away.

Watch, and don't rescue. Stay in the truck, and let him struggle, and let him fall twice, because this is the hardest thing in fathering, and mothers usually find it harder than fathers do, so you're going to have to lead here, gently, and explain why. Rescuing a boy who isn't in danger teaches him he needs rescue. Watching a boy who isn't in danger teaches him he doesn't.

Let him fail small so that he doesn't fail big. A boy who's allowed to lose forty dollars at twelve because he didn't plan learns something that will save him forty thousand at thirty. A boy who's never allowed to fail at anything fails for the first time at twenty-three with a wife and a mortgage and nobody in the truck.

Debrief him afterward. When he comes down, ask what happened, what he learned, what he'd do differently. Not a lecture; questions, and then waiting. He's teaching himself with your help.

Name what he did. "I figured you could." "That's what a man does." "You didn't quit." Chapter 27. Every hill ends with a name, or it's only a hard day.

And raise the hill. Next time, a little more. That's the whole method. A boy who climbs a slightly bigger hill every year for fifteen years is a man by eighteen, not because you told him so but because he has the calluses to prove it.

:::margin
Hebrews 12:11 (NIV)
"No discipline seems pleasant at the time, but painful. Later on, however, it produces a harvest of righteousness and peace for those who have been trained by it."
:::

Trained by it, the writer says, and that's what hills are: training. It isn't pleasant for him, and it isn't pleasant for you watching from the truck. Later on, a harvest.

## How fathers get it wrong

Some fathers give no hill at all. Comfort, protection, and a screen. The boy is loved and safe, and he has never once been asked to do something he wasn't sure he could do. He'll be thirty and living in your basement, or thirty and taking stupid risks to feel something, and both are boys who were never handed a calf.

Some fathers give too big a hill too fast. The father reads a book about raising men and hands his nine-year-old a man's job and crushes him. Then the boy learns that the hill is a place where he fails, and he stops climbing. Slightly beyond, not a mountain.

Some fathers give their own hill instead of the son's. The sport the father wanted to play. The business the father wants to hand down. The hill has to be real for the boy, in the direction of who the boy is, and not a monument to who the father wishes he'd been.

Some fathers rescue, finishing his science project at midnight, calling the coach, paying off the debt he ran up. Every rescue is a message: you can't. Stay in the truck.

And some fathers shame the fall. He fails and you make him pay for it with contempt. Now the hill is a place of fear, and he'll avoid every hill he can for the rest of his life. Hollis fell twice on the caprock. His father didn't say a word about the falls. He said, "I figured you could."

## Hills in every season

If you have a baby boy, the hill is years away, but the habit starts now. Don't do for him what he can do. Let him struggle to sit up before you prop him. Let him work at the toy before you fix it. Fathers of infants learn to rescue reflexively because a crying baby is unbearable, and some of that is right. But a father who catches himself doing everything for his son at six months is a father who'll be doing everything for him at sixteen. Watch a little, from the truck, even now.

If you're divorced, watch the weekend trap. It's easy to make your time with your son all fun, pizza and movies and the lake and no chores, because you feel guilty and you want him to want to come. That's a boy with no hill at your house. He needs work at your place: his own jobs, his own responsibilities, a project you build together across weekends so that it's always waiting for him when he comes back. Kenny and his son built a smoker over eleven weekends. "It was the only thing at my house that was ours," he said. "He couldn't wait to get back to it. Not because it was fun. Because it wasn't done."

If you're an older man, you give hills better than anyone alive. You have time, tools, a shop, and patience the boy's father doesn't have. Roy's grandson learned to fish from Roy, and then to clean the fish, and then to gut a deer, and then to change the oil on Roy's truck, and the boy's father told me his son comes home from Grandpa's "taller." Grandfathers hand out hills that fathers are too busy or too anxious to give. That's a job, and it's yours.

If you're a single man, or a man with no sons, there are boys in your church who have never been up a hill because there was nobody in the truck. Coach them, and coach them hard. Take one to work with you. Run the church workday and give the fatherless boys the real jobs. Hollis's father sent him up the caprock; some boys need an unrelated man to point at a hill and say "go," because their fathers never did, and you can be that man.

And a word about daughters, because this chapter has a son's name on it. Your daughter needs hills too, because she isn't fragile. She needs real work, real challenge, real ownership, and a father who doesn't rescue her from every difficulty and who names her strong when she comes down. The hills may look different, but the method doesn't.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A son of sixteen needs a hill that's a man's work with a man watching from farther off: a job that will fire him, a project he owns to the end, a decision his father doesn't override. The takeaway for the father of a teenager is that the hardest part of this age is the distance; he has to move the truck farther from the hill and stay in it anyway.

A man of forty-five is often the father who has given no hill at all, out of love and out of a culture that removes every difficulty, and his son is comfortable and starving. The takeaway for him is that the boy in the basement and the boy taking stupid risks in a truck are the same boy, and that both needed a calf to bring down.

## Well, I figured you could

Hollis has given a lot of hills in sixty years. To his own sons, to grandsons, to the boys in his Sunday school class, to Cole by a truck. I asked him once what the secret was, and he thought about it and said, "Pick the right hill. Stay in the truck. Say the right thing when he comes down." Then he laughed and said, "And don't tell his mother about the snakes." Paul told a young man he'd fathered in the faith to expect hills for the rest of his life.

:::margin
2 Timothy 2:3 (NIV)
"Join with me in suffering, like a good soldier of Christ Jesus."
:::

Like a good soldier. Not a protected boy, but a man who has been trained on hills and can take the weight. That's what you're building. Not a boy who had a good childhood, but a man who can carry a calf down a caprock when nobody comes.

Your son is standing at the bottom looking up. He isn't sure he can. He's looking back at the truck.

Roll the window down, and watch him, and stay where you are.

## Before you turn the page

Staying in the truck is the hardest thing in fathering, and if you've been getting out of it for years, I don't want you to feel like you've ruined your son. Boys are resilient, and a hill handed to a fifteen-year-old who never had one still counts, and so does one handed to a twenty-five-year-old. The Father who put the wood on Isaac's back and walked up the mountain beside him knows exactly how it feels to watch a son carry something heavy. May He give you the nerve to pick the right hill this week, and the harder nerve to roll the window down and stay, and may you get to say "I figured you could" when he comes down.
