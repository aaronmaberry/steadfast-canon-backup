# 19. Two Men, Two Ways

There were two gardens, and two men, and two nights when everything was on the line.

In the first garden the man had everything and lost it in an afternoon without saying a word. In the second garden the Man had nothing left but a choice, and He made it with His face in the dirt, and it saved the world.

Everything you've ever done as a man is a version of one of those two nights, and I mean that literally. Every time you've stood silent when you should have spoken, every time you've blamed when you should have owned, every time you've hidden when you should have stood up, every time you've grabbed the thing in front of you instead of trusting the thing that was promised, you were in the first garden doing what the first man did. And every time you've done the opposite, on a Tuesday, when it cost you something, you were following the second Man into the second garden. The Bible puts the whole human story on two men, not on two teams or two philosophies, but on two men.

:::margin
Romans 5:19 (NIV)
"For just as through the disobedience of the one man the many were made sinners, so also through the obedience of the one man the many will be made righteous."
:::

The one man, and the one man. This chapter is about the two of them, and about which one you're in, and about which one's way you're going to walk this week.

## The first garden

You know the story, but let me tell it the way a man ought to hear it.

The serpent comes into the garden. He doesn't go to the man. He goes to the woman, and he starts asking questions about the word God gave, the one about the tree. He twists it, and he suggests God's holding out on them. He tells her she won't die; she'll be like God. Now, where is the man?

:::margin
Genesis 3:6 (NIV)
"When the woman saw that the fruit of the tree was good for food and pleasing to the eye, and also desirable for gaining wisdom, she took some and ate it. She also gave some to her husband, who was with her, and he ate it."
:::

He was with her. We read that in chapter 3 of this book, and I told you then that those three words have been indicting men ever since, and here's why. Remember who received the command. Adam did, alone, from God's own mouth, before the woman existed. He was the one who'd been told to work the garden and to keep it, to guard it. And when the thing he was supposed to guard against came through the fence and started twisting the word he'd been given, he stood there.

He didn't argue with the serpent. He didn't say, "That isn't what God said." He didn't take his wife by the hand and walk away from the tree. He did nothing at all. He watched, and then, when she handed it to him, he ate. The first sin of the first man wasn't grabbing the fruit. It was silence while the word was twisted, which is passivity, the first mark inverted before any of the others. Then God comes walking, and they hide, and God asks where he is, and the man answers.

:::margin
Genesis 3:10 (NIV)
"He answered, 'I heard you in the garden, and I was afraid because I was naked; so I hid.'"
:::

I was afraid, so I hid. The man who was naked and unashamed a page earlier is now ashamed and hiding. That's the third mark inverted: cowardice instead of courage. He heard God's voice, the voice he'd walked with in the cool of the day, and instead of going toward it he went into the trees.

Then God asks what he did, and he says the sentence we've already talked about: the woman you put here, she gave it to me. He blames his wife and he blames God and he leaves himself out. That's the second mark inverted: responsibility refused.

And under all of it is the fourth. Why did he eat? Because the serpent said "you will be like God," and that was a reward he could have right then, in his hand, instead of the reward of walking with God every evening for the rest of forever. He traded the greater reward for the nearer one. He chose the fruit over the Father.

Four marks, four inversions, in one afternoon. Passive, evasive, hiding, and grabbing. That's the first man's way, and it's the default setting of every man since, and if you've read the first seventeen chapters of this book you've seen your own face in it more than once, and so have I.

And then the curse. Thorns in the work, pain in the bearing, death at the end, exile from the garden. The man who was made to keep the garden is put outside the fence, with an angel guarding the gate against him.

But even there, two things happen. God makes them clothes with His own hands, from an animal that had to die to provide them. The first covering came at the cost of a life; hold onto that. And God says a word to the serpent in the middle of the curse that turns out to be a promise.

:::margin
Genesis 3:15 (NIV)
"And I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will crush your head, and you will strike his heel."
:::

He will crush your head, which means somebody is coming: a son of the woman who will do what the first man didn't, who will stand in a garden when the enemy comes and will not be silent. It will cost him, since you will strike his heel. But he'll crush the thing that got in through the fence.

The second Man was promised in the first garden, and everything between Genesis 3 and the Gospels is the world waiting for Him.

## The second garden

It's night, in a garden on a hillside outside Jerusalem, olive trees, a place He had gone many times before. He's brought His men, and He's taken three of them farther in and told them to stay and watch, and then He goes on alone and falls on His face.

:::margin
Matthew 26:39 (NIV)
"Going a little farther, he fell with his face to the ground and prayed, 'My Father, if it is possible, may this cup be taken from me. Yet not as I will, but as you will.'"
:::

Look at what's on the table. The first man was offered fruit that would make him like God. The second Man is offered a cup that will make Him like a criminal, and He doesn't want it, and He says so. Every honest fiber in Him is asking for another way. This isn't a Man who felt nothing. This is a Man sweating blood.

And then: not as I will, but as You will. That's the second mark. The first man wouldn't carry the word he was given. The second Man picks up a cup He didn't fill, for a sin He didn't commit, and says that it's His and He'll carry it. He accepts responsibility for the whole broken garden, all of it, every locked drawer and every slammed door and every silence, and He does it on His knees with dirt on His face.

He prays three times. The men He asked to watch fall asleep every time. He wakes them and they fall asleep again. He is completely alone, and He does not go into the trees. He does the opposite.

:::margin
Matthew 26:46 (NIV)
"Rise! Let us go! Here comes my betrayer!"
:::

The first man heard God coming and hid. The second Man hears the mob coming, torches and swords and a friend about to kiss Him, and He stands up and walks toward it. John tells it even more sharply.

:::margin
John 18:4 (NIV)
"Jesus, knowing all that was going to happen to him, went out and asked them, 'Who is it you want?'"
:::

He went out, John says. He didn't wait for them to find Him among the trees; He went out to meet them, knowing. That's the third mark. That's going first, into the worst thing, with nobody following and the men who'd promised to die with Him about to run. Courage isn't the absence of fear; we said that back in chapter 6, and here's the proof. He was afraid enough to sweat blood, and He went out anyway. And while they're arresting Him, He does something the first man never thought to do. He covers His men.

:::margin
John 18:8 (NIV)
"'I told you that I am he,' Jesus answered. 'If you are looking for me, then let these men go.'"
:::

Let these men go. The first man, caught, pointed at his wife. The second Man, caught, points at Himself and tells them to leave everybody else alone. That's what responsibility looks like when it's finished: a man standing between the danger and the people he's been given, saying "me, not them."

And why did He do it? What was He working for? The first man grabbed for a reward he could have that afternoon. The second Man had one in view too, but it lay on the other side of a cross. The writer of Hebrews says He endured it for the joy set before Him. And when it was done, when He'd carried the whole thing to the end, He said one last thing about the work.

:::margin
John 19:30 (NIV)
"When he had received the drink, Jesus said, 'It is finished.' With that, he bowed his head and gave up his spirit."
:::

It is finished, He said, and notice the contrast. The first man's last recorded words before the exile were an excuse. The second Man's last words before death were a completion report. The work I was given, I did, all of it. That's the fourth mark. He wasn't living for the crowd, which had turned on Him. He wasn't living for His men, who had fled. He was living for the Father's "well done," and He got it three days later, and then some.

:::margin
Philippians 2:9-11 (NIV)
"Therefore God exalted him to the highest place and gave him the name that is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue acknowledge that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father."
:::

Therefore, Paul says, and the word means because He did it, because He picked up the cup and went out to meet them and covered His men and finished. The reward came, and it was the greatest reward ever given, and it was given to a Man who looked like a loser to everyone who watched Him die.

## Two ways, laid side by side

Let me set them next to each other, because you're going to be standing in a garden this week and you need to see the two paths clearly.

The first man was silent when the word was twisted. The second Man spoke, even to the mob: "Who is it you want?"

The first man let the danger come to his wife and watched. The second Man went out to meet the danger before it reached His men.

The first man, caught, said "she did it." The second Man, caught, said "take me, let them go."

The first man hid from God's voice in the trees. The second Man sought the Father's face in the dirt, three times, and then got up.

The first man grabbed the reward in his hand. The second Man trusted the reward on the far side of the cross.

The first man's last words were an excuse. The second Man's were "it is finished."

And here's what I want you to notice about the four marks now. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. When we laid those out in chapter 3, they might have sounded like a list of qualities, the kind of thing you'd find on any shelf of books about men, next to the ones about wildness and adventure and finding your heart. They aren't that at all. They are the shape of the second Man in the second garden, written out so a man can carry it in his pocket. Rejecting passivity is "Rise, let us go." Accepting responsibility is "not as I will, but as you will." Leading courageously is "went out and asked them." Expecting the greater reward is "for the joy set before him." The marks aren't a personality profile. They're a portrait of Jesus in the worst hour of His life, and every time you live one of them on a Tuesday, you're wearing His face.

## Which man you're in

Now the part that matters most, and it's the part men skip when they turn the two gardens into a pep talk. The Bible doesn't only say "be like the second man and not the first." It says something stranger and better. It says you're in one of them.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 15:22 (NIV)
"For as in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive."
:::

In Adam, and in Christ. You were born in the first man. His way is your default. That's why passivity comes so easily, why blame is the first thing out of your mouth, why hiding feels natural and grabbing feels right. It isn't only that you copy Adam. You were in him when he did it, the way a family is in a father. His garden is your inheritance, and there's no amount of trying that gets you out of it.

But God isn't asking you to try your way out. He's offering to move you. Into the second Man, into Christ, so that what He did in His garden counts as yours the way what Adam did counted as yours. His obedience becomes your record. His "it is finished" becomes the last word over your life instead of the excuse. That's the gospel, and it's the only reason a man can read the first half of this chapter without despair.

:::margin
Galatians 2:20 (NIV)
"I have been crucified with Christ and I no longer live, but Christ lives in me. The life I now live in the body, I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me."
:::

Christ lives in me. That isn't a metaphor for trying hard. It's a change of location. A man in Christ has the second Man's Spirit in him, the same Spirit that got up off the ground in Gethsemane, and that's why he can get up off his own. Not because he's stronger than Adam, but because he isn't in Adam anymore.

So if you've never done it, do it now, not at the end of the chapter. Tell God you've been in the first man's garden your whole life, doing his silence and his blame and his hiding and his grabbing, and that you're done. Tell Him you want to be in the second Man, that you're trusting what He did in His garden to cover what you did in yours. That's the door, and it's the only one, and it's open, and it's the beginning of everything else in this book actually working.

And if you've already done it, then hear the rest, because you're in Him, and the four marks aren't a standard you're trying to reach so that He'll accept you. They're the family resemblance showing up, because you're His.

## Tuesday's garden

I told you you'd stand in a garden this week, so let me tell you what it will look like, because it won't have olive trees in it.

It'll be a kitchen. Your wife says something that isn't quite fair, and every fiber in you wants to go quiet and let it sit, or to fire back with the thing you've been saving. That kitchen is the garden. The first man's way is silence or blame. The second Man's way is a soft, clear word: "That landed hard. Can we talk about it after the kids are down?" Rise, let us go.

It'll be a phone at eleven at night with nobody watching, and that phone is the garden. The first man grabbed what was in his hand. The second Man trusted the reward on the far side of a hard night. Put it on the charger in the kitchen and go to bed, for the joy set before you.

It'll be a mess at work that's partly your fault and mostly someone else's, with everybody waiting to see who gets blamed, and that meeting is the garden. The first man said "she did it." The second Man said "let these men go." Own your piece first, out loud, before anyone asks.

It'll be two in the morning with the baby crying, and you can hear your wife starting to move, and you could simply lie there, and that dark bedroom is the garden, and the sentence for it is not as I will.

It'll be your father's driveway and a conversation you've been avoiding for ten years, and that driveway is the garden, where the second Man went out and asked them.

The gardens are small, and that's the point of the Tuesday spine this whole book runs on. Adam's afternoon looked small too; it was one piece of fruit. Gethsemane looked like one man praying alone in the dark. The size of the garden has nothing to do with the size of what's decided in it.

## The two men in every season

If you're single, notice that the second Man was single. He stood in His garden without a wife, and He was the fullest man who ever lived. Don't let anyone, including yourself, tell you that your manhood is on hold until you're married. The garden is wherever you're standing, so stand in it.

If there's a baby in your house, Gethsemane is a two-in-the-morning story. A Man who didn't want to, who asked for another way, who got up and did it anyway because the Father asked. You're going to pray "may this cup be taken" a great deal this year, about sleep and about your marriage and about your own patience. Pray it; He did. Then say the second sentence and get up.

If you're divorced, look hard at "let these men go." The first man's way after a marriage breaks is to point: at her, at the lawyers, at the God who put her here. The second Man's way is to stand between the wreckage and the people you've been given and say "me, not them." Your children should never have to carry your war, so cover them, and own your part out loud, and let God sort hers.

And if you're older, think about last words. The first man's were an excuse. The second Man's were "it is finished." You have a stretch of road left, and you get to decide what the completion report says: not "it wasn't my fault," but "the work You gave me, I did." Roy told me he'd started praying for exactly that. "Let me finish. Whatever's left, let me finish it." That is a Gethsemane prayer from a man in his seventies, and it's exactly right.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A young man of twenty is most tempted by the first man's grabbing: the reward he can have tonight instead of the one that's promised. Eli's ten o'clock phone is Adam's fruit, and the takeaway for him is that the second Man trusted a reward on the far side of a hard night, and that putting the phone in the kitchen is a Gethsemane-sized act done in a one-bedroom apartment.

A man of forty-five is most tempted by the first man's blame, pointing at his wife or his boss or his father when the mess is partly his. Anthony's kitchen was Adam's excuse until the night he laid the sentence down. The takeaway for him is that the second Man said "let these men go" while being arrested, and that a husband who says "that's mine" first has walked out of the trees.

## The last Adam

Paul gave Jesus a title I've come to love.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 15:45 (NIV)
"So it is written: 'The first man Adam became a living being'; the last Adam, a life-giving spirit."
:::

The last Adam, Paul calls Him, and not the second in a series but the last, the one who finally did what the first was supposed to do, in a garden, when the enemy came through the fence. There will never be another one, because there doesn't need to be, because He finished it. And He isn't asking you to be Him. He's asking you to be in Him, and then to walk His way through your Tuesday gardens with His Spirit in you and His record over you. Two men, two ways. You were born in one. You've been moved into the other. Now go stand in your garden like the Man you're in.

## Before you turn the page

I've laid the two men side by side, and I know the first one looks a great deal like you, because he looks a great deal like me. I want you to hear the gospel one more time before we leave this garden. You were born in the first man, and his way is your default, and no amount of trying gets you out of him. But God has offered to move you into the second Man, so that His "it is finished" becomes the last word over your life instead of the excuse. If you've never asked Him to, ask Him tonight. If you have, then go stand in your Tuesday garden as a man who's already been moved. May the Father who raised the last Adam raise you up off the ground this week, and may you go out to meet what's coming with His Spirit in you and His record over you.
