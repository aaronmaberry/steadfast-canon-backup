# 14. Ask the Man

When I was young, I figured a long marriage could be explained, if you could just catch the right man and ask him. One Sunday after church I caught Harlan at the door of his truck. He'd been married longer than I'd been alive. I still don't know where I found the nerve.

He looked at me long enough to make up his mind. Then he said, "Ride with me Saturday."

That Saturday I climbed into the passenger seat of the truck he hauled feed in, and we ran his route. Around noon he stopped for fuel and walked over to the pay phone. He told his wife what town he was in and what time he'd be pulling into the driveway. I found out later he had made that call every working day since his wedding. I rode with him two more Saturdays that summer. I learned more about marriage in that cab than in every sermon I'd heard on it.

There's a man about twenty years farther down the road you're on. He has already been through what you're walking into. He's closer than you think, and the asking is up to you.

## Two men, both waiting

The young man has his reasons for keeping quiet. To him, the older man looks too busy to bother. Being a man, he tells himself, means sorting out his own mess. Or his father owed him these lessons and never paid, and asking another man would mean admitting it. The older man has his reasons too. He figures an old man's opinion is the last thing a young man wants. Or he's made so many mistakes that he can't imagine any of them being worth passing on. So they wait each other out, and boys become men with nobody twenty years ahead to tell them about the next mile.

Scripture draws a chain. Joshua had Moses ahead of him. Elisha had Elijah. Timothy had Paul, and Paul wouldn't let the chain stop with Timothy.

:::margin
2 Timothy 2:2 (NIV)
"And the things you have heard me say in the presence of many witnesses entrust to reliable people who will also be qualified to teach others."
:::

Count the generations in that one sentence. Paul, then Timothy, then the reliable people, and then the ones they'll teach. That's four. In plenty of churches and plenty of houses, both ends of the chain are empty. A brother knows your trouble because he's in the middle of the same thing. The man ahead knows it because he has come out the other side, and he can tell you what's over there. You need both of them. One can't stand in for the other.

## What to ask for

Don't lead with the word mentor. It sounds like a job title. An older man hears a promise he may not be able to keep and a title he's not sure he's earned, so he says no, or he says yes and then disappears.

Ask something narrow. What has held your marriage together all these years? He can answer in words, or he can show you, the way Harlan did. How did you know it was time to leave that job? What would you do differently with your kids if you had those years back? What did the year after the divorce take out of you? Can I watch you work?

One question is a complete ask. A good hour earns you another question next week. If he offers the truck, go ready to be useful. If you want to put a fence around it, ask for twenty minutes. "Could we take twenty minutes a week, by phone, for the next two months? Some things are still cloudy from where I'm standing. I doubt they're cloudy from where you are."

## How to pick him

Find a man about twenty years ahead of you who is living the four marks. Leave the impressive man for the people who need impressing. You need a man who took a road like yours and is still standing at the far end of it, where you can see him.

Watch his wife's face while he talks. Decades with a real man and decades with a performance put different looks on a woman's face, and she isn't confused about which one she has. Be wary of a man who only tells you about his wins. Either he's hiding the losses or he never noticed them, and either way he can't take you where you need to go. You came for his scars, and for the noon phone call he still makes.

Pick a man whose life at his age you'd be glad to have, in the things that will still matter then, and pick a man you can actually reach. If no name comes to mind, you're looking too high. He might be the deacon everybody thanks and nobody asks. He might be the man on the back pew who has been married longer than you've been alive and hasn't had a straight talk with anyone half his age in years. He might be the retired man who hangs around the hardware counter. There are more of them around you than your excuse allows, and your silence is part of what they've been living with.

## Once you've asked

Bring a real question every time. An older man will keep opening his life to a man who does. If you only come around to hang out, he'll drift away. Asking him something real is what honors him.

Then do what he told you, at least once, and come back and tell him what happened. Older men have poured counsel into young men who nodded and changed nothing, and they learned to stop. Act on it and report back, and he'll give you everything he has.

Don't try to make him your father. That weight will crush him. He can give you a great deal, but he can't fill the particular hole your father left. Demand that, and you'll wear him out and leave disappointed.

Thank him out loud, and be specific. "I did what you said about my dad, and something moved." A man his age can go a long time without hearing a sentence that specific.

## To the man who's ahead

Half of you are the older man.

The man he's missing is you. You're unfinished, and that's normal. Nobody graduates. Paul wrote from a prison cell, years into his ministry, that he hadn't arrived yet and was still pressing on toward the goal. A younger man is just now walking into country you've already crossed, washouts and all. He doesn't need a spotless record. He needs the truth about where you failed, and he needs the ordinary habits, kept on ordinary days, that held your house together anyway.

:::margin
Titus 2:6-7 (NIV)
"Similarly, encourage the young men to be self-controlled. In everything set them an example by doing what is good."
:::

An example, with a word beside it, in an ordinary week. That's the whole plan. You don't need a program.

He won't start it. You will. He's decided you're too busy, and you make him a little nervous. Give him an easy yes. "Saturday I'm hanging a gate, and I could use a second man, if you want to learn how." Once he's there, skip the speech. Ask him what work is doing to him, how things are with her, and what's heavy. When an older man asks and then leaves him some quiet, a young man will say things he'd never say to a friend his own age.

Tell him where you failed. Let him hear a man he respects admit that he nearly lost his wife, and that it was his own fault. Something opens up in the young man, and he can stop performing.

Work on the same side of a job. Shared work teaches more than a table does. Keep it small. Twenty minutes a week, or a job together every other Saturday. A young man who never had anyone reliable will learn more from eleven straight weeks of you showing up than from the best talk you give on any one of them.

You'll get more than you give. The older men who stay with this all end up telling me the same thing. "I thought I was the one helping. Mostly he was keeping me alive." If the badge or the hard hat is hanging on a hook now and the mornings are quiet, this is what they're for. God sets the lonely in families, and sometimes He does it by putting an old man and a young one in the same truck.

## The man ahead, in every season

A single man should ask a husband how a marriage is kept, and he should ask before he has a wife. A year of questions beats three years of learning by wreck. If there's a baby in your house, find a father whose kids are grown, and ask what he'd change about the little years while you can still use the answer. If you're divorced, shame will tell you to skip this. You need a man ahead as much as anyone here. Find one who has lived through a marriage ending, or a long-married man who won't treat you like an exhibit, and ask him to walk the next stretch with you. In the middle of life, a man faces both directions. There's a man ahead he's never asked about a house that has gone quiet, and there's a younger man behind him, watching his own marriage fray, whom he's never said a word to. The chain needs him looking both ways. And a man whose own dad never did this for him isn't ruled out. The life he's lived is what he has to give.

## Ride with me

That first summer turned into years of Saturdays in Harlan's truck. I asked him hundreds of questions. Plenty of times his answer was to downshift and say nothing for a mile. He's been gone a while now. On my wedding day he sat in the last pew. Afterward he took my hand and said, "Well. You paid attention."

I asked, and he told me to get in. Neither of us stood around waiting for the other one to go first.

Ask him. Later, be him. The chain won't mend itself.

## Before you turn the page

The broken links aren't a private guilt you have to fix before you're allowed to move. The men reading this are the repair. If you're younger, bring one honest question. You'll feel foolish for about eight seconds, the same gap "Go First" told you to expect. Then go back the next week. If you're older, offer the seat. May the Father set a Harlan near you, make you that man for someone coming up behind you, and show you both how thin the busy excuse always was.
