# Introduction

I've spent most of my adult life sitting across tables from men. Kitchen tables, mostly. The folding ones at the back of a church fellowship hall. Tailgates, truck cabs, and the breakfast counter at a café where the waitress has your order on the ticket before you sit down, which in this part of the world serves the same purpose. Some of those men were young enough to be my sons and some were old enough to be my father, and underneath the different jobs and decades and troubles, they were all asking me the same thing. They wanted to know what a man is, whether they were one, and if not, whether it was too late.

This book is my long answer. It isn't the only answer and I won't pretend it is, but it's the one I've watched hold up in the lives of real men over a lot of years, and I've tried to write it the way I'd say it to you if you were sitting across from me with your hands around a cup of coffee going cold.

## Who I'm writing to

A man in every season of life. Find your seat.

Single and still waiting for somebody to call you a man out loud. Newly married, or so deep in the little-ones years that nobody in the house has slept since March. Providing everything except yourself. Divorced and still a father. Alone in the quiet house after the kids left, or retired and wondering what a man does with his mornings, or standing in a kitchen where her coffee cup is still in the drainer. You're all in this room.

I'm not writing only to the man with the mortgage and the minivan. The counsel changes as your season changes, and when it does, I'll turn toward you inside the paragraph and say so. What I won't do is stop every chapter to run the whole roll call again. **Every Season Still Counts**, near the end, is where we sit down and talk about seasons on purpose, all of them in one place. Until then, trust that I know you're here. If the house is loud and the page count looks impossible, start with **A Quiet Tuesday**, keep the closer each week, and come back for the rest when you can. The book will keep.

## What I'm claiming

The center of this book is one sentence. I didn't invent it and I'm not going to pretend I did. Robert Lewis gave it that shape years ago, men have been handing it to one another ever since, and I've never found a better way to say it. I have held it up against my own life for a long time, and against the lives of hundreds of men in every season I just named, and I've never found a place where it lies.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Those are the four marks. **The Four Marks** will explain them, and the chapters after it take them one at a time. For now I only want you to notice what kind of sentence it is. It's not a sentence about what a man feels. There's a whole shelf of books for men that start inside the chest, with your heart and your longings and your wild places, and some of those books were good to me when I was young. I'm not writing against them. But this book makes a plainer claim. The four marks are things you do, on ordinary days, and they show. They mark a man the way thirty years of work marks his hands. You can see them or you can see their absence, and no amount of feeling makes up for either.

## How the book is built

We start with an ordinary day, because that's where a man's life gets decided. **A Quiet Tuesday** names the problem. Then we tell the truth about the room you're in, and we put the four marks in your hands one at a time until you can say them without looking.

From there we go back to what you inherited and what's still yours: the chair at the end of your childhood table, the anger and the drift, the Father who doesn't leave, brothers, the ask, the problem inside. Then further back, to the beginning of the human story, so you quit reading your life like you invented it. Then we walk into the house: the woman, the bed, the daughter, the son. Then the long road: work, feelings, seasons, the years nobody claps for, the next move, and the men who stay with you.

Running through all of it is Tuesday. Not the altar call. Not the retreat. The day with nothing special in it, when nobody's watching. Every chapter lands, somewhere, on a Tuesday-sized picture. That's on purpose.

## The four lines at the end

Every chapter ends the same way, with four short lines, and each line comes with a plain sentence about why it matters for that chapter. I'll explain them once here so I don't have to keep doing it.

**Name it** asks you to say, out loud and in your own words, what the chapter touched in you. Men are good at nodding along and terrible at saying "that's me," and nothing changes in a man until he says it.

**Do this week** gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday. Not a program, not a list. One thing. If you do only that one thing from each chapter, you'll have done more with this book than most men do with any book.

**Tell a brother** asks you to say it to another man. A mark you keep to yourself never hardens into anything. If you don't have a brother yet, **A Man With No Brothers** is for you. Until you get there, telling a brother can mean writing it down where you'll see it and telling God, who has heard worse.

**Carry this** hands you one line to take into the week.

One more thing about those four lines, because I know how a tired man reads a book like this. You're going to get to a chapter having not done the last chapter's thing. When that happens, don't skip it in shame and don't quit the book. Tell your brother you didn't do it, which is itself the tell-a-brother line, and do it this week instead. Every man in these pages has missed a week. The ones who changed are the ones who said so and went again.

## The men you'll meet

You'll meet a number of men in these pages by name. Roy, who turned in a state trooper's badge. Tomás, whose baby doesn't sleep. Kenny, divorced, who calls his son every Tuesday. Micah, who sat at the single men's table at weddings for six years. Others. They're composites. The conversations happened, at real tables with real men, but the names and details have been mixed so nobody can be picked out of a lineup, and so you might recognize yourself in one of them without recognizing anyone else.

## One more thing before you start

This book stands on its own. Read it. Do one thing a week. Find one other man to do it with. That's enough for a man to change.

If there's no other man yet, don't wait on him to start. Keep going. We've got you covered. Write it down, tell God, and let the brother catch up when he shows.

We also built a twenty-four-week training around the same spine: videos, printable guides, and a class a church or a small group or a man alone can run. In the back of this book you'll find condensed training material keyed to those weeks. If you want the full class, the twenty-four-week guide is at walksteadfast.com. The book is the book. The training is the training. Consume either. Consume both. Just don't binge four chapters on a Saturday and call it finished.

So find your coffee. Pull up a chair. Let me tell you about a Tuesday.
