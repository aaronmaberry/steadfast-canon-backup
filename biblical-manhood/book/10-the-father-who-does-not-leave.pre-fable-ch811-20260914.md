# 10. The Father Who Does Not Leave

Watch a boy at a Little League game and you'll see him do something between innings, or after a hit, or after a strikeout, especially after a strikeout. He scans the bleachers.

He isn't looking at the crowd. He's looking for one face, and you can tell from the dugout which boys find it. Their shoulders drop a quarter of an inch and they stop scanning and they go back to being a kid at a ballgame. You can tell which boys don't find it, too. They scan longer, and they scan every inning, and some of them are still scanning at thirty-eight in a doorway between a garage and a kitchen, not knowing anymore what they're looking for, only that they haven't found it.

I want to tell you in this chapter about a Father you can stop scanning for. He is in the bleachers every inning. He was there before you looked up. He isn't leaving between the fifth and the sixth to take a call. And because I know that for some of you the word "father" is the whole problem, I want to start there.

## The trouble with the word

If I say "God is your Father," what happens inside you depends almost entirely on the chair at the end of your childhood table.

If the chair was empty, "Father" means someone who isn't coming. You'll believe God exists the way you believe your dad exists: somewhere, doing something, not here.

If the chair was full and silent, "Father" means someone who's in the room and has nothing to say to you. You'll pray the way a man talks to a newspaper.

If the chair was dangerous, "Father" means someone whose mood you have to read before you speak. You'll spend your Christian life trying to work out what God is angry about today, and you'll be fairly sure it's you.

If the chair was good, you're ahead of most, but you've inherited limits too. Your father couldn't be everywhere, and he got tired, and he missed things, so somewhere in you is the quiet assumption that God gets tired and misses things.

Here is what I want you to see. You've been reading God through your dad. Every man does it, because the chair at the end of the table was the only picture of a father you had, so you held it up to heaven and assumed that was what was there. And for most men that picture has been quietly poisoning their walk with God for decades, because they've been relating to a Father they built out of an absence or a silence or a storm.

The Bible does the opposite. It tells you to read your dad through God. God is the original and your father was a copy, and copies are always worse than the original, some of them badly. So set the copy down for a minute, and let me show you what the original is like, from the only Man who has seen Him.

:::margin
John 14:9 (NIV)
"Jesus answered: 'Don't you know me, Philip, even after I have been among you such a long time? Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father. How can you say, "Show us the Father"?'"
:::

Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father. If you want to know what God the Father is like, you don't look at your dad. You look at Jesus, and everything Jesus did, the Father is like.

## What He is like

He comes toward you. The most famous story Jesus ever told about a father is about a boy who left, spent everything, and came home stinking of pigs with a speech ready. And the father doesn't wait at the door with his arms folded.

:::margin
Luke 15:20 (NIV)
"So he got up and went to his father. But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion for him; he ran to his son, threw his arms around him and kissed him."
:::

While he was still a long way off. The father had been watching the road; that's the only way you see someone a long way off. He'd been scanning the bleachers too, in his way, and when he saw the face he ran. Grown men in that culture did not run. This one did, and that is your Father. He isn't waiting for you to get it together. He's watching the road. He tells you who you are before you've done anything. When Jesus was baptized, before He had preached a sermon or healed anyone or done a single thing you could put on a résumé, the Father spoke from heaven.

:::margin
Matthew 3:17 (NIV)
"And a voice from heaven said, 'This is my Son, whom I love; with him I am well pleased.'"
:::

My Son, whom I love, with whom I am well pleased: those are three things every boy needs to hear from his father, and Jesus heard them at the start rather than at the finish. That's the order the Father works in. He blesses first and then He sends you to work. Your own father may have had it backward, making you earn what should have been given, and the Father doesn't. If you're in Christ, that voice is speaking over you right now, on a Tuesday, before you've done a thing. He carries you, and Moses, looking back over forty years of wilderness, reached for a picture that any father would recognize.

:::margin
Deuteronomy 1:31 (NIV)
"and in the wilderness. There you saw how the LORD your God carried you, as a father carries his son, all the way you went until you reached this place."
:::

As a father carries his son. You've seen it: a man with a sleeping boy over his shoulder, coming in from the truck, careful on the steps so he won't wake him. All the way you went, Moses says, and not part of the way, and not until you got too heavy. He knows what you need before you ask. Jesus said so to men who were anxious about food and clothes and tomorrow.

:::margin
Matthew 6:8 (NIV)
"Do not be like them, for your Father knows what you need before you ask him."
:::

The man who learned from the empty chair that needing things is dangerous needs to sit with that sentence for a long time. You don't have to hide the need. He already sees it, and He isn't put off by it, because He's a Father. He disciplines you, because you're His. I won't pretend this one is comfortable, but a man with a dangerous father needs to hear it more than anyone, because he has discipline and rage confused in his memory.

:::margin
Hebrews 12:7 (NIV)
"Endure hardship as discipline; God is treating you as his children. For what children are not disciplined by their father?"
:::

God is treating you as his children. The hard season isn't proof that He left. It's proof that He stayed. A father who doesn't care lets a boy do whatever he wants. Your Father cares enough to shape you, and He never does it in a rage, and He never does it to make you small. He does it to make you like His Son. And He does not leave. This is the one the whole chapter hangs on, so hear it in His own words.

:::margin
Hebrews 13:5 (NIV)
"Keep your lives free from the love of money and be content with what you have, because God has said, 'Never will I leave you; never will I forsake you.'"
:::

Jesus said it a different way the night before He died, to men who were about to watch Him taken and were about to scatter.

:::margin
John 14:18 (NIV)
"I will not leave you as orphans; I will come to you."
:::

Orphans is the word He chose, because He knew that's what they'd feel like, and He knows that's what some of you have felt like since you were nine years old. And He says: not you. Not on my watch. I will come to you.

## The hardest thing for a man to do

Now I have to tell you the truth about why this chapter, which ought to be the easiest one in the book, is actually the hardest.

A man who has fathered himself since he was a boy doesn't know how to be fathered. It isn't that he doesn't believe God is a Father; he'd tell you he does. It's that when it comes to actually being carried, actually being told who he is, actually letting somebody else hold the weight, every muscle in him locks up. He has been the corner post so long that leaning on anything feels like falling.

Anthony said it exactly. "I know God's my Father. I just don't know what to do with that. I don't know how to let Him be one. I never learned how to be somebody's son."

That's the honest sentence, and I'd rather you say it than fake something you don't feel.

Here is what I've watched work in men who learned to be sons late. Call Him Father out loud. Not "God" and not "Lord," but Father, in the truck, alone, when it's awkward. Paul says the Spirit inside you is already doing this.

:::margin
Romans 8:15 (NIV)
"The Spirit you received does not make you slaves, so that you live in fear again; rather, the Spirit you received brought about your adoption to sonship. And by him we cry, 'Abba, Father.'"
:::

Abba is the word a small boy used. You're allowed to use it. It will feel strange in your mouth for a while. Say it until it doesn't.

Bring Him your exit. Whatever your home exit is, anger or the itch or the drift, take it to Him first, before the brother and before the counselor, and don't clean it up on the way. "Father, I'm about to blow up at my kids and I don't know why." "Father, I'm reaching for my phone again and I hate it." "Father, I've been gone and I don't know how to come back." He isn't disgusted. He's the father watching the road.

Let Him tell you who you are. Every day, read something He said about His sons and say it back to Him as true about you, not as a mantra but the way a boy repeats what his dad just told him. "You said I'm your son. You said you love me. You said you're pleased with me because I'm in Christ." A man who was never told who he was needs to be told many times, and the Father is patient.

Let yourself be carried. This is the one that will cost you. Somewhere this year something is going to be too heavy, and your instinct will be to grit it out alone the way you always have. Don't do it. Say, out loud, "I can't carry this. Carry me." Then let a brother help. Then let your wife see that you need help. That isn't you failing to be a man. That's you finally learning to be a son, which every real man is first.

And stay in the house. A son who's learning to be fathered doesn't wander off. He's in the Word, where the Father talks. He's in the church, where the Father's other children are. He's in prayer, where the Father listens. A man who only visits the house on holidays never learns what the Father's voice sounds like on a Tuesday.

## What this does to your other father

Here's something I didn't expect when I started walking this road with men. Once a man begins to be fathered by God, he starts to see his own father differently.

Not more softly, necessarily. Sometimes more clearly, which is harder. But he stops needing his father to be something the man never was, because he's getting it from Somebody else, and the scanning stops. A man who has stopped scanning can look at his dad, whatever kind of chair he sat in, with a kind of steadiness. He can honor what was good. He can name what wasn't without rage. He can forgive, because he isn't waiting for a payment anymore. He may even be able to reach toward the man, which is what chapter 12 is about.

None of that is possible while you're still trying to get from your dad what only your Father can give. The chair at the end of your table can't fill the chair in your chest. It was never big enough, and only He is.

## Learning it in your season

Let me turn toward a few of you, because sonship gets learned at different ages and it's never learned too late.

If you're young, learn this now, because the men who find the Father at twenty-five are spared thirty years of scanning. Don't wait until you have a family to start being a son.

If you've just had a baby, you're about to understand something about the Father that you couldn't have understood before. You'll look at your child asleep and feel a love that has nothing to do with anything he's done, because he hasn't done anything; he's simply yours, and that is how the Father looks at you. Let the baby teach you.

If you're divorced, the Father didn't leave when your marriage did. I know it felt as though He had. Some of you stopped praying because you figured He was as disappointed in you as everyone else seemed to be. He was watching the road the whole time, and He's watching it now. Come home.

And if you're older, it isn't too late to be somebody's son. Roy learned to call God Father at sixty-eight, in his truck, in a parking lot, because he'd never once done it in sixty-eight years and decided that was long enough. "It felt like a fool," he told me. "Then it felt like coming in out of the wind."

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A young man of twenty is at the best age to learn it and the least likely to think he needs to. He's just gotten free of his parents and the last thing he wants is another father. But the men who find the Father at twenty are spared thirty years of scanning bleachers, and the takeaway for him is that calling God "Father" out loud in a parked car at twenty is the single most useful thing he could do this year, more useful than any career decision.

A man of forty-five learns it when he finally admits that he's been praying to a newspaper, or to a storm, or to an empty seat, and that his whole walk with God has been shaped by a copy instead of the original. Anthony's honest sentence was that he'd never learned to be somebody's son. The takeaway for him is that the sentence is the start, and that a man who says it out loud to God and to a brother has begun to be fathered.

## Stop scanning

Back to the boy in the dugout. He's looking for one face, and when he finds it he can go back to being a kid. He can strike out. He can boot a grounder. He can get a hit and not have to look up to see whether anybody noticed, because he already knows Somebody did.

That's what the Father does for a man. He lets you stop scanning and stop auditioning. He lets you fail on a Tuesday and get up on Wednesday, because your standing with Him was never about the Tuesday. It was about the Son, and it's settled.

:::margin
1 John 3:1 (NIV)
"See what great love the Father has lavished on us, that we should be called children of God! And that is what we are! The reason the world does not know us is that it did not know him."
:::

And that is what we are. Not what we're trying to become. What we are: sons.

He's in the bleachers. He has been there every inning. Drop your shoulders and go back to the game.

## Before you turn the page

I've asked you to do something in this chapter that may be the hardest thing in the book, which is to let yourself be carried by Someone after a lifetime of carrying yourself. I don't expect it to come easily, and neither does He. He's a Father, and fathers know that sons learn slowly. So say the word tomorrow morning, badly, and then again the next day, and let Him do the rest. May the Father who watched the road for the prodigal watch it for you, and may you drop your shoulders a quarter of an inch this week and go back to the game.
