# 10. The Father Who Does Not Leave

Stand outside a grade school when the bell lets out at three and watch where the children's eyes go. Every one of them is hunting for one car. From the sidewalk you can tell who has found it. The backpack loosens on the shoulders. The search stops. They're already talking before the car door is all the way open. A few keep looking. Car after car goes by that isn't theirs, and one child ends up on the curb beside a teacher who checks her phone while the line thins out to nothing.

Curtis has held a spot in that line every school day for years. He gets there twenty minutes early and turns the radio off. He swore no child of his would ever be the one left on the curb with the teacher, and he has kept that promise. But he has never once been the child who comes through those doors and finds his own ride waiting. Nobody was ever in the line for him. He learned the walk home.

This chapter is about a Father who ends the search. He's already in the line when the bell rings. For some of you, the trouble starts with the word father, so that's where I'll start.

## The trouble with the word

When somebody tells you God is your Father, what you hear depends on who sat at the head of your childhood table.

If the chair was empty, you learned that a father is someone who doesn't show up. So you believe in God about the way you believe in your dad. He's out there somewhere, busy with something else. He isn't here.

If a man sat in the chair and never had a word for you, you learned that a father can share a room with you and still have nothing to say. Your prayers go out like words spoken at a page that never answers.

If the chair was dangerous, you learned to read the weather before you risked a word. The Christian life turns into a daily investigation of what God is angry about, and you're fairly sure the answer is you.

If the chair was good, you started out ahead of most of the room. Even so, that father got tired and missed things, and somewhere under your prayers is a hunch that God will miss them too.

You've been reading heaven through your dad. His was the only picture of a father you had, so you held it up to God and assumed the two matched. I've done it myself, for longer than I like to admit. Scripture turns the pictures around. God is the original, and your father was the copy. Set the copy down. Look at the original through the only Man who has seen Him.

:::margin
John 14:9 (NIV)
"Jesus answered: 'Don't you know me, Philip, even after I have been among you such a long time? Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father. How can you say, "Show us the Father"?'"
:::

Once you've met Jesus, you know what the Father is like.

## What He is like

He comes toward you. Jesus told a story about a father whose son left home, spent his whole inheritance, and came back smelling like the pigs he'd been feeding, with a speech rehearsed. Watch the father. He's already out on the road.

:::margin
Luke 15:20 (NIV)
"So he got up and went to his father. But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion for him; he ran to his son, threw his arms around him and kissed him."
:::

A long way off. You only see someone at that distance if you've been watching the road for a while. In that culture a grown man didn't run. It cost him his dignity. This father ran anyway. He didn't make his son get himself together first.

He tells you who you are before you've done anything. At the Jordan, before Jesus had preached a sermon or healed a single person, the Father spoke.

:::margin
Matthew 3:17 (NIV)
"And a voice from heaven said, 'This is my Son, whom I love; with him I am well pleased.'"
:::

A boy needs to hear three things from his father. You're mine. I love you. I'm pleased with you. Jesus heard all three at the start. That's the Father's order. The blessing comes first, and the work is handed out after. Your own father may have made you earn words that should have been a gift. This Father gives them. If you belong to Christ, they've already been spoken over you.

He carries you. Moses reminded Israel that the Lord had carried them through the desert the way a father carries his son, all the way to where they stood. He knows what you need before you ask, and your needing doesn't put Him off. Stay with that for a while if needing someone was dangerous in your house. When He disciplines you, it's because you're His. Hebrews treats a hard season as proof that you're a son. Discipline and rage were never the same thing. A hard season means He stayed.

And He stays. He has promised never to leave you and never to forsake you. The night before the cross, Jesus gave that promise another shape for men who were about to watch Him taken away.

:::margin
John 14:18 (NIV)
"I will not leave you as orphans; I will come to you."
:::

He used the word orphans because that's exactly how those men were about to feel. Some of you have felt that way since you were a boy. The promise is that He comes.

## The hardest thing for a man to do

This ought to be the easy chapter. For a lot of men it's the hardest one. If you've been fathering yourself since boyhood, you've never practiced being a son. You can believe every word of the doctrine and still lock up when it's time to let someone else take the weight. When you've stood alone long enough, leaning on anyone feels like falling.

Curtis said the honest version out loud. "I know God's my Father. I don't know how to be anybody's son. Nobody ever showed me the receiving end." I'll take that sentence over a polished one any day. Men who learn to be sons late in life usually get there through the same few practices.

Say Father where you can hear it. Keep God and Lord for your other prayers if you like, but give this one the name. Say it alone, while it still feels awkward. Paul writes that the Spirit living in you cries out "Abba, Father," and Abba is the word a small boy uses. You're allowed to use it. It will feel strange. Keep at it until the strangeness wears thin.

Bring Him your exit, whether it's anger, the itch, or the drift. He hears about it before your brother does, and before any counselor, and He can hear it unpolished. "Father, I'm about to blow up at my kids." "Father, I'm reaching for the phone again." The One you're talking to has been watching the road.

Let Him tell you who you are. Every day, read something He has said about His sons, and answer it as if it's true of you, because it is. A man who was never told his name needs to hear it more than once. The Father has the patience to keep telling him.

Let Him carry you, for real. Sometime this year, something will be too heavy for you, and your reflex will be to grind it out alone. Say the other sentence out loud. "I can't carry this. Carry me." Then let a brother help. A real man is a son before he's anything else. Learning that order is the work.

Stay in the house. His Word is where He talks. His church is where His other children gather. Prayer is where He listens. If you only come around on holidays, you'll never learn His voice on an ordinary afternoon.

## What this does to your other father

Once God is fathering you, you see the man who raised you, or didn't, a different way. Sometimes you see him more clearly, and clearer can cost more. You stop trying to force him into a shape he never had, and you stop scanning him for what he never gave. You can honor what was good in him and name what was harmful. You can set the rage down. Forgiveness becomes possible once you've quit waiting to be paid back. You may even move toward him, and "Leave Without Slamming the Door" walks that road with you. But none of those moves open up while you're still trying to get from your dad what only your Father can give. No man at the head of any table could have filled the place you've been asking him to fill. That place was made for God.

## Learning it in your season

If you learn this while you're young, when you're least convinced you need another father, you'll be spared decades of scanning. A new baby teaches it from the crib. The way you love that sleeping child has nothing to do with anything the child has done, and that's the way He looks at you. If your marriage has ended, you need this more than anyone. The Father stayed when the marriage didn't. Some of you quit praying because you were sure His disappointment had joined everyone else's. He was watching the road the whole time. And if you're older and have never once said Father out loud, the word will feel foolish for about a week. Then it will feel warm, the way heat feels after a long time in the cold. You've waited long enough.

## Before the bell

The pickup line became the place where Curtis practiced the word. He was alone in the car, so the awkwardness cost him nothing. Father. At first he might as well have been talking to the windshield. He said one sentence a day and kept at it. Somewhere in the second week the glass stopped being his audience. What took its place was the sense of someone sitting in the seat beside him.

Then came an afternoon when his youngest pushed through the doors, looked down the line, found their car, and stopped looking. Curtis watched it happen and understood what he was being offered. For years he'd been the man who held a place in the line. He'd never once been the child who finds the car. Now he was being told that place was open to him too.

That's the Father's work in a man. The scanning can stop. The audition can stop. Your standing with Him was settled in His Son, and today isn't the verdict. John wrote it this way. "See what great love the Father has lavished on us, that we should be called children of God! And that is what we are!" You're a son, and that's already been said. He was in the line before the bell rang. Let your shoulders down. You've been found.

## Before you turn the page

He knows sons learn slowly. Say the word badly in the morning, and say it again the next morning. May the Father who watched the road for the prodigal watch for you. This week, come out through those doors and find the One who was already waiting in the line.
