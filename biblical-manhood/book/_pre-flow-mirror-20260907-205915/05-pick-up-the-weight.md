# 5. Pick Up the Weight

*Part 1: The man you were built to be*

If you've ever built a fence that had to hold, you know the whole thing depends on the corners.

Line posts you can set shallow and quick. They just hold the wire up off the ground. But a corner post takes the strain of the entire run. Every foot of wire you stretch pulls against it. So you dig it deeper than seems reasonable. You set it in concrete if you've got it. You brace it with an H and a diagonal, and you tamp it until your shoulders burn. And then you never think about it again, because a corner post that does its job is invisible. Nobody drives past a fence and admires the corners. They only notice when one leans.

That's responsibility. That's the second mark. A real man accepts responsibility, and what that means on a Tuesday is that he sets himself at the corner and takes the strain, and mostly nobody notices, and he's alright with that.

## The oldest excuse

When God asked Adam what he'd done, Adam gave the first excuse in human history, and men have been giving versions of it ever since. He said the woman gave him the fruit. And then he went one step further and said the woman God had put there gave it to him. In one sentence, he blamed his wife and his Creator and left himself out entirely.

I'd like to say I've never done that. I've done it in the last month.

Here's what blame is. Blame is a man setting down weight that's his and pointing at whoever is standing closest. It's fast and it works in the moment. It also leaves the weight on the ground, where it stays, and where it grows. Nothing in a man's life has ever gotten better because he correctly identified whose fault it was.

The next generation did it too. When God asked Cain where his brother was, Cain answered with a question that's become a kind of motto for men who don't want to carry anything.

:::margin
Genesis 4:9 (NIV)
"Then the LORD said to Cain, 'Where is your brother Abel?' 'I don't know,' he replied. 'Am I my brother's keeper?'"
:::

Am I my brother's keeper? The whole Bible is going to spend the rest of its pages answering yes. Yes, you are. You're your brother's keeper and your wife's and your kids' and your neighbor's, and not because you caused their trouble, but because you're the man standing there.

That's the piece most men miss about responsibility. They think it means owning your fault. It does include that. But a man who only takes responsibility for what he caused is still measuring by fairness, and fairness is how a boy thinks. A man takes responsibility for what's in front of him. The bill nobody opened. The kid nobody's talking to. The widow down the road whose gutter came off in the storm. The mess in the church parking lot after the thing you didn't even attend. Nobody would blame him for walking past. He picks it up anyway, because that's what a corner post is for.

## What the weight is

"Responsibility" is one of those words that can mean everything and therefore mean nothing.

**Your own life.** Your body. Your money. Your time. What you put in front of your eyes. Whether you're at church or in bed. Whether you've told the truth this week. Before a man can carry anything for anybody else, he has to carry himself, and a shocking number of grown men are still waiting for someone to do that for them. If you're single, this is where the weight starts and it's not a small thing. A man who pays his own rent on time, gets himself to the doctor, keeps his word about small things, and can be counted on to show up when he said he would is already carrying more than a lot of husbands.

**Your house.** If you're married, the state of your marriage is your responsibility. Not fifty percent. Yours. I'm not saying she has no part. I'm saying that you don't get to wait for her part before you carry yours, and a man who keeps a ledger in a marriage has already started losing it. If you've got children, their formation is yours. Not the school's, not the youth pastor's, not their mother's alone. Yours. That doesn't mean you do everything. It means that if it isn't happening, you don't get to say "I don't know," like Cain.

**Your work.** Whatever it is. The job isn't just how you get paid. It's a place where somebody is trusting you with something, and how you handle a trust is how you handle a trust, whether it's a fleet of trucks or a spreadsheet or a classroom or a hospital floor at 3 a.m.

**Your people.** Your church. Your brothers. Your street. The older man at the end of the pew who stopped coming. The younger man who keeps hovering at the edge of the group and hasn't been asked in.

**What you've done wrong.** This one's the heaviest and the fastest to set down. When you've sinned against somebody, the weight is naming it, without a "but," and making it right where you can.

Paul puts two sentences a few lines apart in Galatians that sound like they contradict each other, and they don't.

:::margin
Galatians 6:2 (NIV)
"Carry each other's burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ."
:::

:::margin
Galatians 6:5 (NIV)
"for each one should carry their own load."
:::

Two different words in the original. A burden is something crushing that no one should carry alone. A load is a man's own pack, the daily weight that's his to carry and nobody else's. A real man carries his own load without complaint and reaches for his brother's burden without being asked. A boy does the reverse. He wants help with his pack and looks the other way when his brother goes down.

## The wrong weight

Two ways this mark goes bad, and I've watched both wreck good men.

The first is the man who grabs weight that isn't his. He's got to fix everyone. His grown daughter's marriage. His brother's drinking. Every problem at church. He can't sit in a room where something's wrong without taking it on, and he calls it responsibility, but it's often something else. Sometimes it's control. Sometimes it's a way to feel needed. Sometimes it's a boy who learned early that if he didn't hold the family together, nobody would, and he's still doing it at fifty with people who never asked him to. There's a weight God gives you and a weight you grab to feel important, and only one of them shapes you. The other one just breaks your back.

Moses hit this wall. He was trying to carry a whole nation by himself, and he finally said it out loud to God.

:::margin
Numbers 11:14 (NIV)
"I cannot carry all these people by myself; the burden is too heavy for me."
:::

God's answer wasn't "yes you can." It was seventy elders. Sometimes the responsible thing is to admit the weight is too big for one man and go find the others.

The second way this mark goes bad is quieter, and I've watched it take down more good men than the first.

## Providing without disappearing

Walt is fifty-five and by every outside measure he's done it right. He built a small business from one truck to eleven. His kids went to college without loans. His wife has never worried about a bill in thirty years. He'd tell you, and he did tell me, that he's carried the weight of that house every single day, and he's right.

He's also barely in it.

He leaves before anybody's up and gets home in time to eat what's left in the pan standing at the counter. He knows the names of his daughter's boyfriends because his wife tells him. He hasn't been to a church service in a year that didn't fall on a holiday. And when his wife finally said, one night in the kitchen, "I don't need more. I need you," he honestly did not understand the sentence. Wasn't he there? Wasn't all of it for them?

Scripture is not soft on a man who won't provide.

:::margin
1 Timothy 5:8 (NIV)
"Anyone who does not provide for their relatives, and especially for their own household, has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever."
:::

Provision is a weight and a man carries it. A hardworking man doesn't need guilt for working hard. Look at the word: household. Not paycheck. A man is responsible for a household, and a household is people, and people need more than a roof over them. They need a man in the room. Walt was providing everything for his house except himself, and he'd started to believe the check was the man.

Provision that costs your presence isn't provision. It's a very expensive way to disappear.

And I understand how it happens. Work rewards you. Work has a scoreboard. Work tells you clearly when you've done well, and home mostly doesn't. Home is a toddler screaming at a plate of food you paid for. Home is a teenager who grunts. Home is a wife who's tired in a way you can't fix with a purchase. So a man drifts toward the place that claps for him, and he tells himself he's carrying the weight, and he is, just not the one that matters most.

So how does a man provide without disappearing?

He decides that presence is part of provision, not a luxury he earns after. He writes it into the week the way he'd write a client meeting. Tomás, who you'll meet more later, has a four-month-old and works ten-hour shifts on a pipeline crew. He can't shorten the shifts. What he did was claim the twenty minutes between his boots coming off and dinner. Phone on the charger by the door, not in his pocket. He holds the baby so his wife can sit down with both hands free. That's twenty minutes. It's also the difference between a man who's providing and a man who's disappearing, and his wife would tell you so.

He learns to tell his wife the truth about capacity. Not "I'll be home by six" when he knows it's eight. Not silence about the busy season until she finds out by living through it. A man who says "the next six weeks are going to be brutal, here's what I'm going to protect, here's what I'm going to need from you" is carrying weight in a way she can lean on. A man who just vanishes into the busy season and shows up on the other side expecting credit is asking her to carry the not knowing, which is a weight nobody should have to carry alone.

He keeps a floor. A floor is the minimum that doesn't move no matter what the job does. Sunday morning. One dinner a week with no screens. The bedtime prayer with the kids, even by phone from a hotel room in Midland. The floor is what tells your family that they're the corner post and the work is the wire, not the other way around.

If you're the older man reading this, past the paycheck years, don't think you've aged out. Roy told me that after he retired he realized he'd spent thirty years providing for a house he'd barely lived in, and his wife had made a whole life in the spaces he wasn't in. "I had to ask to be let back in," he said. "Not because she was cold. Because I'd been gone." He started by making the coffee. Every morning. It sounds small until you've been gone thirty years.

## How to pick it up

**Say "that's mine."** Out loud if you can. When you see the thing that's in front of you, before your mind starts working out whose fault it is, say the words. You'll be surprised how much a sentence changes what your hands do next.

**Stop your sentence at the period.** When you're owning something you did wrong, the sentence ends at "I did it." Not "I did it, but you." Not "I did it because." A man who can say "I was wrong, I'm sorry, I'm going to fix it" and then stop talking is rarer than you'd think.

**Own the outcome, not just the effort.** A boy says "I tried." A man says "it isn't done yet, and here's what I'm doing next." Effort matters, but responsibility means you stay with the thing until it holds, like a corner post.

**Carry your load before you reach for a burden.** If your own life is a mess, don't go fix your brother's marriage. Get your own house in order first, then reach. You'll reach with cleaner hands.

**Ask for the seventy.** When it's too heavy, say so, to God and to men. That's not setting the weight down. That's being wise about how a weight gets carried.

## The weight He carried

Every mark ends at Jesus, and this one has to.

The Man we follow picked up a weight that wasn't His. He didn't sin. He wasn't at fault for a single thing that had gone wrong in the world since the garden. And He walked toward the whole of it, on purpose, and took it.

:::margin
Isaiah 53:4 (NIV)
"Surely he took up our pain and bore our suffering, yet we considered him punished by God, stricken by him, and afflicted."
:::

Took up. Bore. Those are carrying words. Nobody handed Him that weight and made Him take it. He reached for it because it was in front of Him and He was the Man who could.

So when you set yourself at the corner, when you carry a thing that nobody's going to thank you for, you're not just being responsible. You're looking like Him. And He notices. That's the fourth mark showing up in the second. The Father who watched His Son carry the weight of the world sees a man in Lubbock get up at 5 a.m. to carry the weight of his house, and He does not forget it.

Pick it up. Dig it deeper than seems reasonable. Take the strain.

**Name it.** What weight have you been setting down and pointing at someone else about? Finish the sentence: "That's mine. I've been calling it ___'s."

**Do this week.** Pick one thing you provide for your house that isn't money (the coffee, the bedtime prayer, the twenty minutes after your boots come off) and do it every day this week, no exceptions. If you're single, pick one thing in your own life you've let somebody else carry, and carry it.

**Tell a brother.** Tell him the weight you named. Ask him to ask you next week whether you're still carrying it.

**Carry this.** Provision that costs your presence isn't provision. It's a very expensive way to disappear.
