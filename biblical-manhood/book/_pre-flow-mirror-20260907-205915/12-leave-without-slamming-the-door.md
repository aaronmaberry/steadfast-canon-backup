# 12. Leave Without Slamming the Door

*Part 2: What you inherited, and what is yours*

There are two ways to fail at leaving home, and most men have done one of them.

The first is the one you're picturing. Eighteen or twenty-two or thirty, a fight in the kitchen, a bag in the truck, a door slammed hard enough to rattle the frame. Then three states away and a phone call at Christmas if the man's lucky. Some of you did it with more noise than that and some of you did it with less, but the shape was the same: you left angry, and the anger came with you, and it's still in the truck.

The second way is quieter and I see it more. The man never left at all. Maybe he moved out. Maybe he's forty-four with a mortgage. But the center of gravity in his life is still his parents' house. He can't make a decision without hearing his father's voice on it. His wife has spent fifteen years competing with his mother and losing. He calls his dad before he calls his pastor, and his dad still tells him what to do, and he still does it. He didn't slam the door. He never walked through it.

God's design for a man is neither one. It's in the second chapter of the Bible, before there was a single broken family to complicate it.

:::margin
Genesis 2:24 (NIV)
"That is why a man leaves his father and mother and is united to his wife, and they become one flesh."
:::

A man leaves. That's God talking, not a rebellious teenager. Leaving is the design. And notice it comes before "is united to his wife," which means leaving isn't just a marriage thing. A single man has to leave too. A man's life is supposed to have its own center, under God, and that center is not his parents' kitchen.

But the same God who said "leave" also said this, and put it in the ten commandments where it can't be missed.

:::margin
Exodus 20:12 (NIV)
"Honor your father and your mother, so that you may live long in the land the LORD your God is giving you."
:::

Leave, and honor. Both. A man who slams the door has left and failed to honor. A man who never leaves has honored, sort of, and failed to leave. And the man God's after does both at once, which is harder than either, and it's the subject of this chapter.

## Why you have to leave

First, the man who never walked through the door, because he's the one who doesn't think this chapter is for him.

If you're married and your mother's opinion still outranks your wife's in your own house, you haven't left. If your father can still make you feel twelve years old with one comment, and you rearrange your decisions to avoid that comment, you haven't left. If you're single and thirty-five and you've never made a major decision without running it past your parents first, you haven't left.

Don't stop loving them. Don't stop listening; a wise man takes counsel from his elders. The man God made you to be has to stand on his own ground, answerable to God, and that ground can't be a room in your parents' house. Your wife needs to know that she's first. Your children need to see a father who's actually the father, not a grown boy relaying instructions from the grandparents. And you need it. A man who never leaves never becomes the corner post of his own place. He stays a line post in someone else's fence.

Leaving well looks like this. You make the decision and then tell them, instead of asking them and then deciding. You say "I love you, and we're going to do it this way" and you don't argue the point. You protect your wife from your mother's criticism even when your mother is right. You stop needing your father's approval before you act, which is chapter 11's work and chapter 10's before that. And you do it all with a soft voice and a steady spine, because leaving well isn't a fight. It's a man walking out of a room he's outgrown, gently, and closing the door behind him without a sound.

## Why you can't slam it

Now the other man. The one who left hard.

You may have had every reason. Some of you left houses that no boy should have had to live in. I'm not saying you should have stayed, and I'm not saying go back to a man who's still dangerous. But here's what the slammed door does. I've watched it in too many men.

A slammed door doesn't stay behind you. It comes with you. The man who cut off his father in fury has his father in every room of his life, because fury is a kind of attention, and the thing you're furious at is the thing you're organized around. He's not free of his dad. He's more bound to him than the son who calls every Sunday. He just can't see it because the binding is made of anger instead of affection.

Here's the other thing the slammed door does. It teaches your children how to leave you.

Kids watch how their father treats his father. That's the manual. If they see contempt, they learn that contempt is what grown sons feel for old men, and they'll file that away for when you're the old man. If they see a father who honors a man who didn't deserve it, they learn something about honor that no sermon could teach them.

So you can't slam it. Not because your father earned better. Because you're a man, and men don't slam doors, and because the Father who did not leave you is watching how you handle the father who did.

## What honor means when he wasn't honorable

This is where men get stuck, so be concrete.

Honor doesn't mean pretending. You don't have to say your father was good if he wasn't. Scripture never asks you to lie. Honor is about how you treat the office, not how you rate the performance. A soldier salutes the rank even when he can't respect the man wearing it, and he's not being a hypocrite; he's being a soldier.

Honoring a hard father looks like this. You tell the truth about him without mocking him. There's a difference between "my father was a hard man and he hurt us" and the version you'd tell at a bar to get a laugh. You speak about him carefully in front of your children; they don't need to carry your contempt for their grandfather, and if you hand it to them, that's your sin, not his. You provide for him if he needs it in old age, even if he never provided for you; Jesus had hard words for men who found religious reasons to skip that. You don't wish him harm, and you don't rejoice when it comes. You pray for him, honestly, even if the prayer is "Father, I don't know how to pray for this man, but You know him."

And you forgive him. That's the heaviest one, so be careful about what it means.

## Forgiveness

Forgiveness is not saying it didn't matter. It mattered. God knows exactly how much.

Forgiveness is not trust. You can forgive a man and still not leave your kids alone with him. Trust is earned and rebuilt; forgiveness is given.

Forgiveness is not necessarily a relationship. Some of you will forgive fathers you'll never sit at a table with again, because the table isn't safe, and that's not a failure of forgiveness.

Here's what forgiveness is. It's a man deciding that the debt his father owes him is going to be settled by God and not by him. He stops collecting. He stops waiting for the apology as a condition of his own peace. He hands the whole account to the only Judge who has all the facts and lets it go out of his own hands.

:::margin
Romans 12:19 (NIV)
"Do not take revenge, my dear friends, but leave room for God's wrath, for it is written: 'It is mine to avenge; I will repay,' says the Lord."
:::

Leave room for God's wrath. Most men read that as a threat against their enemies. Read it as a relief. You don't have to be the one who makes him pay. That's not your job. You can set it down.

Joseph's brothers threw him in a pit and sold him and let their father think he was dead. Years later, when he had every power to make them pay, he said something that's the clearest picture of forgiveness I know.

:::margin
Genesis 50:20 (NIV)
"You intended to harm me, but God intended it for good to accomplish what is now being done, the saving of many lives."
:::

Notice he didn't say they hadn't intended harm. He named it. You intended to harm me. Then he said what God had done with it anyway. That's the shape. Full honesty about the wrong, full trust that God was working in it regardless, and no bill presented.

## The conversation

If your father is alive, and it's safe, there may be a conversation to have. Not everybody needs to have it. Some fathers can't hear it and some conversations only reopen a wound that God's already closing. But for many men it's the door they've been standing outside for years. Here's a way through it.

You wrote a letter in chapter 8, the one you weren't going to send. Read it again. That's your preparation, not your script. The conversation itself is shorter than the letter and it has four parts.

*What I needed and didn't get.* Say it plainly, once, without a list of examples. "Dad, I needed to hear you say you were proud of me, and I never did." One sentence. Don't pile on. He'll hear one sentence; he won't hear ten.

*What I'm grateful for.* Find something. Even a hard father usually gave you something: a work ethic, a roof, a hunting trip, a sentence at the right moment. Name it. This isn't flattery. It's honor.

*What I'm going to do.* "I'm going to raise my kids differently, and I'm not going to carry this against you anymore." Say it as a statement, not a threat. You're telling him what's yours.

*The door.* "I'd like to know you as a man, if you're willing." And then leave it there. Don't demand he change. Don't wait for an apology as the price of the door staying open. You've said your piece like a man; how he answers is his weight to carry, not yours.

Cole did this with his father, the one who'd never said a kind word in thirty-three years. He drove out to the old man's place on a Tuesday and said the four things sitting on the tailgate. His father didn't apologize. He didn't say he was proud. He said, "Well," and looked at the barn for a while, and then he said, "You want to look at that pump with me?" And Cole said yes. He told me later that was the closest thing to an embrace his father had ever offered, and he took it, because he'd stopped needing more than the man could give.

If your father is dead, or too dangerous to approach, have the conversation anyway. Say the four things to God, out loud. Say them to a brother. Write them down. The words need to leave you even if they can't reach him.

## About your mother

Genesis 2:24 says a man leaves his father and mother, and I've been talking mostly about fathers, so turn.

Some of you were raised by your mother alone, and she was the whole family, and she was strong, and you love her more than anyone on earth. Good. Leave her anyway. She raised you to be a man, and a man has his own house. If you're married, your wife needs to be first, and your mother needs to hear you say so, kindly and clearly, the first time there's a conflict. Not the tenth time. The first. If you're single, you still need a life whose center isn't your mother's kitchen table, or you'll never be ready for a wife when she comes, and your mother, if she's wise, knows that better than you do.

If you're divorced, watch this one closely. There's a pull, in the year after, to go home emotionally, to let your mother become your confidante and your kids' second mother and your reason to not build a life of your own. She loves you and she'll let you. Don't. Honor her, lean on her some, and stay a man with his own ground.

## To the older man whose son slammed the door

Some of you are reading this chapter from the other side. You're the father. Your son left hard, or drifted, or he calls on Christmas, and you don't know what you did, or you do.

Leave the door unlocked. That's the whole strategy. You can't make him come back and you shouldn't try. But you can be the father in Jesus's story, who went out to both his sons, the one who ran off and the one who stayed home bitter.

:::margin
Luke 15:28 (NIV)
"The older brother became angry and refused to go in. So his father went out and pleaded with him."
:::

His father went out. Twice in that story the father is the one who moves. Go first. Make the call, even if he doesn't pick up. Send the text on his birthday with nothing in it but love. Don't keep score of who called last. Don't demand an apology as the price of the door. And if he comes, don't relitigate. Just set another plate.

And if there's something you did, say so. Plainly, once, without excuses. "I wasn't the father you needed. I'm sorry." A grown son who hears that from his father, even at fifty, even at seventy, will feel something shift that he didn't know could move. Roy did this with his daughter, who'd been distant for a decade after his years on the road. He said the sentence in her driveway. She didn't answer for a long time. Then she asked if he wanted to come in for supper.

## Walk out. Don't slam.

You have to leave. That's God's design and you can't be a man without it. Your life has to have its own center, under Him, and it can't be in your parents' house.

And you have to honor. That's God's command and you can't be a man without that either. Whatever kind of chair sat at the end of your table, the man in it was your father, and how you treat him now is a mark that shows on you.

So walk out. Gently, with a steady spine, closing the door behind you without a sound, and leaving it unlocked. Then go build a house of your own, and make sure the door on it is the kind a son can come home through.

**Name it.** Which way did you fail at leaving: the slammed door or the door you never walked through? Say it plainly, and say what it's cost your wife or your kids or your own life.

**Do this week.** If you never left: make one decision this week without running it past your parents first, and tell them afterward with love. If you slammed the door: pray for your father by name every day this week, and if he's alive and safe, pick a date for the four-part conversation.

**Tell a brother.** Tell him which father you have and which door you've been standing at. If you're going to have the conversation, tell him the date so he can ask you how it went.

**Carry this.** Honor the office, tell the truth about the man, hand the debt to God, and leave the door unlocked. Leave. Don't slam.
