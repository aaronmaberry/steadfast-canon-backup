# 28. Give Your Son a Hill

*Part 4: The house*

Hollis was twelve the day his father sent him up the caprock alone after a stray calf.

They'd been checking fence in the truck when they spotted it, way up in the rocks where the pasture broke off into a wall of caliche and cedar. His father stopped the truck, looked at the hill for a minute, and said, "Go get him." Not "let's go get him." Go. Hollis remembers standing at the bottom looking up and being certain he couldn't. The calf was scared and half-wild, the rocks were loose, there were rattlesnakes in that country, and he was twelve.

"I looked back at the truck," he told me, sixty years later. "He was just sitting there. Window down. Watching. He didn't get out."

It took Hollis an hour and a half. He fell twice. He got the calf cornered against a ledge and got a rope on it and got kicked in the thigh and got it down the hill anyway, and when he walked up to the truck with the calf on a lead and his jeans torn and blood on his shin, his father looked at him for a long moment and said, "Well. I figured you could."

"I knew he was watching the whole time," Hollis said. "He never got out of the truck. That was the point. If he'd come, I'd have learned I couldn't do it without him. He stayed in the truck and I learned I could."

Give your son a hill. The last chapter was the name; a name without a hill is just a compliment. A boy becomes what you called him by climbing something that proves it.

## Boys need difficulty

Here's what every father knows in his bones: boys don't become men by being protected from difficulty. They become men by being handed difficulty that's slightly beyond them, with a man watching who doesn't rescue too soon.

That's how it's always worked. Isaac carried the wood up the mountain.

:::margin
Genesis 22:6 (NIV)
"Abraham took the wood for the burnt offering and placed it on his son Isaac, and he himself carried the fire and the knife. As the two of them went on together,"
:::

Placed it on his son. There's a father giving a boy a weight to carry up a hill. Abraham carried the fire and the knife, the dangerous things, and gave his son the load. As the two of them went on together. A father and a son, one carrying, one watching, going up.

David's father gave him the sheep. That doesn't sound like much until you read what David did with them.

:::margin
1 Samuel 17:34-35 (NIV)
"But David said to Saul, 'Your servant has been keeping his father's sheep. When a lion or a bear came and carried off a sheep from the flock, I went after it, struck it and rescued the sheep from its mouth.'"
:::

I went after it. A teenage boy, alone in the hills with his father's flock, and a lion comes, and he goes after it. Nobody sent him a lion. But his father sent him to the hills with something real to keep, and the lion found him there, and by the time Goliath showed up, David had already been up a few hills. He told Saul so. The lion and the bear were the training. The sheep were the hill his father gave him.

And Jesus grew up in a carpenter's shop. We don't have the details, but we have the word: the boy who spoke the world into being spent His growing years with tools in His hands under His father's eye, and Luke says He grew in wisdom and stature. That's a boy with a hill.

Now look at what happens to a boy without one. He's comfortable. He's protected. Every difficulty is removed before it reaches him, every failure is cushioned, every risk is managed by adults who love him. And he's starving. Boys are built for hills, and a boy who isn't given one will go find one, and the ones he finds on his own are almost always worse: the fight, the dare, the video game with ten thousand hills in it that cost nothing and build nothing, the drugs, the recklessness in a truck at seventeen. Or he'll do the other thing, which is quieter and I see more of it now: he'll never leave the basement, because nobody ever taught him he could climb anything, and the world looks like all caprock and no truck.

## What a hill is

A hill is not a chore chart and it's not a travel ball schedule.

**A hill is real.** It matters to somebody besides him. The family depends on it getting done, or a person is actually helped, or something actually gets built. Boys can smell a made-up job from across the yard. Feeding the dog is real if the dog doesn't eat otherwise. Sweeping a clean garage to "learn responsibility" is a lie and he knows it.

**A hill is slightly beyond him.** Not what he can already do; not what a man can do. The next thing. The calf was beyond a twelve-year-old and just barely not. That's the zone. Too easy and he learns nothing. Too hard and he learns he can't. The father's whole skill is finding the next hill, and it's a skill you get by watching your son closely, which is chapter 22 applied to a boy.

**A hill has real risk, bounded.** He can fail. He can get hurt a little. He can lose money, break the thing, come down without the calf. If there's no way to fail, it's not a hill; it's a ride. But the risk is bounded by a father who's chosen the hill with care. Hollis's father knew that hill. He knew the snakes were mostly gone that time of year. He was in the truck. Bounded risk is how a boy learns courage without learning recklessness.

**A hill has an outcome he owns.** Not effort. Outcome. "I tried" is what a boy says. "It's done" or "it's not done yet, here's what I'm doing" is what a man says, and he learns to say it on hills where nobody accepts "I tried." The calf came down or it didn't.

**A hill is followed by a name.** When he comes down, whatever happened, the father speaks. "I figured you could." That's the name from chapter 27 landing on the hill from chapter 28, and together they make something a compliment alone never can. He was called capable. Then he climbed. Now he knows it's true.

## The hills, by age

The range I've seen work by age. Your son is his own boy; adjust.

**Four to six.** Real jobs a small boy can do that actually matter. Carrying the groceries in, all of them, even when it takes four trips. Feeding the animal that depends on it. Handing you tools and learning their names. Picking up the whole yard before the mower. He's not helping; he's working. Tell him so.

**Seven to ten.** Ownership. Something that's his to keep alive or keep working: a garden bed, a pet's whole life, the trash on trash day with no reminders, a small tool set he's responsible for. First real tools, with instruction and then with trust. First fire, built himself, with you a step back. First time doing a whole task alone from start to finish while you're somewhere else. The hill at this age is "yours, without me standing over you."

**Eleven to thirteen.** A real job with real money: mowing yards, hauling hay, a summer working for a man who isn't you. A physical challenge that scares him a little: the long hike, the first hunt, the build he's not sure he can finish. Caring for someone weaker: a younger sibling for an afternoon, the widow's yard without pay. And at least one hill he climbs alone with you in the truck. The caprock.

**Fourteen to seventeen.** Work for someone else who'll fire him if he's lazy. Money he earns and manages and loses some of. A project he owns from the idea to the finish: rebuild the engine, build the deer stand, run the fundraiser. Hardship he chooses and endures: the mission trip to somewhere hard, the cold camp, the fast. A decision with consequences that he makes and you don't override. The hill at this age is "a man's work with a man watching from farther off."

**Eighteen and beyond.** The launch. Chapter 12. He leaves, without slamming the door, and the hill is his own life, and you're a man he can call, not a man who climbs it for him.

Notice what's not on that list. There's nothing about trophies. A boy can play sports for a decade and never climb a hill if the sport is his father's ego and every failure gets explained away by the coach's bad call. And there's nothing about screens, because a screen is a machine for simulating hills without the weight. Ten thousand levels beaten and not one calf brought down.

## The father's part

**Give it. Don't do it.** Your instinct will be to do the job yourself because you're faster and better and it's easier. Every time you do that, you've stolen a hill. Hand it over and walk away.

**Watch. Don't rescue.** Stay in the truck. Let him struggle. Let him fall twice. This is the hardest thing in fathering, and mothers usually find it harder than fathers, so you're going to have to lead here, gently, and explain why. Rescuing a boy who's not in danger teaches him he needs rescue. Watching a boy who's not in danger teaches him he doesn't.

**Let him fail small so he doesn't fail big.** A boy who's allowed to lose forty dollars at twelve because he didn't plan learns something that will save him forty thousand at thirty. A boy who's never allowed to fail at anything fails for the first time at twenty-three with a wife and a mortgage and nobody in the truck.

**Debrief.** When he comes down, ask: what happened? What'd you learn? What would you do differently? Not a lecture. Questions, and then waiting. He's teaching himself with your help.

**Name what he did.** "I figured you could." "That's what a man does." "You didn't quit." Chapter 27. Every hill ends with a name or it's just a hard day.

**Raise the hill.** Next time, a little more. That's the whole method. A boy who climbs a slightly bigger hill every year for fifteen years is a man by eighteen, not because you told him so but because he's got the calluses to prove it.

:::margin
Hebrews 12:11 (NIV)
"No discipline seems pleasant at the time, but painful. Later on, however, it produces a harvest of righteousness and peace for those who have been trained by it."
:::

Trained by it. That's what hills are: training. It's not pleasant for him and it's not pleasant for you watching from the truck. Later on, a harvest.

## How fathers get it wrong

**No hill.** Comfort, protection, and a screen. The boy is loved and safe and he's never once been asked to do something he wasn't sure he could do. He'll be thirty and living in your basement, or he'll be thirty and taking stupid risks to feel something. Both are boys who were never handed a calf.

**Too big too fast.** The father reads a book about raising men and hands his nine-year-old a man's job and crushes him. Then the boy learns the hill is a place where he fails, and he stops climbing. Slightly beyond. Not a mountain.

**The father's hill, not the son's.** The sport the father wanted to play. The business the father wants to hand down. The hill has to be real for the boy, in the direction of who the boy is, not a monument to who the father wishes he'd been.

**Rescuing.** Finishing his science project at midnight. Calling the coach. Paying off the debt he ran up. Every rescue is a message: you can't. Stay in the truck.

**Shaming the fall.** He fails and you make him pay for it with contempt. Now the hill is a place of fear, and he'll avoid every hill he can for the rest of his life. He fell twice on the caprock. His father didn't say a word about the falls. He said "I figured you could."

## In your season

If you've got a baby boy, the hill's years off, but the habit starts now. Don't do for him what he can do. Let him struggle to sit up before you prop him. Let him work at the toy before you fix it. Fathers of infants learn to rescue reflexively because a crying baby is unbearable, and some of that's right. But a father who catches himself doing everything for his son at six months is a father who'll be doing everything for him at sixteen. Watch, a little, from the truck, even now.

If you're divorced, watch the weekend trap. It's easy to make your time with your son all fun: pizza, movies, the lake, no chores, because you feel guilty and you want him to want to come. That's a boy with no hill at your house. He needs work at your place. His own jobs, his own responsibilities, a project you build together across weekends so it's always waiting for him when he comes back. Kenny and his son built a smoker over eleven weekends. "It was the only thing at my house that was ours," he said. "He couldn't wait to get back to it. Not because it was fun. Because it wasn't done."

If you're an older man, you give hills better than anyone alive. You've got time, tools, a shop, and patience the boy's father doesn't have. Roy's grandson learned to fish from Roy, and then to clean the fish, and then to gut a deer, and then to change the oil on Roy's truck, and the boy's father, Roy's son, told me his son comes home from Grandpa's "taller." Grandfathers hand out hills that fathers are too busy or too anxious to give. That's a job, and it's yours.

If you're a single man, or a man with no sons, there are boys in your church who've never been up a hill because there was nobody in the truck. Coach them, and coach them hard. Take one to work with you. Run the church workday and give the fatherless boys the real jobs. Hollis's father sent him up the caprock; some boys need an unrelated man to point at a hill and say "go," because their fathers never did. Be that man.

And a word about daughters, because this chapter carries a son's name. Your daughter needs hills too. She's not fragile. She needs real work, real challenge, real ownership, a father who doesn't rescue her from every difficulty and who names her strong when she comes down. The hills may look different. The method doesn't.

## Well, I figured you could

Hollis has given a lot of hills in sixty years. To his own sons, to grandsons, to the boys in his Sunday school class, to Cole by a truck. I asked him the secret once, and he thought about it and said, "Pick the right hill. Stay in the truck. Say the right thing when he comes down." Then he laughed and said, "And don't tell his mother about the snakes."

Paul told a young man he'd fathered in the faith to expect hills for the rest of his life.

:::margin
2 Timothy 2:3 (NIV)
"Join with me in suffering, like a good soldier of Christ Jesus."
:::

Like a good soldier. Not a protected boy. A man who's been trained on hills and can take the weight. That's what you're building. Not a boy who's had a good childhood. A man who can carry a calf down a caprock when nobody comes.

Your son is standing at the bottom looking up. He's not sure he can. He's looking back at the truck.

Roll the window down. Watch. Stay.

---

**Name it.** Does your son have a hill right now, something real, slightly beyond him, with an outcome he owns? Say what it is. If the answer is no, say whether it's because you've been protecting him or doing it yourself.

**Do this week.** Hand your son one hill before Tuesday, scaled to his age. Give it, walk away, don't rescue, and when he comes down, ask what he learned and name what you saw. If you have no son, give a fatherless boy a real job and stay in the truck.

**Tell a brother.** Tell him the hill you gave and how hard it was to not rescue. Ask him what hills his father gave him, and what hills he's giving. Fathers help each other find the right size.

**Carry this.** A name without a hill is just a compliment. Pick the right hill. Stay in the truck. Say the right thing when he comes down.
