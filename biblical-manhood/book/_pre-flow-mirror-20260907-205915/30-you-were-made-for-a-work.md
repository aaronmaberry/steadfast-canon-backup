# 30. You Were Made for a Work

*Part 5: The long road*

There's a man in a town near me who runs the wastewater plant on the night shift. Ten at night to six in the morning, four nights a week, for nineteen years. Nobody in that town thinks about him. Nobody knows his name. Every one of them flushes a toilet and turns on a tap and never once wonders who's awake at 3 a.m. making sure the water that comes back to them won't kill them.

He's a believer. He asked me once, after church, standing by his truck, whether his work "counted." He meant for God. He'd been sitting through years of sermons about calling and ministry and the mission field, and he'd started to feel like his real life was the twelve hours a week he spent at church and the forty he spent at the plant were a kind of necessary evil, something he did to fund the part that mattered.

"I'm just running a plant," he said. "It's not like I'm doing anything for the kingdom."

I told him a whole town drinks clean water because he stays awake, and that God noticed before I did. But I've thought about his question for years, because most men are asking it in some form. Does my work count? Is it part of the thing, or is it the thing I do so I can get to the thing?

Most of a man's road is work. Forty, fifty years of it. If your work doesn't count, most of your life doesn't count. I don't believe that, and neither does Scripture.

## Work came before the curse

We've been to Genesis 2:15 several times in this book. Going there again, because men keep forgetting it.

To work it and take care of it. Before the fall. Before the serpent, before the thorns, before sweat was a curse word. God made a man and gave him a job, and the job was part of the good, not a punishment added later. The curse in chapter 3 didn't invent work. It made work hard: thorns, sweat, ground that fights back. But the work itself is in the blueprint, and a man who thinks of his job as a necessary evil has read Genesis 3 and skipped Genesis 2.

And go higher than that. God works. The first thing the Bible shows God doing is six days of labor and a day of rest. Jesus said it plainly when the religious men accused Him of working on the wrong day.

:::margin
John 5:17 (NIV)
"In his defense Jesus said to them, 'My Father is always at his work to this very day, and I too am working.'"
:::

My Father is always at his work. The God you serve is not a God who sits. He builds, He tends, He keeps, He makes things grow, and He made you in His image, which means among other things He made you to work. That's why a man who can't work feels like he's coming apart. That's why a man laid off or retired or disabled goes sideways in ways that aren't just about money. The image in him is straining against a life with no garden.

And Jesus worked. Not just the three years. Most of His life on earth, from boyhood to thirty, He spent in a carpenter's shop with tools in His hands, making things for people who paid Him. Nobody wrote it down because it wasn't remarkable. It was a man working. The Son of God spent six times longer building furniture than preaching, and He didn't consider the furniture years a waste.

Here's the plain answer to the man at the plant. Yes. It counts. It's not the thing you do so you can get to the thing. It is the thing, or most of it, and the way you do it is most of what God's purpose in your generation looks like.

## What work is for

Be specific, because "your work matters" can mean anything. Four things work is for, in Scripture, and three things it isn't.

**Provision.** You work so your house has what it needs. We've said this; it's a real weight and a man carries it, and a man who won't has denied the faith. Paul was blunter than I'd dare to be.

:::margin
2 Thessalonians 3:10 (NIV)
"For even when we were with you, we gave you this rule: 'The one who is unwilling to work shall not eat.'"
:::

Unwilling. Not unable. There's grace for the man who can't. There's a rule for the man who won't.

**Service.** Your work is for your neighbor. The plant operator's work is clean water for four thousand people. The electrician's work is a house that doesn't burn. The teacher's work is a kid who can read. The trucker's work is groceries on the shelf. This is the piece men miss most: your job is how you love your neighbor forty hours a week, and the quality of it is the quality of your love. Paul told thieves to work, and look at the reason.

:::margin
Ephesians 4:28 (NIV)
"Anyone who has been stealing must steal no longer, but must work, doing something useful with their own hands, that they may have something to share with those in need."
:::

Something useful. That they may have something to share. Work turns a man from a taker into a giver. That's not a side effect. That's what it's for.

**Making the world more than it is.** Genesis 1:28. Subdue and rule, which we said means cultivate: take the raw thing and bring it to order and fruit. Every honest job does this. The mechanic takes a broken machine and makes it run. The farmer takes dirt and makes food. The accountant takes chaos and makes it legible. The nurse takes a body that's failing and holds it steady. You're doing, in small, what God did in six days, and that's why a finished job feels the way it does. It's the image showing.

**Worship.** Colossians 3:23. Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord. The man at the plant is working for the Lord at 3 a.m., whether or not anybody in the town knows it, and the Lord knows it, and the Lord is the audience that matters. When you do a job with all your heart because God's watching, you've turned the plant into a sanctuary. That's not a metaphor. That's the fourth mark applied to a time card.

Now the three things work isn't.

**Work is not your identity.** It's what you do, not who you are. The man who is his job dies when the job does, and we'll get to that. Your name is son, brother, husband, father, keeper of a garden. Your job is one of the gardens.

**Work is not an escape.** Chapters 5 and 24. Work claps and home mostly doesn't, and a man can hide in a job and call it provision. If you're staying late because the plant is easier than the kitchen, you've turned a good thing into an exit.

**Work is not your salvation.** You don't earn your standing with God by working hard. You already have it, in Christ, and now you work because you're His, not to become His. A man who's trying to justify himself by his output never rests and never finishes, because there's always more.

## When you hate the job

Some of you are reading this at a job you can't stand, and "work is worship" sounds like a slap. Straight:

The thorns are real. Genesis 3 wasn't kidding. There are bosses who are fools, customers who lie, systems that grind, tasks that mean nothing to anybody. Some of what makes work miserable is the curse, and the curse won't lift until Jesus comes back, and pretending otherwise is a lie I won't tell you.

But here's what Solomon, who had every job and every luxury, concluded after looking hard at all of it.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 2:24 (NIV)
"A person can do nothing better than to eat and drink and find satisfaction in their own toil. This too, I see, is from the hand of God,"
:::

Find satisfaction in their own toil. From the hand of God. Satisfaction in work is a gift, and it's available in a lot more jobs than men think, and it comes from three places.

It comes from excellence. A man who does even a job he hates with skill has something the job can't take from him.

:::margin
Proverbs 22:29 (NIV)
"Do you see someone skilled in their work? They will serve before kings; they will not serve before officials of low rank."
:::

Skilled in their work. Whatever the work. Be the best one at it in the building, not for the boss, but because skill is a form of dignity and God gave you hands to be skilled with.

It comes from people. The job is a garden, and the garden has people in it, and those people are your assignment as much as the tasks are. The apprentice who's watching how you handle a bad day. The coworker whose marriage is coming apart. The customer nobody else is patient with. You may hate the work and love the people, and the people may be why God's got you there.

And it comes from the audience. If you're working for the boss, the job's as good as the boss. If you're working for the Lord, the job's as good as the Lord, and He's watching a man do a thing well that nobody else values, and He counts it.

If you should leave, leave like a man. Not on a Tuesday because you had a feeling; chapter 20 covered that. With a plan, with your wife in the room, with the next thing lined up or the savings to bridge it, with your reasons written down and checked by a brother. Men leave jobs badly in two directions: they quit in a huff, or they stay in a coffin. The man in the middle plans his exit and provides through it.

## When you lose the job

This one's different, and it's coming for more of you than you think. Nobody prepares men for it.

You get called in on a Thursday and by Friday you're a man with no garden. And the first thing that happens isn't about money. It's about who you are. Because you've been introducing yourself by your job for twenty years, and now the sentence has nothing after "I'm."

Here's what a man does.

Tell your wife the same day. Not after you've figured out a plan. The same day. Men hide layoffs from their wives for weeks and it's the worst thing they can do, because she'll find out, and what she'll learn is that you hid. Tell her, and tell her you're scared if you're scared, and tell her you're going to handle it.

Tell your brother that week. The twenty minutes was built for this. "What's heavy?" has an answer now.

Treat the search as your work. Get up at the same time. Shave. Dress. Sit down at 8 and work at finding work until 5. A man who treats unemployment as a vacation drifts into the recliner and doesn't come out. A man who treats it as a job stays a man.

Take the bridge job. Paul made tents so he could preach. If the plant closes and the only thing available is driving a delivery truck at two-thirds the pay, drive the truck. It's provision, it's service, it's making the world more than it is, and it's worship if you do it for the Lord. It's not beneath you. Nothing that feeds your house is beneath you.

And kill the shame. Chapter 15: guilt says you did a wrong thing; shame says you are a wrong thing. Losing a job is neither. It's a thorn. It happened to you. Your Father saw it coming and He's not surprised and He's not disappointed, and He's the one who told you not to worry about what you'd eat, because He knows what you need.

## When your work is the house

Some of you have work that doesn't look like work to anybody, including you.

You're home with the kids because of how the math worked out, and you feel like less of a man for it, and you shouldn't. You're disabled and can't do what you used to. You're a caregiver: Mr. Alvarez bathing his wife every morning is doing the second verb of Genesis 2:15, keeping what he was given, and it's the hardest work in this book. If your garden is a house and a body that needs you, that's a garden. Work it and keep it. The Father who sees in secret is watching a man do the unglamorous thing with all his heart, and He doesn't grade on visibility.

## After the badge

Now the longest section. Men past sixty have been under-served on this for a hundred years of Christian books.

Scripture has no retirement. I've looked. There's no verse where a man stops working God's purpose because he hit a number. What there is, though, is a shift, and it's in a passage most men have never read.

:::margin
Numbers 8:25-26 (NIV)
"but at the age of fifty, they must retire from their regular service and work no longer. They may assist their fellow Levites in performing their duties at the tent of meeting, but they themselves must not do the work. This, then, is how you are to assign the responsibilities of the Levites."
:::

Retire from their regular service. And then: they may assist. The Levites who did the heavy lifting in the tabernacle stopped doing the heavy lifting at fifty. They didn't stop. They moved from carrying to assisting, from doing the work to helping the ones who did. That's the biblical shape of retirement. Not the recliner. A change of garden.

Roy's first Tuesday after thirty-one years of wearing a badge was, in his words, the worst day of his life. Nobody needed him. There was no assignment. He'd been a man with a work since he was nineteen, and then he was a man with a pension and a television and a wife who'd built a life in the spaces he wasn't in.

Here's what pulled him out, and it's a rhythm any retired man can build.

He kept getting up at 5:30. Not because he had to. Because a man who sleeps until he feels like getting up will soon feel like getting up around ten, and a man who gets up at ten is halfway to the drift. The alarm stayed.

He made the coffee. Chapter 5. Then he sat with his wife while she drank it, and that became the first work of every day: knowing her, after forty-four years of not.

He read. Mark first, then Proverbs, a chapter a day, out loud on the porch. Chapter 17. The plumb line. For thirty-one years he'd let the badge be his north star, and it was gone, and he needed the one that doesn't move.

He gave himself a job every day. Not a hobby. A job: something he'd committed to that somebody else was counting on. The north gate. The widow's gutters. The church van. The grandson's oil change. "If I didn't have a job by breakfast," he said, "I found one by lunch. A man with no job by noon is a man in a chair by two."

He built the Thursday table. Chapter 13 and 14. Two men in their thirties who needed a man ahead of them. That became his main work, and he'll tell you it saved his life. Retirement from the regular service; assisting the ones still carrying.

He fished with his grandson on Tuesdays. Chapter 28. Hills.

And he stayed in church, not as an attender but as a man with a job there. Sunday wasn't a day off from work anymore. It was the work.

That's a post-badge rhythm. Six things. None of them is a career. All of them are Genesis 2:15. Roy said the day he read that verse after retiring was the day he stopped feeling like a man who'd been let go and started feeling like a man who'd been reassigned. Same job. Different garden.

If your health took your work before your age did, the shape is smaller but it's the same. Assist. Mr. Alvarez, when he can get an hour away from his wife's care, calls every widower in the church, one a day, and asks them what's heavy. He does it from a chair. He's seventy-eight. It's a work, and men are being kept alive by it.

The danger for the older man is the drift wearing a fishing license, and its cousin, the anger that comes through the television. Both are what happens to a man who was made for a work and stopped. Don't stop. Shift. Carry less; assist more. Declare it to the next generation, like the psalm says, till you're done.

## In the other seasons

If you're single, work is your main garden and you should work it like a man who intends to hand it to a wife someday: with excellence, with money handled, with a reputation you'd be proud for her to hear about. And watch that you don't make the work your wife. Men who marry their jobs at twenty-six have a hard time un-marrying them at thirty-two.

If you've got a baby in the house, you're working exhausted, and the temptation to stay an extra hour at the plant because it's quieter than the house is going to be real. Name it. The floor from chapter 5 holds this year more than any other. Provision is holy; disappearing isn't; and a wife with a newborn knows the difference by 6:40.

If you're divorced, the money's tighter than it's ever been: two households, support, lawyers. You may need to work more. Do it without vanishing from the kids. The Tuesday call from the truck at 7:30 costs nothing and it's the most important work you'll do that week. And let your kids see a father who's steady at his job. In a world that shook, a dad who goes to work every day and comes home every night is ground they can stand on.

## He finished the work

Jesus said it in a prayer the night before He died.

:::margin
John 17:4 (NIV)
"I have brought you glory on earth by finishing the work you gave me to do."
:::

Finishing the work you gave me to do. That's the sentence every man wants to be able to say at the end. Not "I had a great career." Not "I made a lot of money." The work You gave me, I did, and I finished it, and it brought You glory. The carpenter years and the preaching years and the cross, all one work, all finished.

You were made for a work. It might be a plant at 3 a.m. It might be a classroom or a crew or a spreadsheet or a body that needs bathing or a Thursday table or a north gate. It's all Genesis 2:15. It all counts, and the way you do it is most of what your life will have been.

The man at the plant is still there. Nineteen years, and he told me last spring that he'd started praying over the town while he ran his checks at 3 a.m. Four thousand people, asleep, drinking his water in the morning, none of them knowing that a man they've never met asked God to keep them while he kept the plant.

That counts. Go do your work.

---

**Name it.** What has work been for you: provision, service, making the world more, worship? Or identity, escape, salvation? Say which one's been steering, honestly.

**Do this week.** Do one ordinary task at your job with all your heart, as for the Lord, and tell no one. If you're between jobs, treat the search as your job from 8 to 5 starting Monday. If you're retired, name your job for tomorrow before you go to bed tonight, and do it before noon.

**Tell a brother.** Tell him whether your work has been an exit, and what your house pays for it. If you're retired, tell him your six-thing rhythm and ask him to check whether you're keeping it.

**Carry this.** Work came before the curse. You weren't let go. You were reassigned. Same job, different garden. Finish the work you were given.
