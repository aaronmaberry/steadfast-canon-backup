# 3. The Four Marks

*Part 1: The man you were built to be*

You made it through chapter 2 and you're not crazy. Good. Now you've got a harder problem, because a man who knows the picture he was handed is wrong still has to answer the question underneath it. If that's not a man, what is?

This chapter answers that in one sentence you can carry in your pocket. The next four chapters take the pieces. But before the sentence, a picture.

## What a mark is

My grandfather's hands looked like a map of every job he ever worked. There was a white ridge across his left palm from a fence stretcher that slipped in 1961. His right index finger didn't straighten all the way. The knuckles were wide from years of being used the way knuckles get used when a man works outside. You didn't have to ask him what he'd done with his life. It was written on him.

That's what a mark is. A mark isn't a feeling you have or a title you hold. A mark is what's left on you by what you've done over and over. Nobody gets a callus from thinking about a shovel.

I say that first because there's a whole shelf of books for men, and a lot of them start inside the chest. They'll tell you about your heart, your desires, your longing for something wild and dangerous, and some of that is true and some of those men were good to me when I was younger. I'm not writing against them. But this book makes a plainer claim, and I want you to see the difference from the first page. The marks I'm about to give you aren't things you feel. They're things you do, and they show. A man could sit in a deer blind at dawn and feel every wild thing a book ever promised him and still go home and let his wife carry the whole house alone. What's in his chest didn't leave a mark. What he did on Tuesday did.

So here's the sentence. I didn't invent it. Men said it before me, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But I've held it up against my own life for a long time, and I've watched it hold up against the lives of men in every season, and I've never found a place where it lies.

**A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.**

Read it again, slower. Four marks. Not four moods. Four things you can see on a man the way you could see the fence stretcher on my grandfather's palm.

## The first mark: he rejects passivity

Every Texas town has a man on a tailgate. He's not a bad man. He's got opinions about the drought and the school board and the way the city runs the water department, and he'll tell you about all of it while the work waits behind him. He's waiting for the right time, the right price, the right feeling, the right sign. He's been waiting so long that waiting has started to look like wisdom.

Passivity is the default setting of a man. It's not something you choose so much as something you fall into, like a rut in a caliche road. And what makes it dangerous is that it almost never looks like laziness. It looks like patience. It looks like keeping the peace. It looks like "I didn't want to overstep." The first man in the Bible stood right next to his wife while the whole world went wrong, and the text goes out of its way to tell us he was there. He didn't grab the fruit. He didn't argue for it. He just didn't do anything, and that was enough to break everything.

:::margin
Genesis 3:6 (NIV)
"When the woman saw that the fruit of the tree was good for food and pleasing to the eye, and also desirable for gaining wisdom, she took some and ate it. She also gave some to her husband, who was with her, and he ate it."
:::

Who was with her. Three words, and they've been indicting men ever since.

Rejecting passivity doesn't mean becoming loud or pushy or the kind of man who mistakes volume for strength. It means that when something needs doing, and you know it, and you're the one standing there, you get off the tailgate. It means you're the one who brings up the hard conversation instead of hoping it gets brought up. It means you don't need a feeling to start. Chapter 4 is all about this.

## The second mark: he accepts responsibility

The second mark is weight.

I helped a friend move a gun safe one August. Four hundred pounds of steel, and a narrow hallway, and a hundred and four degrees. There's a moment with a load like that when you're committed. You've lifted it, your buddy's lifted it, and now the only way through is through. You can't set it down in the hallway. You can't decide halfway that this isn't really your safe.

That's what responsibility feels like on a man. It's a weight he picks up and doesn't set down in the hallway. And notice I said picks up. A boy waits for responsibility to be assigned to him, and then argues about whether it's fair. A man looks at what's in front of him and says, "That's mine," even when it isn't his fault, even when nobody would blame him for walking past it. The bill nobody wants to open. The kid nobody's talking to. The apology nobody's making. The neighbor's fence that blew down on your side. A man reaches for the weight.

There's an old line in Lamentations that surprised me the first time I read it slow.

:::margin
Lamentations 3:27 (NIV)
"It is good for a man to bear the yoke while he is young."
:::

Good. Not tolerable. Good. The weight is what shapes you. Chapter 5 will show you how to pick it up without it crushing you, and how to tell the difference between weight God gave you and weight you grabbed to feel important.

## The third mark: he leads courageously

Every March there's a stock tank somewhere in this state with a group of boys standing on the bank, and the water's cold, and everybody's waiting for somebody to go first. That's leadership. Not the title. Not the corner office. Not being in charge. Going first.

Somebody has to go first into the cold water. Somebody has to be the first one to say "I was wrong" in a marriage that's been quiet for three days. Somebody has to be the first one to pray out loud at the table, the first one to call the brother who's been drifting, the first one to tell the truth at the meeting where everybody's nodding. That's what courage is for. It isn't the absence of fear. If you weren't afraid, it wouldn't take courage. Courage is fear with its boots on, moving.

:::margin
Joshua 1:9 (NIV)
"Have I not commanded you? Be strong and courageous. Do not be afraid; do not be discouraged, for the LORD your God will be with you wherever you go."
:::

Notice God doesn't tell Joshua to stop being afraid. He tells him what to do while he's afraid. Chapter 6 is about going first. It's more ordinary than you think.

## The fourth mark: he expects the greater reward

The last mark is the one that separates a good man from a godly one, and it's the one most men skip.

Pecan trees don't hurry. If you plant one this fall, you'll water it for years before it gives you a single nut worth cracking, and it won't hit its stride for a decade or more. Every man who has ever planted a pecan orchard was planting for somebody else. For a son. For a grandson. For a family that might sell the place before the trees mature. You don't plant pecans because you're going to eat pecans. You plant pecans because you believe in a harvest you won't hold in your own hands.

That's the fourth mark. A real man expects the greater reward, God's reward, and that expectation changes what he does with his Tuesdays. He can do the right thing when nobody's looking, because he's not working for the people who are looking. He can stay married through a hard decade, because he's not measuring the marriage by this year's return. He can give money that won't come back and time that won't be thanked, because he believes what Jesus said about the Father who sees in secret.

:::margin
Hebrews 11:6 (NIV)
"And without faith it is impossible to please God, because anyone who comes to him must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who earnestly seek him."
:::

He rewards. God isn't embarrassed by that word, and you shouldn't be either. The man who doesn't expect a reward from God will go looking for one somewhere else, and there are only so many places to look: applause, money, women, a bigger truck, a boy's approval, his father's approval. Chapter 7 is about living for a reward that lasts, and it's the chapter that makes the other three possible.

## Why four, and why these

You could argue for a different list. Men have. But I'll tell you why I've stopped arguing.

Each of these four marks answers a specific way men break. Passivity is how a man breaks by doing nothing. Blame and evasion are how he breaks by refusing weight. Cowardice, which usually goes by nicer names, is how he breaks by hanging back. And living for the wrong reward is how he breaks slowly, over forty years, while looking successful. Most of the trouble I've seen on a man traces back to one of these four.

And here's what I want you to notice about the shape of them. The first three are things a good man of any faith or none might do. Plenty of men reject passivity and carry weight and go first, and I'm grateful for every one of them. The fourth is what makes the other three Christian. It's the reason a man can keep doing the first three when it costs him and nobody claps. If you take the fourth one out, you've got a self-improvement list. Leave it in, and you've got a way of life that will outlast you.

## A word about seasons

I want to say this once here, so I don't have to say it in every chapter.

You're going to read this book in a particular season. Maybe you're twenty-four and single and nobody's ever called you a man out loud. Maybe you've got a four-month-old and haven't slept a full night since spring. Maybe you're divorced and you see your kids every other weekend and you're not sure this book is for you. Maybe you're sixty-eight and you turned in a badge or a hard hat or a set of keys last year and you're wondering what a man does with his mornings now. Maybe you buried your wife.

You're all in the room. I'm not going to stop every chapter and run down the list, because you'd start skipping and I would too. When your season actually changes what I'd tell you, I'll turn and tell you, right there in the paragraph. And in chapter 32 we'll sit down and talk about seasons on purpose, all of them, in one place. Until then, trust that I know you're here.

One more thing. The men you'll meet in these pages, Roy and Tomás and Kenny and the rest, are composites. The conversations happened. The names and details are mixed so no one can be picked out of a lineup.

## How to read the rest of this book

Every chapter from here on ends the same way, with four short lines. *Name it* asks you to say plainly, in your own words, what the chapter touched. *Do this week* gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday. Not a program. One thing. *Tell a brother* asks you to say it out loud to another man, because a mark you keep secret doesn't harden into anything. And *Carry this* hands you one line to take with you.

If you don't have a brother yet, don't skip that third line. Chapter 13 is for you. Until you get there, "tell a brother" can mean writing it on the back of a receipt and telling God, who has heard worse.

Four marks. Look at each one the way you'd look at a hand.

---

**Name it.** Which of the four marks is faintest on you right now? Say it in a sentence: "The one I'm missing most is ___."

**Do this week.** Write the four marks on an index card in your own handwriting and put it where you'll see it on Tuesday morning: the truck visor, the shaving mirror, the inside of your lunchbox.

**Tell a brother.** Read him the sentence. Ask him which mark he'd say is faintest on you. Let him answer before you defend yourself.

**Carry this.** A mark is what's left on you by what you've done over and over. Nobody gets a callus from thinking about a shovel.
