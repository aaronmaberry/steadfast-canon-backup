# 15. The Problem Inside

*Part 2: What you inherited, and what is yours*

I chased a problem on an old Ford for most of one summer. It'd run fine and then it'd run hot and then it'd run fine again. I did the radiator. I did the thermostat. I did the water pump, the hoses, the fan clutch, the belt. Every part I replaced was a part that could've been the problem, and every time I put the hood down I was sure I'd got it.

The block was cracked.

It had been cracked the whole time, a hairline in the casting that let a little coolant go where it shouldn't, and all summer I'd been rebuilding everything bolted onto an engine that had a problem inside it that no bolted-on part could fix. When the old mechanic in town finally told me, he wasn't unkind about it. He just said, "Son, you've been fixing the wrong thing."

We've spent seven chapters on what you inherited: the chair, the exits, the handoff you didn't get, the men you didn't have. All of that is real. All of it shaped you. And if I stopped there, I'd be leaving you under the hood replacing hoses, because there's a problem inside that no amount of understanding your father is going to fix.

The problem is you. It's me. It's every man who's ever lived, and Scripture has a plain word for it, and until you're willing to say the word, you'll spend your life fixing the wrong thing.

## Even with a perfect father

Here's a thought experiment I've put to a lot of men. Suppose the chair at your table had been full every night. Suppose the man in it was kind and present and strong and told you he loved you and handed you off at fourteen with a ceremony and a blessing. Suppose you had brothers and a mentor and a church that knew your name.

Would you be a good man?

Better, probably. That stuff matters; I just spent seven chapters saying it does. But would you be good? Would you never lie to your wife? Never look at what you shouldn't? Never lose your temper, never nurse a grudge, never quietly decide you're better than the man in the next pew? Never once put yourself first when it cost somebody else?

You know the answer. And the reason you know is that the first man had all of it. Adam had a perfect Father, present every day. He had a perfect place, a perfect work, a perfect wife, and no wound of any kind. Nobody had ever left him. Nobody had ever failed him. And he still reached for the fruit, or stood there silent while it was reached for, and then hid and blamed.

The problem inside a man didn't come from his father. It came through his father, from a father before that, all the way back to a garden. And it would be in you if you'd been raised by saints.

:::margin
Jeremiah 17:9 (NIV)
"The heart is deceitful above all things and beyond cure. Who can understand it?"
:::

Beyond cure. That's the block. You can't bolt a part on it. Jesus said the same thing when the religious men of His day were busy managing their outsides.

:::margin
Mark 7:23 (NIV)
"All these evils come from inside and defile a person."
:::

From inside. Not from your father. Not from your culture. Not from your wound. Those things opened doors and handed you tools and taught you which exit to take. But what walked through the doors was already in you.

## Why this is good news

That doesn't sound like good news. Here's why it is.

If your problem is entirely what was done to you, you have no way out. You can't un-have your father. You can't go back and get handed off at fourteen. You can understand it and grieve it and forgive it, and you should, but understanding a crack doesn't seal it. A man who believes his whole problem is his inheritance has found a way to never repent, because you can't repent of what somebody else did. He'll spend his life in a very sophisticated version of Adam's excuse: the father you gave me, he did it.

But if the problem is inside you, if it's yours, then it can be dealt with, because God deals with what's yours. He doesn't offer to fix your father. He offers to fix you. That's the whole gospel. Jesus didn't come for men whose circumstances were bad. He came for men whose hearts were, and that's all of us, and it means nobody's outside the reach of the cure.

:::margin
Romans 5:8 (NIV)
"But God demonstrates his own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us."
:::

While we were still sinners. Not while we were victims. Not while we were misunderstood or under-fathered or doing our best with what we had. Sinners. He named the real problem and then died for it. If you'll name it too, you get the cure.

## The word

The word is sin. Land on it. Men avoid it for two opposite reasons and both are wrong.

Some men avoid it because it sounds too harsh. They'd rather say "struggles," "issues," "brokenness," "baggage." Those words aren't wrong; they're just soft in a place where you need something hard. A struggle is something that happens to you. Sin is something you do. The first one you manage. The second one you repent of. And only one of those two roads leads anywhere.

Other men avoid it because it sounds too familiar. They grew up in churches that used the word like a club, and every time they hear it they feel eight years old and in trouble. I understand. But the word doesn't belong to the men who misused it. It belongs to God, and in His mouth it's a diagnosis, not a beating. A doctor who tells you the block is cracked isn't attacking you. He's the first person all summer who's told you the truth.

Here's what sin is. It's the bend in a man toward himself. The bend that makes him hide when he should stand up, blame when he should own, take when he should give, and go quiet when he should speak. It's what Adam did in the garden and what you did last Tuesday. God spoke to Cain about it before Cain ever raised a hand, and the picture He used is the best one I know.

:::margin
Genesis 4:7 (NIV)
"If you do what is right, will you not be accepted? But if you do not do what is right, sin is crouching at your door; it desires to have you, but you must rule over it."
:::

Crouching at your door. It's an animal. It wants you. And God says you must rule over it, which tells you two things. You're responsible, and you're not helpless. Cain didn't rule over it and it had him and his brother was dead in a field by the end of the chapter. Same animal. Same door. Every man.

## What it feels like from inside

Paul, who was as serious a man as ever lived, described the problem inside from the inside, and every honest man I've ever read it to has gone quiet.

:::margin
Romans 7:15 (NIV)
"I do not understand what I do. For what I want to do I do not do, but what I hate I do."
:::

That's it, isn't it. You want to be patient with your kids and you're not. You want to be present with your wife and you reach for the phone. You want to tell the truth and something smoother comes out. You want to pray and you sleep. You know what a man does, and you do the other thing, and you don't understand it, and neither did Paul.

And then he says the thing every man says at the bottom of himself, when he's finally quit fixing hoses.

:::margin
Romans 7:24-25 (NIV)
"What a wretched man I am! Who will rescue me from this body that is subject to death? Thanks be to God, who delivers me through Jesus Christ our Lord! So then, I myself in my mind am a slave to God's law, but in my sinful nature a slave to the law of sin."
:::

Who will rescue me. Not what will I do. Who. He stopped looking for a part and started looking for a Person. That's the turn. And the Person is named in the next breath.

## What to do with the problem inside

I'm not going to give you a program for defeating sin. Men have written libraries on it and most of them boil down to trying harder, which is a hose. Here's what actually works in men, and it's older than any program.

**Say the word about yourself.** Not "I've got some stuff." "I sinned." Out loud, to God, specific. "I lied to her about the money." "I looked at what I shouldn't have on Thursday." "I've been nursing a grudge against my brother for two years and enjoying it." David tried the other way for a while and described what it did to him.

:::margin
Psalm 32:3-5 (NIV)
"When I kept silent, my bones wasted away through my groaning all day long. For day and night your hand was heavy on me; my strength was sapped as in the heat of summer. Then I acknowledged my sin to you and did not cover up my iniquity. I said, 'I will confess my transgressions to the LORD.' And you forgave the guilt of my sin."
:::

When I kept silent, my bones wasted away. That's what a hidden crack does to a man. Then I acknowledged it. And you forgave. The whole distance between wasting away and being forgiven is one honest sentence.

**Know the difference between guilt and shame.** Guilt says "I did a wrong thing." Shame says "I am a wrong thing." Guilt is useful; it's the check-engine light. It tells you where to look, and once you've confessed, it goes off. Shame is useless. It doesn't tell you anything true about your standing with God, and it drives men straight back to their exits, because shame is exactly the feeling anger and appetite and absence are designed to numb. If you're in Christ, guilt is a signal. Shame is a lie. Learn to tell them apart, and don't let the liar drive.

**Turn, don't just feel bad.** Repentance isn't a feeling. It's a direction change. A man can feel terrible about something for twenty years and never repent of it, because he never turned. Turning looks like: confess it, make it right where you can, put something in place so you don't walk through that door as easily tomorrow, and get back to work. Then turn again tomorrow, because you'll need to. That's not failure. That's what a Christian life looks like from the inside: a man who keeps turning back toward the Father he keeps walking away from, and a Father who keeps receiving him.

**Bring a brother into it.** This is why chapter 13 comes before this one. Sin lives in the dark. It cannot survive being said to another man who loves you.

:::margin
James 5:16 (NIV)
"Therefore confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed. The prayer of a righteous person is powerful and effective."
:::

Confess to each other. Not just to God. There's something that happens when a man says the true thing to another man's face and the other man doesn't leave. Healed, James says. Not just forgiven. Healed. The twenty minutes was built for this. "What's heavy?" is where the sin comes out, and "what's next?" is where the turn gets said out loud.

**Ask for a steadfast spirit.** David, after the worst sin of his life, didn't ask God to fix his circumstances. He asked for a new inside.

:::margin
Psalm 51:10 (NIV)
"Create in me a pure heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me."
:::

A steadfast spirit. The block can't be patched. It has to be replaced, and only God can do that, and He does, and He keeps doing it. That's the prayer of a man who's stopped fixing the wrong thing.

## In your season

If you're young, learn this now, before the stakes go up. A single man who knows the problem is inside him and knows what to do about it walks into marriage with a tool most husbands don't have until they've done real damage.

If you've got a baby in the house, you're going to meet parts of yourself this year that you didn't know were there. Exhaustion strips paint. What's under the paint is the problem inside, and it's not new; it's just visible. Don't be shocked by it. Confess it and turn and get some sleep when you can.

If you're divorced, there's a version of this chapter you've been dreading, where I tell you the divorce was all your sin. I'm not going to tell you that, because I don't know, and neither does anyone but God and the two of you. But there's a part that's yours, however big or small, and you can't heal until you've named that part without attaching her part to it. "I did this" is a sentence you need to say, full stop. Then let God sort the rest.

If you're older, you may have been managing the outside for so long that you've forgotten there's an inside. Roy said he'd spent thirty years being a man other men respected and had never once said the word "sin" about himself out loud until he was sixty-nine. "I'd confessed in church a thousand times," he said. "In unison. With everybody. That's not the same as saying it about yourself, in your truck, by name." It's not too late. The block can be replaced at any age.

## The right thing

The old mechanic did the job. Pulled the engine, put in a rebuilt block, and the truck ran cool for another decade. And I remember standing in his shop feeling two things at once, which is the feeling I want to leave you with.

I felt foolish. I'd wasted a summer and a lot of money on the wrong things.

And I felt relieved, more relieved than I'd been in months, because finally somebody had told me the truth and the truth had a fix.

That's what it feels like to stop blaming the chair and say the word about yourself. Foolish, for a minute. And then relieved, all the way down, because the problem inside has a name and the name has a cure and the cure has already been paid for by a Man who knew exactly what was in you and came anyway.

You've been fixing the wrong thing. Stop. Say the word. Let Him replace the block.

---

**Name it.** Say the word about yourself, specifically, to God, out loud: "I sinned. I ___." Not "I struggle with." Not "I've got issues." The thing you did.

**Do this week.** Every night this week, before sleep, take two minutes and name the day's sins to God by name, and then say "thank you" for the forgiveness that's already yours in Christ. Seven nights. Notice what happens to the shame.

**Tell a brother.** Confess one of them to him. Out loud, to his face or on the phone. Let him pray for you. Notice that he doesn't leave.

**Carry this.** You've been fixing the wrong thing. The problem inside has a name, and the name has a cure, and the cure has been paid for.
