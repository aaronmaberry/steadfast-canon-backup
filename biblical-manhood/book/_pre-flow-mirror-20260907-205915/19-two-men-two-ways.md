# 19. Two Men, Two Ways

*Part 3: The story that names you*

Two gardens. Two men. Two nights when everything was on the line.

In the first garden the man had everything and lost it in an afternoon without saying a word. In the second garden the Man had nothing left but a choice, and He made it with His face in the dirt, and it saved the world.

Everything you've ever done as a man is a version of one of those two nights. Every time you've stood silent when you should have spoken, every time you've blamed when you should have owned, every time you've hidden when you should have stood up, every time you've grabbed the thing in front of you instead of trusting the thing that was promised, you were in the first garden, doing what the first man did. And every time you've done the opposite, on a Tuesday, when it cost you, you were following the second Man into the second garden.

The Bible puts the whole human story on two men. Not two teams, not two philosophies. Two men.

:::margin
Romans 5:19 (NIV)
"For just as through the disobedience of the one man the many were made sinners, so also through the obedience of the one man the many will be made righteous."
:::

The one man, and the one man. Which one you're in, and which one's way you're going to walk this week.

## The first garden

You know the story. Here's how a man should hear it.

The serpent comes into the garden. He doesn't go to the man. He goes to the woman, and he starts asking questions about the word God gave, the one about the tree. He twists it. He suggests God's holding out on them. He tells her she won't die; she'll be like God.

Now, where's the man?

:::margin
Genesis 3:6 (NIV)
"When the woman saw that the fruit of the tree was good for food and pleasing to the eye, and also desirable for gaining wisdom, she took some and ate it. She also gave some to her husband, who was with her, and he ate it."
:::

Who was with her. We read that in chapter 3 of this book and I told you those three words have been indicting men ever since. Here's why. Remember who received the command. Adam did, alone, from God's own mouth, before the woman existed. He was the one who'd been told to work the garden and *keep* it, to guard it. And when the thing he was supposed to guard against came through the fence and started twisting the word he'd been given, he stood there.

He didn't argue with the serpent. He didn't say "that's not what God said." He didn't take his wife by the hand and walk away from the tree. He didn't do anything. He watched. And then, when she handed it to him, he ate. The first sin of the first man wasn't grabbing the fruit. It was silence while the word was twisted. Passivity. The first mark, inverted, before any of the others.

Then God comes walking, and they hide, and God asks where he is, and the man answers.

:::margin
Genesis 3:10 (NIV)
"He answered, 'I heard you in the garden, and I was afraid because I was naked; so I hid.'"
:::

I was afraid, so I hid. The man who'd been naked and unashamed a page ago is now ashamed and hiding. That's the third mark inverted: cowardice, not courage. He heard God's voice, the voice he'd walked with in the cool of the day, and instead of going toward it he went into the trees.

Then God asks what he did, and he says the sentence we've talked about already: the woman you put here, she gave it to me. He blames his wife. He blames God. He leaves himself out. Second mark inverted: responsibility refused.

And under all of it, the fourth. Why did he eat? Because the serpent said "you will be like God," and that was a reward he could have right now, in his hand, instead of the reward of walking with God every evening for the rest of forever. He traded the greater reward for the closer one. He chose the fruit over the Father.

Four marks. Four inversions. In one afternoon. Passive, evasive, hiding, and grabbing. That's the first man's way, and it's the default setting of every man since, and if you've read the first seventeen chapters of this book you've seen your own face in it more than once. So have I.

And then the curse. Thorns in the work. Pain in the bearing. Death at the end. Exile from the garden. And the man who was made to keep it is put outside the fence with an angel guarding the gate against him.

But even there, two things. God made them clothes, with His own hands, from an animal that had to die to provide them. The first covering came at the cost of a life; hold that. And God said a word to the serpent, in the middle of the curse, that was actually a promise.

:::margin
Genesis 3:15 (NIV)
"And I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will crush your head, and you will strike his heel."
:::

He will crush your head. Somebody's coming. A son of the woman, who'll do what the first man didn't. Who'll stand in a garden when the enemy comes and won't be silent. It'll cost him; you will strike his heel. But he'll crush the thing that got in through the fence.

The second Man was promised in the first garden. Everything between Genesis 3 and the Gospels is the world waiting for Him.

## The second garden

It's night. A garden on a hillside outside Jerusalem, olive trees, a place He'd gone many times. He's brought His men, and He's taken three of them farther in, and He's told them to stay and watch. And then He goes on alone and falls on His face.

:::margin
Matthew 26:39 (NIV)
"Going a little farther, he fell with his face to the ground and prayed, 'My Father, if it is possible, may this cup be taken from me. Yet not as I will, but as you will.'"
:::

Look at what's on the table. The first man was offered fruit that would make him like God. The second Man is offered a cup that will make Him like a criminal, and He doesn't want it. He says so. Every honest fiber in Him is asking for another way. This isn't a Man who felt nothing. This is a Man sweating blood.

And then: not as I will, but as you will.

That's the second mark. The first man wouldn't carry the word he was given. The second Man picks up a cup He didn't fill, for a sin He didn't commit, and says: mine. I'll carry it. He accepts responsibility for the whole broken garden, all of it, every locked drawer and every slammed door and every silence, and He does it on His knees with dirt on His face.

He prays three times. The men He asked to watch fall asleep every time. He wakes them and they fall asleep again. He is completely alone, and He does not go into the trees. He does the opposite.

:::margin
Matthew 26:46 (NIV)
"Rise! Let us go! Here comes my betrayer!"
:::

Rise, let us go. The first man heard God coming and hid. The second Man hears the mob coming, torches and swords and a friend who's about to kiss Him, and He stands up and walks toward it. John tells it even more sharply.

:::margin
John 18:4 (NIV)
"Jesus, knowing all that was going to happen to him, went out and asked them, 'Who is it you want?'"
:::

Went out. He didn't wait for them to find Him in the trees. He went out to meet them. Knowing. That's the third mark. That's going first, into the worst thing, with nobody following, with the ones who promised to die with Him about to run. Courage isn't the absence of fear; we said that. Here's the proof. He was afraid enough to sweat blood and He went out anyway.

And then, while they're arresting Him, He does something the first man never thought to do. He covers His men.

:::margin
John 18:8 (NIV)
"'I told you that I am he,' Jesus answered. 'If you are looking for me, then let these men go.'"
:::

Let these men go. The first man, caught, pointed at his wife. The second Man, caught, points at Himself and tells them to leave everybody else alone. That's what responsibility looks like when it's finished: a man standing between the danger and the people he's been given, saying "me, not them."

And why? What was He working for? The first man grabbed for a reward he could have that afternoon. The second Man had one in view too, but it was on the other side of a cross. The writer of Hebrews said He endured it for the joy set before Him. And when it was done, when He'd carried the whole thing all the way to the end, He said one last thing about the work.

:::margin
John 19:30 (NIV)
"When he had received the drink, Jesus said, 'It is finished.' With that, he bowed his head and gave up his spirit."
:::

It is finished. The first man's last recorded words before the exile were an excuse. The second Man's last words before death were a completion report. The work I was given, I did, all of it. That's the fourth mark. He wasn't living for the crowd, which had turned. He wasn't living for His men, who'd fled. He was living for the Father's "well done," and He got it, three days later, and then some.

:::margin
Philippians 2:9-11 (NIV)
"Therefore God exalted him to the highest place and gave him the name that is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue acknowledge that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father."
:::

Therefore. Because He did it. Because He picked up the cup and went out to meet them and covered His men and finished. The reward came, and it was the greatest reward ever given, and it was given to a Man who'd looked like a loser to everyone watching on the day.

## Two ways, laid side by side

Put them next to each other. You're going to be standing in a garden this week and you need to see the two paths clearly.

The first man was silent when the word was twisted. The second Man spoke, even to the mob: "Who is it you want?"

The first man let the danger come to his wife and watched. The second Man went out to meet the danger before it reached His men.

The first man, caught, said "she did it." The second Man, caught, said "take me, let them go."

The first man hid from God's voice in the trees. The second Man sought the Father's face in the dirt, three times, and then got up.

The first man grabbed the reward in his hand. The second Man trusted the reward on the other side of the cross.

The first man's last words were an excuse. The second Man's were "it is finished."

Look at the four marks now. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. When we laid them out in chapter 3, they might have sounded like a list of qualities, the kind of thing you'd find on any shelf of books about men. They're not that. They are the shape of the second Man in the second garden, written out so a man can carry it in his pocket. Rejecting passivity is "Rise, let us go." Accepting responsibility is "not as I will, but as you will." Leading courageously is "went out and asked them." Expecting the greater reward is "for the joy set before him." The marks aren't a personality profile. They're a portrait of Jesus in the worst hour of His life, and every time you live one of them on a Tuesday, you're wearing His face.

## Which man are you in

Men skip this when they turn the two gardens into a pep talk.

The Bible doesn't just say "be like the second man and not the first." It says something stranger and better. It says you're *in* one of them.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 15:22 (NIV)
"For as in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive."
:::

In Adam. In Christ. You were born in the first man. His way is your default. That's why passivity comes so easy, why blame is the first thing out of your mouth, why hiding feels natural and grabbing feels right. It's not just that you copy Adam. You were in him when he did it, the way a family's in a father. His garden is your inheritance, and there's no amount of trying that gets you out of it.

But God's not asking you to try your way out. He's offering to move you. Into the second Man. Into Christ, so that what He did in His garden counts as yours the way what Adam did counted as yours. His obedience becomes your record. His "it is finished" becomes the last word over your life instead of the excuse. That's the gospel, and it's the only reason a man can read the first half of this chapter and not despair.

:::margin
Galatians 2:20 (NIV)
"I have been crucified with Christ and I no longer live, but Christ lives in me. The life I now live in the body, I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me."
:::

Christ lives in me. That's not a metaphor for trying hard. It's a location change. A man in Christ has the second Man's Spirit in him, the same Spirit that got up off the ground in Gethsemane, and that's why he can get up off his own. Not because he's stronger than Adam. Because he's not in Adam anymore.

So if you've never done it, do it. Not at the end of the chapter. Now. Tell God you've been in the first man's garden your whole life, doing his silence and his blame and his hiding and his grabbing, and you're done. Tell Him you want to be in the second Man, that you're trusting what He did in His garden to cover what you did in yours. That's the door. It's the only one, and it's open, and it's the beginning of everything else in this book actually working.

And if you've done it already, then the rest: you're in Him. The four marks aren't a standard you're trying to reach so He'll accept you. They're the family resemblance showing up, because you're His.

## Tuesday's garden

I told you you'd stand in a garden this week. Here's what it'll look like. It won't have olive trees.

It'll be a kitchen. Your wife says something that isn't quite fair and every fiber in you wants to go quiet and let it sit, or fire back with the thing you've been saving. That's the garden. The first man's way is silence or blame. The second Man's way is a soft, clear word: "That landed hard. Can we talk about it after the kids are down?" Rise, let us go.

It'll be a phone at 11 p.m. with nobody watching. That's the garden. The first man grabbed what was in his hand. The second Man trusted the reward on the far side of a hard night. Put it on the charger in the kitchen. Go to bed. For the joy set before you.

It'll be a mess at work that's partly your fault and mostly someone else's, and everybody's waiting to see who gets blamed. That's the garden. The first man said "she did it." The second Man said "let these men go." Own your piece first, out loud, before anyone asks.

It'll be 2 a.m. and the baby's crying and you can hear your wife starting to move and you could just lie there. That's the garden. Not as I will.

It'll be your father's driveway and a conversation you've been avoiding for ten years. That's the garden. Went out and asked them.

The gardens are small. That's the point of the Tuesday spine this whole book runs on. Adam's afternoon looked small too; it was one piece of fruit. Gethsemane looked like one man praying alone in the dark. The size of the garden has nothing to do with the size of what's decided in it.

## In your season

If you're single, notice that the second Man was single. He stood in His garden without a wife, and He was the fullest man who ever lived. Don't let anyone, including yourself, tell you that your manhood is on hold until you're married. The garden is where you are. Stand in it.

If you've got a baby in the house, Gethsemane is a 2 a.m. story. A Man who didn't want to, who asked for another way, who got up and did it anyway because the Father asked. You're going to pray "may this cup be taken" a lot this year, about sleep, about your marriage, about your own patience. Pray it. He did. Then say the second sentence and get up.

If you're divorced, look hard at "let these men go." The first man's way after a marriage breaks is to point: at her, at the lawyers, at God who put her here. The second Man's way is to stand between the wreckage and the people you've been given and say "me, not them." Your kids should never have to carry your war. Cover them. Own your part out loud, and let God sort hers.

If you're older, think about last words. The first man's were an excuse. The second Man's were "it is finished." You've got a stretch of road left and you get to decide what the completion report says. Not "it wasn't my fault" but "the work You gave me, I did." Roy said he'd started praying for that, specifically: "Let me finish. Whatever's left, let me finish it." That's a Gethsemane prayer from a man in his seventies, and it's exactly right.

## The last Adam

Paul gave Jesus a title I've come to love.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 15:45 (NIV)
"So it is written: 'The first man Adam became a living being'; the last Adam, a life-giving spirit."
:::

The last Adam. Not the second in a series. The last. The one who finally did what the first was supposed to do, in a garden, when it came through the fence. There's never going to be another one, because there doesn't need to be. He finished it.

And He's not asking you to be Him. He's asking you to be in Him, and then to walk His way through your Tuesday gardens with His Spirit in you and His record over you. Two men, two ways. You were born in one. You've been moved into the other. Now go stand in your garden like the Man you're in.

**Name it.** Think of the last garden you stood in this week: the kitchen, the phone, the 2 a.m. Which man's way did you take? Say it plainly: "I did what the first man did when I ___."

**Do this week.** Each morning, name the garden you're most likely to face and say the second Man's sentence for it out loud: "Rise, let us go." "Not as I will." "Let these men go." Then walk into the day.

**Tell a brother.** Tell him which man you've been in, in the deepest sense. If you've never trusted Christ to move you out of Adam, tell him that and let him walk you through it. If you have, tell him one garden you walked the second Man's way through.

**Carry this.** The four marks are not a list of qualities. They're the portrait of Jesus in the worst hour of His life. Every time you live one, you're wearing His face.
