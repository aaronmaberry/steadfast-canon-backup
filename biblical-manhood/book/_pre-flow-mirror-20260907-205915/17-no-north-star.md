# 17. No North Star

*Part 2: What you inherited, and what is yours*

An old rancher taught me to find the North Star when I was a boy, standing in a pasture outside of Post with no lights for twenty miles in any direction. He showed me the Big Dipper first, and then how to follow the two stars on the end of the cup up and out until you hit a star that wasn't very bright and didn't look like much.

"That one doesn't move," he said. "Everything else up there goes around all night. That one stays put. You get lost, you find that one, and you know which way's which."

I asked him why it didn't move. He said he didn't know and didn't need to. "It just doesn't. That's what makes it useful."

Years later a mason taught me something similar with a plumb line, which is a string with a weight on the end. You hang it next to a wall and the weight settles and the string points straight down, every time, no matter how the wall looks, because gravity doesn't have opinions. If the wall's off, the string tells you. The wall doesn't get to argue with the string.

Two things that don't move. Most men are measuring their lives against things that do.

## Everyone did as they saw fit

There's a sentence near the end of the book of Judges that's the saddest summary of a people I know.

:::margin
Judges 21:25 (NIV)
"In those days Israel had no king; everyone did as they saw fit."
:::

Everyone did as they saw fit. Not everyone did evil. That's the thing. Some of what they did was probably decent. But nobody was measuring by anything outside themselves. Each man's own judgment was his north star, and each man's north star moved with him, so nobody could tell they were drifting because everybody was drifting together.

That's most men I know. Not bad men. Drifting men. They're measuring themselves against a reference that moves, and so they don't feel the drift, and one day they're forty miles off course in a marriage or a career or a faith and they honestly can't tell you how they got there. Nobody can, if the thing they were steering by was moving too.

Here's what men use as a north star, and why every one of them moves.

**Their fathers.** For most men the first fixed point was Dad. What he did was what a man does. And then you grew up and found out he was a moving star too, sometimes wildly, and if he was the empty chair you never even had a star to fix on. A father is a good thing to learn from and a terrible thing to steer by.

**Other men.** This is the big one. "I'm doing better than him." I make more than my brother-in-law. I'm home more than the guys on the crew. I've never cheated, like the deacon did. I go to church more than my neighbor. Every one of those comparisons feels like a measurement and every one is a man measuring his wall against another crooked wall. You can be the straightest wall in a crooked town and still be off plumb.

**The culture.** What everybody knows a man should be. The trouble is that what everybody knows changes about every eight years now, and if you're steering by it you're steering by a star that jumps. What made a man in your grandfather's day and what makes one on the screens your son watches are not the same star. Chapter 2 already told you you're not crazy for noticing that.

**Their feelings.** "I just don't feel like this is right anymore." "I feel like God's leading me to." "I don't feel anything when I pray." Feelings are real and they matter, and we'll give them a whole chapter in Part 5. But a feeling is the most moving star there is. It changes with sleep and blood sugar and the weather and what you ate. A man who steers by his feelings is a man who's decided to navigate by the clouds.

**Their success.** If it's working, it must be right. The business grew, so I must be doing something right with my life. The marriage hasn't blown up, so it must be fine. David ran a kingdom very well for a year with a murdered man in his drawer. Success is not a plumb line. It's just a wall that hasn't fallen yet.

:::margin
Proverbs 14:12 (NIV)
"There is a way that appears to be right, but in the end it leads to death."
:::

Appears to be right. That's the drift. It never feels like drifting. It feels like a reasonable way, and you only find out at the end.

## The one that doesn't move

God has a plumb line. He told Amos about it.

:::margin
Amos 7:7-8 (NIV)
"This is what he showed me: The Lord was standing by a wall that had been built true to plumb, with a plumb line in his hand. And the LORD asked me, 'What do you see, Amos?' 'A plumb line,' I replied. Then the Lord said, 'Look, I am setting a plumb line among my people Israel; I will spare them no longer.'"
:::

I am setting a plumb line among my people. God holds a string with a weight on it up against the wall of a man's life, and the string points true, and the wall doesn't get to argue. That's what His Word is. Not a set of rules, though it has rules in it. Not a book of inspiring thoughts, though it'll inspire you. It's the fixed reference. The thing that doesn't move so you can tell what does.

:::margin
Psalm 119:105 (NIV)
"Your word is a lamp for my feet, a light on my path."
:::

A lamp for my feet. Not a floodlight for the whole valley. Enough light for the next step, which is what a man on a Tuesday actually needs. And the reason it can be that is that it's the one thing in your life that isn't drifting along with you.

Paul told Timothy the same thing near the end of his life, when Timothy was surrounded by people doing as they saw fit.

:::margin
2 Timothy 3:16-17 (NIV)
"All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training in righteousness, so that the servant of God may be thoroughly equipped for every good work."
:::

Teaching, rebuking, correcting, training. Look at that list. It teaches you what's true. It rebukes you when you're off; that's the string against the wall. It corrects you, which means it shows you how to get back to plumb. And it trains you, over years, until your hands start to build true without checking every course. Thoroughly equipped. That's a man with a north star.

## Why men don't use it

Here's what men say.

*I don't know how.* Nobody taught you. Your father didn't read it, or read it in a way that made it look like homework. You've tried starting at Genesis in January and you were in Leviticus by February and that was that. That's not a moral failure; it's a training failure, and it can be fixed in about a month.

*It feels like homework.* It does, if you read it like a textbook. Don't. Read it like a man listening to his Father talk. Read it out loud in the truck. Read it with a question in your hand: what does this say a man does?

*I don't have time.* You have twenty minutes for a brother, and you have ten for this. Chapter 34 will put it in the week for you. A man who says he doesn't have ten minutes for the only thing in his life that doesn't move has told you what he's steering by.

*I don't feel anything when I read it.* Good. You're not reading it to feel something. You're reading it to find out which way's north. The rancher didn't feel anything about that dim star either. He just knew it didn't move.

## How a man reads

Here's how to start, if you never have, or if you've tried and quit.

Start with one Gospel. Mark is shortest; John is deepest. Read a chapter a day. You'll be done in two or three weeks and you'll have watched the Man you're following do everything He did, and you'll know Him better than most men who've been in church thirty years.

Then read Proverbs, a chapter a day, for a month. There are thirty-one chapters, one for each day. It's a father talking to his son about money and women and work and anger and friends, and it's the most practical thing ever written for men, and you can read it for fifty years and it'll still be telling you where the wall's off.

Pray the Psalms. When you don't know how to pray, which is most mornings, open to a Psalm and read it out loud to God as your own. David was a man with every exit you have and he prayed his way through all of them, and he left you the words.

Ask one question every time: what does this say a man does? Not what does this mean in the original language. Not what would a scholar say. What does this say a man does, today, on Tuesday? Then do it.

Read it with your brother. In the twenty minutes, one of you reads the verse you've been sitting on and says why. Iron sharpens iron better with a plumb line in the room.

And read it out loud when you can. In the truck, on the porch, at the table with your kids. There's something about hearing the Word in your own voice that makes it stop being homework and start being a Father talking.

## A story bigger than your story

The north star is more than a rule book.

We've spent this whole second part on your story. The chair, the exits, the handoff, the brothers you didn't have, the problem inside and how it looks on you. All of that is your story and it's real and God knows every line of it.

But your story is not the master story. It's a chapter in something much older and much bigger, and if all you know is your own chapter, you'll read it wrong. You'll think the empty chair is the beginning, and it isn't. You'll think your sin is the end, and it isn't. You'll think the point of your life is to fix what was done to you, and it isn't.

The master story starts before the chair, in a garden, with a Man who was built for something. It turns on two men and two ways of being a man. And it ends with a destiny that isn't a feeling. That's the next three chapters: the whole story of manhood, from the first man to the last, held up against the plumb line, so you can see where you are in it.

Because a man with no north star isn't just lost on Tuesday. He's lost in the story. He doesn't know what he was made for, so he can't tell if he's doing it. The fixed reference isn't just a set of instructions. It's a story that tells you who you are.

## The house that held

Jesus ended the longest sermon He ever gave with a picture that every man in Texas understands.

:::margin
Matthew 7:24-25 (NIV)
"Therefore everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man who built his house on the rock. The rain came down, the streams rose, and the winds blew and beat against that house; yet it did not fall, because it had its foundation on the rock."
:::

Hears these words and puts them into practice. Not hears. Hears and does. That's the wise man. He's building on something that doesn't move, and when the weather comes, and it comes for every house, his holds.

The other man in that story built on sand. He probably built a nice house. It probably looked fine right up until the rain. He was doing as he saw fit, measuring by walls that were also on sand, and he never knew until the water rose.

The rancher's dead now. The pasture outside of Post is still there and so is the star. I've found it on a lot of nights since, from a lot of places, and it's never once moved, and every time I look at it I think about a man who told a boy that you don't have to understand why a thing doesn't move to be saved by it.

Find the one that doesn't move. Hold the string against your wall. Then turn the page. The next part is the story that string is measuring you by.
