# 11. When Home Never Handed You Off

*Part 2: What you inherited, and what is yours*

Micah asked me a question at his grandfather's funeral that I've never forgotten. He was twenty-six, standing at the back of a fellowship hall with a paper plate of somebody's casserole, watching the older men from the church talk in a loose circle by the coffee.

"When did you know you were a man?"

I asked him what he meant. He said he honestly couldn't tell me when it had happened for him, or whether it had. He had a job. He paid his rent. He'd voted in two presidential elections. And he still felt, in that fellowship hall, like a kid who'd wandered into the men's section, waiting for somebody to notice and either send him out or tell him he could stay.

"Nobody ever said it," he told me. "Nobody ever said, 'You're a man now.' I think I've been waiting for somebody to say it."

He's not unusual. He's just honest. Most men in this country never got handed off. They went from boy to man the way a river goes from one county to the next: at some point they were across the line, and there was no sign, and nobody was standing there.

## What a handoff is

In most of human history, boys didn't become men by accident. They became men because men made them men, on purpose, at a certain point, with words and a weight and a welcome. The details changed from one people to the next, but the shape was the same. The older men took the boy from the women, put him through something, told him what a man was, gave him a man's responsibility, and brought him into their company. He went to bed a boy and woke up a man, not because his body had changed but because the men had said so and meant it.

The Bible does this. It's not just an anthropology thing. Watch David, dying, hand off to Solomon.

:::margin
1 Kings 2:2-3 (NIV)
"'I am about to go the way of all the earth,' he said. 'So be strong, act like a man, and observe what the LORD your God requires: Walk in obedience to him, and keep his decrees and commands, his laws and regulations, as written in the Law of Moses. Do this so that you may prosper in all you do and wherever you go'"
:::

Look at what's in that. A man, older, about to leave. A word: act like a man, and here's what that means. A weight: a kingdom, and God's law to keep in it. Moses did the same with Joshua, in front of the whole people. Paul did it with Timothy, whose own father barely rates a mention in Scripture, and he did it with his hands.

:::margin
2 Timothy 1:6 (NIV)
"For this reason I remind you to fan into flame the gift of God, which is in you through the laying on of my hands."
:::

Through the laying on of my hands. Paul had put his hands on the young man and said, in effect, "You. You're one of us. Here's your work." And years later, when Timothy was scared and tired and thinking about quitting, Paul reminded him: remember when I handed you off. Fan it back into flame.

That's a handoff. Four things: a man, a word, a weight, and a welcome. Most of you got none of them. Or you got the weight without the word, a father who piled responsibility on you at twelve and never once told you that you were doing a man's job. Or you got words without weight, a dad who said "you're the man of the house now" when he left and then never came back to see whether you were.

## The counterfeits

A boy who isn't handed off doesn't stop wanting to be. He just goes looking for it somewhere else, and the world has counterfeits ready.

The first is sex. For a lot of boys, the first time is the closest thing to a rite of passage they'll ever get, and they treat it that way, and it doesn't work, because there's no older man in the room and no word and no weight. Just a girl who didn't sign up to make him a man and couldn't have anyway.

The second is a paycheck. A job feels like manhood, and it's part of it, but a boy with a paycheck is still a boy. Ask any foreman.

The third is a fight, or a truck, or a drink, or turning eighteen or twenty-one. Every one of them is a boy trying to hand himself off, and you can't hand yourself off. That's the whole point. A handoff is something a man receives from men.

The fourth counterfeit is the quietest, and it's the one Micah was living in. It's waiting. A man who was never handed off can spend his whole adult life waiting for someone to tell him he's allowed in the circle by the coffee. He'll defer to men his own age. He'll shrink around older men. He'll be forty-five and still feel like he's auditioning for a room he's been standing in for twenty years.

And here's the cost of that. A man who doesn't know he's a man doesn't take a man's weight. Why would he? He's waiting for someone to tell him it's his. He doesn't go first. He doesn't reject passivity. He can't, really, because underneath everything he's still a boy waiting for permission. Every one of the four marks depends on this. You can't act like a man for very long if nobody, including you, has ever said you are one.

## Jacob's night

There's a man in Genesis who spent his whole life trying to get a handoff by the wrong door.

Jacob wanted his father's blessing so badly he stole it. He dressed up as his brother, lied to a blind old man, and got the words. And it didn't work. He spent the next twenty years running, scheming, working for a man who cheated him, still trying to get somewhere. The stolen blessing didn't make him a man. It made him a fugitive.

Then one night, alone, on the way home to face everything, a Man came and wrestled him till dawn. And Jacob did the one thing he'd never done. He stopped scheming and just held on.

:::margin
Genesis 32:26 (NIV)
"Then the man said, 'Let me go, for it is daybreak.' But Jacob replied, 'I will not let you go unless you bless me.'"
:::

I will not let you go unless you bless me. That's a boy who's finally stopped stealing what he needs and started asking for it, from the only One who could actually give it. And he got it. He got a new name, Israel, which meant he wasn't Jacob the grabber anymore. He got a limp, which meant he'd never walk like a boy again. And he walked toward his brother the next morning like a man.

The handoff Jacob spent his life trying to get from his father, he finally received from God, on the ground, in the dark, at forty-something years old. If you're forty-something and it's dark, it is not too late for you to be handed off.

## How a man receives it now

So how does a grown man get what should have been given to him at fourteen? Here's what works.

**Hear it from the Father first.** We said last chapter that the Father speaks identity before performance. He already said the words over you. "This is my son, whom I love." If you're in Christ, that's not a hope; it's a legal fact. The handoff you never got from your dad, you have from your Father, and it outranks anything a man could say. Start there or nothing else will hold.

**Ask an older man to say it.** This will feel like the most awkward thing in this book. Do it anyway. Find a man older than you who's living the four marks, and tell him the truth: nobody ever told me I was a man. Would you? And then let him. Let him put a hand on your shoulder and say it. Chapter 14 is about finding that man. You'd be surprised how many older men have been waiting their whole lives for a younger man to ask them for something real.

**Be welcomed by brothers.** A handoff has a welcome in it. You need a circle of men who know you and count you as one of them. Not a crowd. A few. Chapter 13. Micah's whole question at that funeral was really "am I allowed in the circle," and the answer is that the circle should have pulled him in years ago, and if it didn't, he needs to find one that will.

**Take the weight before you feel ready.** Something about the David and Solomon handoff I missed for years. David says "act like a man." It's a command. Solomon didn't feel like a man; he was young and scared and said so to God a chapter later. David told him to act like one anyway, and the acting was part of how he became one. Some of you are waiting to feel handed off before you pick up the weight. Reverse it. Pick up the weight. Carry it for a year. The men around you will start treating you like a man because you're doing a man's work, and one day you'll notice the feeling caught up.

**Mark it.** Write down the date. The day you decided, the day the older man said it, the day you asked God on the ground in the dark. Men need markers. Jacob got a limp; you may need to settle for a date on the inside cover of your Bible. But have something you can point to and say: that's when. Not because the date is magic. Because a man who can't point to when he became a man will always half-wonder whether he did.

## When the man in the chair won't hand you off

Cole's father is alive and present and has never once, in thirty-three years, told his son he did a good job. Cole rebuilt a transmission at seventeen; his dad found the one bolt he'd torqued wrong. Cole got a promotion at twenty-nine; his dad asked why it took so long. Cole's still trying. He calls every Sunday. He fixes things at the old man's house. He's waiting for a sentence that isn't coming.

If that's you, the hard thing: Some fathers will never hand you off, not because you haven't earned it but because they never received it themselves and don't know how, or because keeping you a boy is the only way they know to stay a man. You can honor that father. You should. Chapter 12 is about how. But you cannot wait on him. Every year you spend waiting for his sentence is a year you don't spend being the man God already said you are.

Cole finally heard it from a seventy-year-old deacon named Hollis who'd watched him serve for six years and one Sunday pulled him aside by the truck and said, "Son, I've been watching you. You're a good man. I'd trust you with anything I've got." Cole sat in his truck and cried for twenty minutes. He was thirty-three. It was the first time.

That's a handoff. It didn't come from the chair. It came from a man who was willing to stand in for the chair, and God arranges those men for those who ask.

## The other side of this

If you're the older man reading this:

You probably never got handed off either. Most men your age didn't. And you may be thinking that means you can't give what you didn't get. That's wrong. You've lived the four marks, or you've lived enough of them to know what they look like. You've watched younger men and thought, that one's got it, and never said so. Say so. That's the whole job.

Roy started doing this at his Thursday table almost by accident. One of the men in his thirties told him about a hard call he'd made at work, standing up to a boss over something dishonest, and Roy said, "That's what a man does. You did right." The young man didn't say anything. But he came back the next week early, and the week after, and a year later he told Roy that sentence was the first time any man over fifty had ever told him he'd done right about anything.

You don't need a ceremony. Chapter 29 is about ceremonies, and they're good. But most of the handoffs in Scripture were a man, a word, a hand on a shoulder, and a "go." You can do that at a truck window. You can do it this week.

## In your season

If you're divorced, the divorce may have un-manned you in your own eyes. You had a handoff of sorts, a wedding, a house, a role, and it came apart, and now you're not sure what you are. The Father's word over you didn't get revoked in a courtroom. You're still His son. You're still a man. The weight you're carrying now, kids across two houses, a life rebuilt from a one-bedroom apartment, is a man's weight, and you're carrying it. Let someone older say so.

If you're a young father, you're about to be the one who hands off, and you'll be tempted to think you can't because you never got it. You can. Part 4 will show you how, and you'll find that giving it heals something in the giving.

## Micah, five years on

Micah's thirty-one now. A man named Dale, twenty years older, took him to breakfast every other Tuesday for two years and one morning told him plainly that he was a man and it was time to stop waiting for permission to act like one. Micah wrote the date inside his Bible. He got married last spring. And at his own wedding, at the back of a different fellowship hall, he told me he'd caught himself looking around at the older men by the coffee and realizing, for the first time, that he wasn't waiting to be let in.

He was one of them.

You're allowed in the circle. You always were. The Father already said so. Now go find a man who'll say it out loud, and then go be one who does.

---

**Name it.** Did anyone ever hand you off? Say who and when, or say plainly: "No one ever told me I was a man." Say which counterfeit you went looking for instead.

**Do this week.** If you're under fifty: ask one older man who's living the marks to tell you honestly whether he sees a man in you. Take whatever he says. If you're over fifty: find one younger man and tell him, plainly, one thing you've seen him do that a man does.

**Tell a brother.** Tell him whether you got handed off. Then tell him the date you're going to write in your Bible, and what it marks.

**Carry this.** A man, a word, a weight, and a welcome. You can't hand yourself off. But the Father already did, and He's arranged men to say it out loud if you'll ask.
