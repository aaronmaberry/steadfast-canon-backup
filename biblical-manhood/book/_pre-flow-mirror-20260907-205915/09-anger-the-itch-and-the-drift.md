# 9. Anger, the Itch, and the Drift

*Part 2: What you inherited, and what is yours*

Three men sat at the same table at a Thursday breakfast in a town I won't name. They didn't know each other well. They'd have said they didn't have much in common.

The first one had thrown a drill across his shop the day before because a screw stripped. Not at anybody. Just threw it, and then stood there breathing hard while his apprentice pretended not to have seen. He's got a wife who's started flinching when his voice changes pitch, and he hates that, and he doesn't know what to do about it, so mostly he gets angry about that too.

The second one had been up until two in the morning on his phone again, and not reading the news. He's single, twenty-six, a decent man who's in church every Sunday and has lied to every accountability partner he's ever had. He tells himself it doesn't hurt anybody. He knows it does, and he knows who.

The third one hadn't done anything. That's the point. He's fifty-two, he's been married twenty-six years, and he can't remember the last time he felt much of anything. He works, he comes home, he watches whatever's on, he goes to bed, and his wife told him last month that she feels like she's living with a roommate, and the honest truth is that he didn't feel much about that either.

Three men. Anger, the itch, and the drift. Sitting there over eggs, they don't know they've got the same wound. They just took three different exits.

## Three exits from the same pain

Last chapter I said every man inherited something from the chair at the end of the table. This chapter is about where it goes. Because pain doesn't sit still in a man. It moves. It has to go somewhere, and it almost always goes to one of three places.

It goes out, as anger. It goes down, as appetite. Or it goes away, as absence. Every man I've ever sat with has a home exit, the one he takes when things get heavy, and most of them have never noticed that it's an exit at all. They think it's their personality. It isn't. It's the way they learned to not feel something a long time ago, and it's become a habit they call themselves.

## Anger

Anger is the exit that looks like strength, which is why men like it.

The man who threw the drill would tell you he has a temper. That's what men call it, like it's a feature of the truck. But listen to what anger actually is, underneath. Nearly every time I've sat with an angry man long enough to get below the anger, I've found one of three things: fear, shame, or helplessness. He's afraid the money's not going to work. He's ashamed of something he did or something he is. He's watching something he loves go wrong and can't stop it. And instead of feeling any of those, which would feel like weakness, he feels the one thing that feels like power. He gets mad.

A lot of male anger is grief with its fists up.

The Bible doesn't tell you not to be angry. It tells you what to do with it, and it's specific.

:::margin
Ephesians 4:26-27 (NIV)
"'In your anger do not sin': Do not let the sun go down while you are still angry, and do not give the devil a foothold."
:::

In your anger. It assumes you'll have it. Then it gives you a deadline, sundown, and a warning: this is where the enemy gets a foot in the door of your house. Not through the big sins. Through the anger you carried to bed and woke up with and carried to bed again, until it was just the weather in your home.

:::margin
Proverbs 29:11 (NIV)
"Fools give full vent to their rage, but the wise bring calm in the end."
:::

Full vent. That's the drill across the shop. That's the voice that changes pitch. A fool thinks letting it all out is honest. A wise man knows that what comes out in full vent isn't the truth; it's the pressure. The truth is underneath, and it's usually quieter and more frightened than the rage.

Here's what's yours to do, if anger is your exit. First, learn your ninety seconds. Anger in the body peaks and starts to fall in about a minute and a half if you don't feed it. Walk out to the truck. Don't say the sentence you've got loaded. Let it peak. Second, come back and say what's under it. "I'm scared about the money." "I feel like I'm failing at this." "I don't know how to fix it and that makes me crazy." Your wife can work with a scared man. She can't work with a storm. Third, don't let the sun go down. Whatever it takes. The apology, the conversation, the "I'm not okay but I'm not leaving." Before bed.

And know that the man who conquers this doesn't become soft. He becomes the strongest man in the room.

:::margin
Proverbs 16:32 (NIV)
"Better a patient person than a warrior, one with self-control than one who takes a city."
:::

## The itch

The second exit goes down. Into appetite. And I'm going to talk mostly about the one that's got the most men by the throat, which is sexual, but everything I say applies to the bottle, the plate, the cart, the scroll, and the sports book. It's all the same itch. Comfort, now, without cost, and without being known.

The twenty-six-year-old on his phone at two in the morning isn't chasing pleasure, mostly. I've talked to enough men to know that. He's chasing relief. Relief from loneliness, from failure, from a Tuesday that didn't go well, from a body that's tired and a heart that hasn't been seen by anyone in a week. The screen doesn't ask anything of him. It doesn't need him to be a man. That's the pull. And that's why it's a lie, because being needed to be a man is exactly what would heal him.

James lays out the mechanics in a few lines, and I've never seen them improved on.

:::margin
James 1:14-15 (NIV)
"but each person is tempted when they are dragged away by their own evil desire and enticed. Then, after desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, gives birth to death."
:::

Dragged away. That's the feeling, isn't it. Nobody plans to be up at two. And the end of the road is death: not usually a dramatic one, but the slow death of a man's ability to be present, to want a real woman, to feel a real thing, to look his future wife in the eye without a hidden room in his chest.

Here's what the itch is stealing. Most men have never done the accounting. If you're single, it's stealing from the woman you haven't met yet. Every image trains you to want something she isn't, and she's going to pay for that training on her honeymoon and for years after. If you're married, it's stealing from your wife directly, and she knows something's off even if she doesn't know what. Jesus said what you look at with intent is already adultery, and He wasn't being dramatic. He was being accurate.

:::margin
1 Corinthians 6:18-20 (NIV)
"Flee from sexual immorality. All other sins a person commits are outside the body, but whoever sins sexually, sins against their own body. Do you not know that your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit, who is in you, whom you have received from God? You are not your own; you were bought at a price. Therefore honor God with your bodies."
:::

Flee. Not manage. Not moderate. Flee, the way Joseph ran from a house with a woman in it who wanted him. There's no dignified way to fight this one. There's only running.

Here's what's yours. First, tell one man the whole truth. Not "I struggle with purity." The actual truth: what, how often, since when, what it's costing. The itch lives on secrecy, and it starts to die the day a brother knows. Second, cut the supply. Filters on every device, with the password held by that brother. The phone charging in the kitchen at night, not the bedroom. Yes, that feels like a child's rule. You've been acting like a child at two in the morning; a child's rule is appropriate until you're not. Third, fill the hour. The itch comes in the empty spaces. Find out what your empty spaces are and put something real in them: a man to call, a walk, a book with pages, a prayer with words. Fourth, deal with the loneliness, because that's what this actually is. Chapter 13 is about brothers. The itch shrinks in men who are known.

## The drift

The third exit is the quiet one, and it's the one I worry about most in men over forty-five, because nobody notices it and it feels like nothing's wrong.

The drift is a man leaving without moving. He's in the house. He goes to work. He hasn't blown up and he hasn't blown it. He's just not there. His attention lives somewhere else: the game, the news, fantasy football, a boat, the job, the phone, the garage. His wife stopped asking him things a while ago because his answers were "fine" and "whatever you think." His kids learned early that Dad's body was in the recliner and Dad was somewhere else.

This is passivity, the thing we talked about in chapter 4, gone chronic. And what's under it is usually one of two things. Either the man got hurt somewhere along the way and decided, without ever saying it, that caring was too expensive. Or he got tired, honestly tired, and rest turned into retreat, and he never came back.

The Bible has a picture for this that's almost funny until you see your own house in it.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 10:18 (NIV)
"Through laziness, the rafters sag; because of idle hands, the house leaks."
:::

Nobody kicked the rafters. Nobody punched a hole in the roof. The house just leaked, slowly, because the man who should've been on the ladder was in the recliner. The drift doesn't destroy a home in a night. It destroys it in fifteen years, one evening at a time, and by the time anybody names it the rafters are already bowed.

Straight to older men: the drift is the exit that comes for you hardest after the working years end. You've had a place to be and a reason to get up for forty years. Then it stops. And there's a version of retirement that's really just the drift with a fishing license. The television gets more of you than your wife does. You get angry about the news, which is the drift and anger holding hands. Roy told me he watched three men from his old unit go that way in the first five years after they turned in their badges, and he'd started to go too. "I was a spectator in my own house," he said. "I'd never been a spectator in my life." What pulled him back was one decision: he got up before his wife every morning and made the coffee and sat with her while she drank it. That's a small thing. It's also a man refusing to drift, one morning at a time.

Here's what's yours, if the drift is your exit. First, name it. Say to your wife, "I've been gone. I've been in the house and I've been gone, and I'm sorry." She knows. Hearing you say it will change something. Second, take one thing back. Not your whole life. One evening a week with the television off and your attention in the room. One question a day to your wife or your kid that you actually wait for the answer to. Third, find out what you stopped caring about and why. Somewhere back there, a man decided it wasn't worth it. Go find that man and talk to him. You might need a brother or a counselor to help you find him. That's not weakness. That's a man on a ladder looking at the rafters.

## The same root

The three men at the breakfast had the same wound. Here's how.

The angry man is a boy who learned that feeling scared got him hurt, so he swapped it for something that felt like power. The itching man is a boy who learned that he wouldn't be seen, so he found a place that would give him something like comfort without requiring him to be known. The drifting man is a boy who learned that caring cost too much, so he stopped.

Every exit is a way to not feel the empty chair. And every exit works, for a while, which is why men keep taking them. And every exit leads out of the house.

You've already tried just stopping. The men who actually get free of these things get free the same way, and it's not by gritting their teeth harder. It's by taking the pain somewhere it can be healed instead of somewhere it can be numbed. That's the next chapter. There's a Father who does not leave, and He's not afraid of your anger, and He's not disgusted by your itch, and He hasn't drifted from you while you drifted from Him.

He knows the boy under the exit. He's been waiting for the man to bring him home.

## In your season

If you're young and single, the itch is probably your loudest exit, and I've said what I have to say about it. But watch the drift too. A man can drift at twenty-six, into games and screens and a job he doesn't care about and a church he attends without ever being known in. Don't wait until you're fifty to notice you've been gone.

If you've got a baby in the house, anger is going to surprise you. You didn't think you were an angry man, and then it's 3 a.m. and the baby won't stop and your wife says something and you hear a voice come out of you that you last heard from your father. That's exhaustion opening a door your father built. Ninety seconds. Out to the truck. Come back and say the true thing: "I'm so tired I'm scared of myself." That sentence will save your marriage more than once.

If you're divorced, you may be taking all three exits at once in the first two years, and I understand why. Anger at her. The itch, because you're alone and you weren't. The drift, because caring is what got you here. Kenny said the first year after his divorce he was "a mad, lonely ghost." What brought him back was one older man at church who called him every Tuesday night and asked him the same three questions and didn't let him hang up until he'd answered. You'll meet that method later. It works.

If you're older, watch the drift and watch the anger. Those two like to travel together in a man past sixty, and they usually arrive by way of a television. The remedy is the same as it was at thirty. Get up. Make the coffee. Sit with her. Ask a question and wait for the answer.

## The Man who took none of them

Jesus got angry. He turned over tables in the temple and He looked at hard-hearted men with something the Gospels call anger, and He never once sinned in it. He knew what it was for and He aimed it and He put it down.

Jesus was tempted, in every way we are, and Hebrews says it flatly: without sin. Every itch a man has ever felt, He felt the pull of, alone, in a desert, for forty days, and He did not go down.

Jesus never drifted. Not once. When the time came to go to Jerusalem, He set His face and went. When His men fell asleep, He stayed awake. When everything in Him wanted another way, He said "not as I will," and He stayed.

He took none of the exits. And He did it so that when you take one, there'd be a Man who could find you in the ditch and bring you back, without shame, and show you the way He walked instead.

You've got a home exit. Now you know its name. Next chapter, we go to the Father.

**Do this week.** For anger: the next time it rises, take your ninety seconds and then say what's under it. For the itch: tell one man the whole truth and set up the filter with him holding the password. For the drift: one evening this week, screens off, your attention in the room, one real question asked and waited on.

**Tell a brother.** Tell him your exit and the last time you took it. Ask him his. Neither of you gets to say "I'm fine."

**Carry this.** A lot of male anger is grief with its fists up. Under every exit is a boy who's trying not to feel the chair.
