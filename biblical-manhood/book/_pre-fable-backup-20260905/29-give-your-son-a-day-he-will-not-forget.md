# 29. Give your son a day he will not forget

He can still tell you the cake.

Rented pavilion. Folding tables. His name in icing. Phones up for the candles, then down again. Relatives who love him and also have to get back. Somebody handed him a box. Somebody said grow up so fast. He smiled. You drove home with leftover sheet cake on the floorboard and called it a good day. It was a party. It was not a crossing.

I have thrown those. I have spent money. I have been the man with the lighter. I have not marked a day, in front of other men, where a boy could tell you afterward what changed.

This page is the day. Last page was the hill. A hill is ground. A name is a claim. Neither one is a date he can point to when he is thirty and somebody asks when he knew. Give your son a day he will not forget. Not a program. Not a catalog of gear. A day. Witnesses. Words. Cost. A before and an after.

I have waited for him to feel ready. I have waited for a weekend that looked official. I have waited until the house was less loud. Passivity loves a later date. Later is how a boy becomes a man with no memory of being called one.

In the future, when your children ask you, "What do these stones mean?" you tell them. That's Joshua 4:6-7.

Israel did not hope the kids would pick it up from the mood. They stacked stones so a question would have an answer. The day had a mark. The mark had a story. The story was God, not dad's mood. You are not Joshua at the Jordan. You are a man who can still give a son a day he can ask about later, and a true sentence waiting when he does.

We already ceremony what we think matters. A diploma. A wedding. A grave. We photograph those. We do not photograph a boy becoming a man, and then we wonder why he borrowed a lesser rite from a team, a frat, a feed, a group that will hurt him for belonging. Vacuum gets filled. If you will not mark him, someone else will, and they will not love him.

A day like this costs. Time. Other men in the room who will say your name and his, out loud, and not flinch. A sentence you cannot walk back on Monday. A small thing he can keep that is not a toy, if a symbol helps you mean it. The point is not the object. The point is that life looked like this, and now it looks like that, and he did not have to guess.

I have tried to do it cheap. A talk in the truck that I later remembered as a ceremony. It was a talk in the truck. Kind, even. Forgettable, usually. A boy can love you and still have no day. Love is not the same as a memorial.

You do not need a borrowed script from another house. You need a date, two or three men who actually know him, a blessing that tells the truth, and a life afterward that does not make you a liar. If you cannot get the men, do not cancel the day. Get one. Get your own mouth. Do not wait for a crowd to become a father.

Say what a man is. The four marks belong in his ears from you, not only from a page. Reject passivity. Accept responsibility. Lead courageously. Expect God's reward. Then bless him as yours, and as God's, in front of people who will remind him when you are tired. Keep it short. Speeches are how we hide. A day can be a meal and a walk and a sentence that lands. It can be a trip that costs you a Friday. It cannot be a mood you meant to get to.

If he is four, you still pick a later date and you start living toward it. The stones are not only for the hour they go in the ground. They are for the years that make the hour true. If he is fourteen, do not wait for eighteen because eighteen felt official in your head. If he is twenty-eight, you are not too late. Late is not never. A grown son can still be summoned. He may roll his eyes. Mark him anyway.

If you're young, you may never have had a day. I will not mock that. Delayed manhood wants the first real mark to wait for a son of your own. You can receive one. Go get it from a man who will actually say it. You can also mark a younger man this year without a child in the house. A 22-year-old can put a date on a page for a little brother, a roommate who is drifting, a boy he already influences. You do not graduate into being allowed to bless someone.

If you're single, this is not a dads-only page you watch from the hall. Marriage is good. A son is a gift. Neither one is the start of marking a life. A nephew. The kid who stays after because nobody is coming. One costly public sentence, and a day if you can give him one. Do not save your first real rite for a boy who does not exist yet.

If you're married and you have a son, this is your page. She already knows if you mark what matters or only fund the party. Honor what she has carried. Do not make her plan the day and then show up as talent. You set the date. You ask the men. You open your mouth. If you have daughters and no son, stay. This page is not hers, and there are boys in reach who will take a day if a man will give one.

If you got hurt on the way here, I will go slow. No day is a wound. A cruel day is a wound. A hazing you were told was making you a man is a wound. I am not going to joke that, and I am not going to tell you it made you stronger so we can move on. You can still give a different day than the one that raised you. You do not have to become theatrical because you were raised with none. If the house broke, or he will not take the call, I will not hand you a weekend that wins him by Friday. You can still tell the truth to God about the day you never got, and you can still mark a younger man who is actually in the room.

If you're older, a grandson will take a day. A 34-year-old still has ears. Empty nest is not a refund on your mouth. If you never had a son, you are not the mascot and you are not off the page. There is a younger man in reach. The Father is not embarrassed by a late memorial. Neither should you be.

If the job is going well, you already know how to mark a close. A dinner. A toast. A plaque. Then you treat your boy's crossing like something that will happen if the calendar is kind. The office will ceremony a sale. It will not stack stones for your son. Put the date on your work calendar like it is a close. Protect it.

Name the day you have been substituting. The party. The talk in the truck. The season finale. Nothing. Then put a real date on a page this week. If the boy is small, the date can be years out. Write it anyway, and tell one man. If he is old enough now, pick a month. Invite the men. Write the sentence you will say. One day. Not a rebuilt childhood.

A 22-year-old can do that. A 62-year-old can do that. You are not installing a whole manhood by Sunday. You are giving him a day he can ask about later, and an answer that is true.

Tell one man the date, and the sentence. If he only hears that you love your people, he will nod and you will go back to leftover cake. If he hears the actual day, he can ask you whether it happened. Let him ask.

The pavilion will still rent. Cake is easy to order. Stack a day he can point to.

Name it. What have you been calling a day? The party. The talk. Nothing.

Do this week. Put a date on a page. Tell one man. Write the sentence you will say.
