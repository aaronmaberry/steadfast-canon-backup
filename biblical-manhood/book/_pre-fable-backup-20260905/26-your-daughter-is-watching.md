# 26. Your daughter is watching

The visor is down.

After practice. She's in the passenger seat. Hair still wet, a clip already losing. A gym bag on the floorboard. A water bottle that rolls when he turns. She flips the visor for the little mirror and that cheap yellow light comes on, the one that makes everyone look tired. In the rectangle she can see his face. He is looking at the road. Or at a phone on the console. Or at nothing in particular. He thinks this is a ride home. She is taking notes.

I have been that man. I thought the miles were the gift. She was reading the miles.

This page is a father with a daughter. Last page was the bed. This is not the bed. The pages before that were the woman in your house. This is the girl in the other seat. Some of you do not have a daughter. Stay. You are not a guest. You are also not the whole page.

Your daughter is watching.

Moses told Israel to impress the commands on their children, and to talk about them when they sit at home and when they walk along the road, when they lie down and when they get up. That's Deuteronomy 6:7.

Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up.

Sit at home. Walk along the road. Lie down. Get up. That is a whole day. He is not handing you a later classroom. The road is already the class. She is already in the seat. You are already teaching, even when you are not talking. Silence counts. So does a phone on the console. So does how you talk to her mother in the kitchen. The visor is not only a mirror for her hair. It is a window.

They have to be on you first. The verse before this one puts the commands on your heart. If they are not on you, she gets a speech. Kids can smell a speech. They cannot always name it. They still file it.

I have given her a ride and called it fathering. I have paid the fees and missed the visor. I have asked how practice was in the tone that wants fine, then been grateful when she said it. I have gotten interested when a boy showed up, which taught her that a man pays attention when there is a threat, not because she is a person. That is a cheap way to wake up. She already needed me on a Thursday with wet hair and a rolling bottle.

Passivity loves a quiet passenger. A girl who does not interrupt cannot ask you to be a man. Being in the seat as a father is the job, not being a chauffeur. You can drive her all over town and still leave her to raise her idea of a man from a feed. Leading is not a lecture about boys. It is going first toward her. A question. An hour. A table that is not a speech. She may not clap, and a boy may still come, and a man who needs the clap will go back to being a ride.

She is learning what a man is. She is learning it from you.

Not from the talk you mean to have when she is older. From whether you look up. From whether your word holds. From whether you are kind when nobody is grading you. She is also watching how you talk to her mother. I am not turning this into her page. I am saying the girl sees it. Do not vent to her like she is the friend you should have been to her mother. If you are a visitor at your own table, she will go looking for a feeling that reminds her of home, and home will have been a man who was almost there.

She gets more about being a woman from a man who is actually there than from a lecture about women. She plays off you. Kind gets stored. A wall gets stored. A man who only comes alive for a game gets stored as a performance. You do not have to say a word for any of that to grow.

If you are not in the seat, she will still learn a man. She will learn him from whoever fills the gap. A boy with a phone. A coach. A feed that talks to her more than you do. You do not get to be shocked later that she believed the first person who looked at her like she mattered. You had first look. A lot of us spent it on the console.

This is not a purity talk. I am not going to make her body the subject of a men's chapter, and I am not going to hand you a set of rules for her and call that fathering. She is a person with a soul in the other seat. Not a project you protect so she stays valuable. Not a decoration you fund. She is your daughter. Treat her like one.

She needs a better sentence about being a woman than the one the market will sell her. You and her mother, if you have one, owe her that. Not a seminar. A life she can see. Cheer what is true about her on an ordinary night. Do not wait for a stage. The visor is enough.

Take her out. Not a lecture in a booth. A burger. A walk. Ask something you do not already know and stay for the answer. Do it before a boy does it, and keep doing it after. If you only show up when there is a boy, you taught her that male attention is a crisis. Be the ordinary man who already liked her.

Some of you will only father in public. Games. A recital. A post. She saw you. Then you took a call at the fence. The bleacher was empty when she looked back. Presence that needs an audience is still a kind of absence.

Some of you are in the car and still not in it. Same as a man in a chair who is not in the chair. She can feel that. You do not need a horror story for her to be hungry.

If you're young, they will talk as if this page is later. Nineteen. Twenty-two. A girlfriend can start to look like a girl you manage, or a test drive at being a dad. She is not. Delayed manhood wants girls easy so he never has to pay attention. A little sister already reads you. The girls at church already read you. Some of you already have a child in a seat. You are not waiting for official. A 22-year-old can look up once this week and keep looking. If you will not be a man a girl can trust now, a baby will not install it.

If you're single, this is not a brochure for a daughter you do not have, and it is not a lecture you sit out. Marriage is good. You may have a girl in your house later. You may not. Either way, you are already a man some girl is reading. A niece. A brother's daughter. The teenager who sits two rows down and has no one at her games. Name how you have been in the room with the girls who are actually in reach. Not a hunt. Not a project. Treat her as a person and go home. If you are walking toward a woman, she is watching what a man is too. Do not make her the first person who ever saw you try.

If you're married and she is in the house, this is your page. She already knows if you are a father or a ride. Take her out this week. Put the phone in the back. Ask a question you do not own. Stay. Look at her. The visor is already down.

If you got hurt on the way here, I will go slow. If she left and took the girl, this page is not a strategy for getting a marriage back dressed as finally being a dad. Be a father in the hours you actually have. A drop-off is not a punchline. If the week is two days and a car, that car is the house. Look up. If the girl will not speak, do not use a burger as a battering ram. Keep being a man she can find if she ever looks. If you never had a daughter, you are not a guest. You still do not get to ignore the girls God put in reach and call it not your chapter. If you were the man who did not look up, name it. She already knows. So do you.

If you're older, she may be grown. A 62-year-old can still miss the visor. An adult daughter still watches whether dad will look up, whether he will ask a real question, whether he talks about her mother as a person. Empty nest is not a pass. Granddaughters get in the same seat. The younger men need to see a man who did not retire from being a father. A 62-year-old who never had a daughter is not a mascot. He still treats the girls in reach as people. He still tells a younger dad the truth about the visor.

If the job is going well, you already know how to give a person your face. You would be embarrassed to take a client meeting and watch a phone the whole time. You do it in the car with her. Honor the work. Do not call the skip provision. A high performer can fund the sport and miss the person. He can make the game and glow in the dark of the gym, answering a thing that could have waited. She saw the glow. Name it. Then be in the seat once this week on a ride nobody will clap for.

Name the way she has been watching you. One. The visor. The drop-off. The sister. The niece. The glow. Then be in the seat once this week. Take her, or the girl in reach, and stay. Not a speech about being present. Not a map of her whole life by Friday. One hour with your face.

A 22-year-old can do that. A 62-year-old can do that. You are not finishing a childhood by Sunday. You are stopping the chauffeur long enough to be a man she can actually see.

Tell one man what she has been watching, and that you looked up. If he only hears that you love your kids, he will nod and you will put the phone back on the console. If he hears the visor, he can ask you next week whether you were in the seat. Let him ask.

She is already in the other seat. The road is already the class. Look up.

Name it. How has she been watching you, the daughter you have or the girl in reach?

Do this week. Be in the seat once. Take her, or the girl in reach, and stay. Phone in the back. A question you do not own.
