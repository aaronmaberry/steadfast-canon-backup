# 28. Give your son a hill

The mound is still on the last lot.

Friday. New street. The builder left a pile of caliche and a permit box on a stick. Three boys have been charging it since the bus. Somebody's sister on a plastic trike, official. Sticks. A broken cone. His boy is on top with his heels dug in, yelling like the county line is at his sneakers.

The man is still in the badge. He pulls in. He thinks: good. He's outside.

That is not a hill. That is dirt.

I have been that man. I have been glad a boy was using his body, and I have called the afternoon a life. I have gone inside to hang a bike hook because the pile looked like play and I had a garage to finish. That is how I missed the job.

This page is the hill. Last page was the name. A name tells him whose he is. A hill tells him where to stand. You can tell him he is yours and still leave him with nowhere to put the fight. He will put it somewhere. Boys do not lack fight. They lack ground.

Give your son a hill.

For you know that we dealt with each of you as a father deals with his own children, encouraging, comforting and urging you to live lives worthy of God, who calls you into his kingdom and glory. That's 1 Thessalonians 2:11-12.

:::margin
**1 Thessalonians 2:11-12 NIV**
For you know that we dealt with each of you as a father deals with his own children, encouraging, comforting and urging you to live lives worthy of God, who calls you into his kingdom and glory.
:::

Paul is talking about a church. He still reached for a father to say it. A father is not only a ride and a fee. He puts a worthy life in a boy's hands and does not apologize for wanting it. Kingdom and glory. That is a hill. Not a hobby with a louder yell. A place to stand when the week is loud and nobody is clapping.

God is the one who calls him into the kingdom. Your job is smaller and heavier. Urge him toward that life, and do not drop it when he would rather stay on the pile. Comfort is not the same as leaving him on the dirt.

I have given him dirt and called it childhood. I have given him a sport and called it a spine. I have given him "don't embarrass us" and called it a code. A fence is not a hill. A fence tells him where not to go. A hill tells him what is worth staying for when leaving would be easier.

Passivity loves a boy who is fine. Fine does not ask you to be a man. Fine plays until someone else assigns him a fight: a ranking, a slight, a brand, a feed that will keep him angry for free. If you do not give him a hill, he will still fight. He will just fight for a pile that cannot keep him.

That is not a poster for your office. It is what you owe a boy about what a man is. He should hear it from your mouth, not only from a wall. Then he should see you on it. Leaving him on the pile while you hang a hook is passivity with a garage door. The prize is not a boy who wins the dirt. It is a man still standing when the dirt is a house.

Sports are good. A team can teach a boy to hurt and stay. It cannot tell him what the pain is for. May ends. The hill cannot end in May.

Some of you handed him your unfinished thing. The shop. The company. The degree you didn't finish. He can feel when he is a second chance in cleats. That is not a gift. That is a job posting.

"Be good" is a thin hill. It will not hold him at sixteen when a lesser one is on offer and the useful kid is tired.

The way you live in the house cannot stay "because I said so" forever. He will meet a day you are wrong, or gone, or quiet. If the only authority behind it is you, it dies when you do. If it is God's, it still stands when he is thirty and you are not in the driveway. Tell him where it comes from. Not a seminar. A sentence, then a week that matches.

If you name twelve things this house stands for, you named none. Pick a few you will actually keep when it would be funnier not to. A code you break for a laugh is not a code. It is a mood.

If you hand him only himself to live for, he will have plenty of energy and nothing that can judge it. He will win a ranking and call it a life. He will nurse a slight he will not drop. That is not a cause. That is a boy with better equipment.

You cannot hand a boy ground you will not occupy. I have talked a worthy life in the truck and then taken the cheap one in the house. He heard both. Leading is standing on it first, where he can see you, on a Friday that was not designed for a speech. You do not need a speech. You need a sentence, and a Friday that does not make you a liar.

Watching from the driveway is not urging. Paying the fees is not a hill. The mound years are short. The boy on the pile will not stay nine. You can start late. Late is not never. Late is also not a speech to win him by Friday.

If you're young, you already fight. The question is what for. Delayed manhood wants the first real hill to wait for a son. You already pick fights. A group chat. A little brother who copies whatever you treat as worth it. A weekend you will defend and a worthy life you will not mention. A 22-year-old can name his hill this week without a child in the house. He can also get off the cheap one he has been charging. You do not wait for a son to have a hill. You take one, then you can hand one.

If you're single, do not wait for a car seat to have ground. Marriage is good. A son is a gift. You already stand for something in front of people who are watching. A nephew who rides home with you. A boy on your team who stays because the gym is quieter than his house. Urge a worthy life while you are in the room. Do not save the first costly stand for a son who does not exist yet.

If you're married and you have a son, this is your page. She already knows if the house has a hill or only a calendar. She may have been handing him a way to live while you funded the pile. Honor that. Do not leave her as the only person in the house who will say what you are for. Walk in. Name what you are for, then occupy it on an ordinary night. If you have daughters and no son, this is still not her chapter. There are boys in reach who will take real ground if a man will give some.

If you got hurt on the way here, I will go slow. A missing hill is a wound. So is a fence you were told was fathering. So is dad's pride with a name on the truck, handed to you as a life. I will not dress that up. You can still hand different ground than the ground that raised you. You do not have to become a tyrant because you were raised with none. If the house broke, you still have the hours you have. Use them to urge a worthy life, not to win an argument about the past. If he will not take the call, keep the hill anyway. He may look later. If you were the man who only funded the pile, stop dressing it up as childhood. Confess to a brother. Start standing.

If you're older, you can think the hill was for when he was nine. He is 28 and still building a life on whatever you treated as worth it, or on the pile you left him. A plate that is no longer a high chair is still a table. Say what you are for. A grandson will copy the hill he can see, including the one you never named. Empty nest is not a refund. If you never had a son, you are not the mascot of this page and you are not off it. There is a younger man in reach. Late ground is still ground.

If the job is going well, the lobby already told you what the company is for. You can say it in your sleep. Then you treat the boy like a status check that wants fine. You already know how to hand people a cause at work. Hand him one that is not the brand. Sit on the tailgate after the badge comes off. Say what you are for. Stay there long enough that it is not a slogan.

Name the hill you have been handing him. Dirt. The sport. The fence. The unfinished thing. Himself. Nothing. Then name the real one once this week, out loud, not as a rule you bark from the garage. A life worthy of God. If there is no son in the house, the younger man in reach. Then occupy it once where he can see you. One Friday. Not a rebuilt childhood.

A 22-year-old can do that. A 62-year-old can do that. You are not installing a whole life by Friday. You are handing him ground and standing on it once.

Tell one man the hill you have been handing him, and the one you named. If he only hears that you want the boy to turn out, he will nod and you will go back inside to the hook. If he hears the actual hill, he can ask you next week whether you stood on it. Let him ask.

The mound will still be there tomorrow. Dirt is easy to charge. Name the hill. Then occupy it.

Name it. What hill have you been handing him? The son, the younger man, the one you still stand on.

Do this week. Name the real one once, out loud, and stand on it where he can see you.
