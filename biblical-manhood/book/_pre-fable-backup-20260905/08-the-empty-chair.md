# 8. The empty chair

There is an empty chair at the table.

The table still has to eat. Someone still passes the dish. Someone still talks about the day, the bill, the game. The chair does not argue. It just sits there. Sometimes a man is even sitting in it. That does not always mean the chair is full.

I have sat at tables like that. I have also been the man in the chair who was not in the chair. A body at dinner is not the same as a father. I used to pretend those were the same thing. Pretending is easier than hunger. The hunger stays. It just learns to chew without looking at that side of the table.

This page is the hole. The father hole. It is real. Name the empty chair. I will not pretend we can heal it all here. We can tell the truth about what you inherited.

Some chairs are empty because he never came. Some because he left. Some because he died and the plate still gets set by habit, then by stubbornness, then because taking it away felt like losing him twice. Some because he came home already gone. Tired. Walled off. Funny in a way that kept you from getting close. You could hear the garage. You could hear the game in the other room. You could not get to him. Present in the house and missing in the room. Absence is not only a missing person. Absence is a missing father.

You do not have to have a horror story for the chair to be empty. Some of you had a decent man who still did not know how to be a dad. He paid. He stayed. He did not know what to do with a boy's heart, so he handed you a tool or a shrug and hoped that counted. Some of you had a hard man and you learned to call hardness strength. Some of you had both, in seasons. The hole has levels. Yours is yours. I will not sand it down so it matches mine. I also will not let you call a hole a personality so you never have to look at it.

Paul told fathers not to exasperate their children, and instead to bring them up in the training and instruction of the Lord. That's Ephesians 6:4.

:::margin
**Ephesians 6:4 NIV**
Fathers, do not exasperate your children; instead, bring them up in the training and instruction of the Lord.
:::

That is what belonged in the chair. Not a perfect man. A father. A man who would not grind a boy down, and would not leave him to raise himself. Training. Instruction. The Lord in the room, not just a paycheck on the table. A voice you could actually get to. A why, not only a no. A week that matched his mouth. A heart, not only a standard. Memories you could spend later when the week went bad. If that was not your house, you are not inventing a wound. You are naming what God said a father is for.

Exasperate is not only yelling. It is also the slow simmer of a man who is there and never comes near. Neglect can boil a boy as surely as a hard hand. So can a joke that always lands on you. So can silence at the one moment you needed a sentence. You brought him the thing and he did not look up. You needed a word after you failed, and the word did not come. The verse is not a club to hate your dad with. It is a picture of what the chair was supposed to hold.

I am not making your dad into a devil. He is a man. Some of what he did not give, he never got. That is true, and it does not fill the chair. You can explain a hole all night. Explaining does not occupy it. You can honor him and still say something was missing. If you only honor, you will lie to keep the peace. If you only prosecute, you will turn a life into a case. This page is a table. Look at the chair.

The hole does not clock out when you turn eighteen. It does not clock out when you get a job, or a wife, or a house with enough chairs. A lot of us tried that. We bought a fuller table and still ate like sons. Success does not fill a chair. A title does not. A clean report card on your own life does not. If it did, the most impressive men in the room would be the most at rest. Some of the most impressive men I know still eat like they are waiting for someone to sit down.

Do not turn this into destiny. The chair is real. It is not the rest of your life. You can name it without giving it the rest of your years. Refusing to name it is how it keeps running you from behind. Hurrying a man past the chair is cruel. Using the chair as a pass for whatever you did this week is a hiding place. We will not do either.

If you're nineteen, or twenty-two, the chair may still feel like it could walk in. You keep a version of him in your head that will finally show up and make the years make sense. I will not mock that. I will tell you what waiting costs. Waiting for a ghost to sit down is how a young man stays a son in every room he enters. Name what was actually there. Name what was not. You can do that now. You do not have to wait for a funeral, or a perfect setting, or a feeling that makes it easy.

If you're single, this is already your life. You do not need a wife to have a father hole. Do not make a future marriage the place you planned to finally feel fathered. That is too much to put on a woman. She cannot sit in that chair. Name it now, with a brother, while the table is still yours.

If you're married, you can father with a hole. You already are. She can feel it even when you cannot hear it. The danger is dumping the empty chair into her lap and calling that honesty. The other danger is pretending you have no chair so you never have to become one that is full. Your son is watching whether a man can tell the truth without making the whole house about his dad. Your daughter is watching too. She is learning what a chair is for by how you sit in yours.

If you got hurt on the way here, I am not going to hurry you. Some of you have a chair that was cruel, not just empty. Some of you have a chair that was loving and then gone, which is its own kind of ache. Kind is not pretending. You can say he was not there and still be a man. You can say I wanted him and not be a boy. Wanting a father is not a defect. God built sons to have one.

If you're older, the chair can still be empty. A 62-year-old can still flinch at a certain tone. He can still hear a truck in the street and feel fourteen. He can also look at his own table and realize he has become the empty chair he ate across from. That is a hard look. It is also a mercy, if he will take it. You do not age out of this naming. You also do not age out of being able to sit down, for real, in a chair that is yours now.

If the job is going well, hear me without me turning it into a speech at your office. A full calendar can be how a man never looks at the chair. People clap for the hours. The hours will not father you. They will not father your kids either, if all they get is a tired man who made it home for the last ten minutes. You can be excellent at work and still be an empty chair at 6:30. The people at the table already know. They stopped bringing you the thing.

I have called my hunger a mood. I have called it being private. I have called it being fine. Fine is a word a man uses when he does not want to say the chair is empty. It keeps the table moving. It also keeps the hole unnamed, which means it keeps running.

Name the chair this week. Not ten chairs. The one that is yours. Then tell one man the true sentence about your father. Not a speech. A sentence. "He was there and I still couldn't reach him." "He left." "He died and I keep eating like he might walk in." "I never met him." "He paid for everything and I cannot remember his voice on a hard day." If you cannot say a sentence, you are still protecting the hole. Protection is not honor. Honor can tell the truth.

Tell him without making him fix it. He cannot sit in that chair for you. He can hear you. That is enough for this page. If he tries to rush you to a cure, ask him to sit still. This chapter is the naming.

The table still has to eat. That was always true. You can eat and still tell the truth about the chair. A man who will not name the empty chair will spend years being hungry and acting surprised. He should not be surprised. It is a chair.

Name it. What is true about the chair at your table?

Do this week. One true sentence about your father. No speech.
