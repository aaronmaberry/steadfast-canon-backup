# 23. Lay it down

Saturday. A second-floor walkup. They were moving a guy from church, and the sofa would not make the turn unless somebody took the downstairs end and ate the weight.

He claimed it before anyone else could. Knees. Doorframe. A sound he will mention later. At the truck he shook his arms out and somebody said that's why we called you. He liked that more than he will admit.

I have been that man. I have taken the heavy end and thought I knew what kind of man I was. Then I went home and kept the life.

A man who lays down his life. That is this page. Last page was knowing her. You can study a woman and still belong to yourself. This one is what you do with you.

I used to like the sentence more than the dying. "I would die for you." I have said it. After a close call. After a baby came. In a fight I was losing and wanted to look like the bigger man. I meant it, as far as a sentence can mean a thing. Then the week asked for something smaller than a bullet, and I kept it.

Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her. That's Ephesians 5:25.

Gave himself up. Not the heavy end of a sofa.

Christ is not collecting. Splitting it so nobody has to go all the way is not this verse either. And he is not leading so the good things run uphill toward him. He gave himself up for her. That is the shape. If what you call leading still leaves you intact, it is not His kind.

Keeping your life and calling it fine is a nicer name for staying intact. Her good is not a hobby you visit. Going first is not the same as dying. You can step into a room first and still keep the credit. The week will not always clap, and a man who needs the clap will pick his life back up before the truck is even loaded.

I have put the better piece on a plate and waited to be thanked. I had not laid anything down. I had put on a show. I have stayed late and called it dying for the house when I was feeding a name. I have grabbed a check so fast nobody else could move, which is a way of staying in charge of a table. I have taken the downstairs end for a friend and then gone home and won a small stupid argument because I did not want to be the man who lost.

I have also kept a tab. Every heavy end logged. Then I brought the tab out when I wanted something. "After all I do." That is not this verse. That is a man who wants to be owed. The Cross does not send a bill.

The speech is easy. A bullet is rare. The assignment is usually your actual life: the hour you already claimed, the last word, the plan that does not get checked, the need to be right, the evening that belongs to you because you paid for the house. That is the life. Lay that down.

You do not become a ghost. Christ did not vanish. He gave himself up, and the church lived. The people near you should live. If they exist to keep you comfortable, you have it backwards. If nobody is allowed to cost you your preferences, you are not loving like this. You are protecting a life.

Some of you will only die in public. A move, a roof, a crisis with a story in it. You are brave when there is an audience, even an audience of two men and a stairwell. Then a quiet room asks you to yield, and you suddenly have a spine made of "I already did my part." You did a part. You kept the rest.

Some of you will not die at all. You will cut everything down the middle so neither of you has to go all the way. That can sound fair. It is often two people agreeing not to love like this. Fair is not the Cross.

The first Adam kept himself. The last Adam did not. You already met those two. This is what the second way looks like when a man stops belonging to himself.

You cannot lay down a life you will not admit you have been keeping. Name the piece. If you cannot name it, it is still running you.

If you're young, you want a war. You want a death that would make a movie. You get a roommate's mess, a shift nobody clapped for, a church that needs a man to stay after. Nineteen. Twenty-two. Delayed manhood loves a heroic later because a present death would cost him Tuesdays. A 22-year-old can lay down an hour this week without a title and without a wife to receive it. The Cross does not wait for official.

If you're single, this is not a guest lecture on someone else's marriage. You already have a life to lay down. A future wife is not the first person you die for. Christ already is. Then the actual people in reach. A brother. A parent. A neighbor. The church you visit and never pick up. Marriage is good. It is not the start of this shape. A man who will only give himself up after a ring is already practicing keeping it. Name what you kept this week. Lay that down.

If you're married, this is your page. Give yourself up. She already knows if you kept the life. She can feel a man who took the heavy end for a friend and then came home as a collector. Do not make her clap. Do not make her the enemy. Do not use the children as the only people you would die for while you keep yourself from her. Tell her the piece. Then lay it down when the house is loud and nobody is watching a stairwell.

If you got hurt on the way here, I will go slow. If she left, I am not going to joke, and I am not going to hand you a strategy for getting her back dressed as a cross. Look at the week. If you were the one who kept yourself, name it without using her exit as cover. If you laid things down and the marriage still died, I will not steal that from you. You still have a life that is not only yours. Brothers. The people in reach. Do not use the wreck as a reason to keep the rest. Laying it down is not staying under sin because someone used the word love. Christ gave himself up. He did not call evil good. If she died, I will not cheer you into a later. You still have a next move that is not a refund. The life was never a prize you get back.

If you're older, a long marriage can start to sound like a completed death, and an empty nest can start to sound like a refund. "I did my years. Finally us. Finally me." You still have a life to lay down. A 62-year-old can still lose the small argument that was about pride. He can still go second at a table. The younger men need to see a man who is not done. Retirement is not a cross you already finished. A widower still has people in reach. A 62-year-old who never married is not a mascot. He still dies for the people God actually put in front of him.

If the job is going well, you will try to buy the dying. A kitchen. A trip. Tuition. Provision is good. It is not a substitute body. You cannot send the money in your place and keep the self that made it. The title can become the one thing you will not lay down. Honor the paycheck. Do not send it to the Cross in your place. If the badge came off on Saturday, would you still give yourself up on a quiet night? Name the piece. Then lay it down on a day the office does not need you.

You will pick it back up. I do. Start over. The Spirit is not shocked that a man tried to keep himself. He is not asking you for a clean record by Friday. He is asking you for the life.

Name the piece of your life you would not lay down this week. One. Then lay that one thing down once, without a speech, without waiting to be thanked. The hour. The last word. The plan. The being-owed. A person already in reach. Not a personality transplant. Once.

A 22-year-old can do that. A 62-year-old can do that. You are not finishing a cross by Monday. You are stopping the speech long enough to die a small true death.

Tell one man the thing you kept, and that you laid it down. If he only hears that you would die for your people, he will nod and you will keep the life. If he hears the actual piece, he can ask you next week whether you picked it back up to be owed. Let him ask.

Christ gave himself up. That is the love. The downstairs end was never the whole of it.

Name it. What piece of your life would you not lay down this week?

Do this week. Lay that one thing down once, without a speech, without waiting to be thanked.
