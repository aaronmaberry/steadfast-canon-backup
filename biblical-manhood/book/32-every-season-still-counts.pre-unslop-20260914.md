# Every Season Still Counts

A cotton field in February looks dead. Bare dirt, stubble, a fence with nothing behind it.

Everything is happening. The ground is resting, the stubble is going back into the soil, and the winter rain is working down to where the roots will need it in July. February isn't the off-season. It's a season with its own job, and a farmer who planted in February because he couldn't stand the waiting would lose the year.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 3:1 (NIV)
"There is a time for everything, and a season for every activity under the heavens:"
:::

A season for every activity. I promised early on not to sort you by age on every page, and I've kept it. This once we do it on purpose, because the culture ranks the seasons, and the ranking is a lie that costs men decades.

## The lie of the peak

Here's the ranking most men carry. Twenties for freedom, thirties for building, forties the peak, fifties for coasting, and past sixty a long slow exit with golf in it. Anything that doesn't fit, a marriage that comes apart, an illness, a wife dying, is a detour on the way back to the arc.

Scripture doesn't know this arc. It knows a man who serves God's purpose in his own generation, which is every season he lives through. Caleb asked for hill country at eighty-five.

:::margin
Joshua 14:12 (NIV)
"Now give me this hill country that the LORD promised me that day."
:::

The hardest assignment on the map, asked for by an old man whose season still counted. So let me walk the seasons: the temptation, the assignment, and which man from these pages is standing in each.

## The young single man

This is the season of strength and open hours, and the most wasted. Micah spent six years at the back table and wasted the first three waiting, which is the temptation: to treat this season as the lobby. The other is the itch, because open hours and no one watching is more exposure to that exit than a man ever has again.

The assignment is to build, and you already have the list. Solomon, looking back from old age, gave the young man one instruction.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 12:1 (NIV)
"Remember your Creator in the days of your youth, before the days of trouble come and the years approach when you will say, 'I find no pleasure in them'"
:::

The men who find the Father at twenty-five are spared thirty years of scanning the bleachers, and the man you build now is the man every later season gets.

## The first year, and the first baby

The season that decides a marriage begins the day after the honeymoon. The wedding was the marker; the Tuesdays are the marriage, and the first year of them sets the floor for the next forty. The assignment is knowing her, laying it down, finding out what she needs, and keeping the twenty minutes with your brother, because a man who drops his brothers hands his wife a weight she can't carry.

A baby in the first year or two breaks more young marriages than any other season. Tomás's daughter was four months old when you met him. A third person who needs everything has moved in, and you're both a little afraid this is what it's going to be now. It won't be, but it's this year, and the assignment is to protect the marriage inside the chaos. Ten minutes at the kitchen counter after the baby's down is the whole liturgy. That isn't waiting for a better season. It is the season. Watch for the postpartum darkness that has a name. Tomás, a year in: "I found out I could be a man when I was too tired to be anything, and she found out she could count on me."

## The middle years

Thirty-five to fifty-five. The culture calls this the peak, and it's where most men lose the house while building the career. Anthony is in it, standing in a doorway between a garage and a kitchen. The temptations are the best disguised: disappearing into provision, the drift that looks like being tired, the itch coming back with a middle-aged face when the marriage has gone quiet.

The assignment is presence: the floor held, the marriage tended because the woman you married is gone and the one who's here needs knowing, brothers because men this age have the fewest friends, hills for your children, and a hard look at what you're working for, where the fourth mark gets tested hardest. This isn't your peak. It's where you find out whether you're a corner post or a line post, and your children are watching too.

## The season nobody plans

The man I'm thinking of didn't plan it. It's a season with a job, and it lasts longer than anyone tells you.

The first year or two is survival: grief in waves at the exchange and on Sunday nights, anger in every direction, the itch because you're alone and you weren't. The temptation is to make a turn, move or marry or quit, anything to make the gauges stop screaming. Don't. Drive straight and slow. The assignment is short: grieve out loud and own your part without attaching hers; children first, before any new woman; one brother on the phone every week; and stay in the house of God even when the house is awkward about you.

In years three to five the gauges settle. You've built a real house, children's drawings on the refrigerator, and become the man the other men on this road talk to. I won't promise you'll marry again, but this season counts. The Father who does not leave didn't leave in the courtroom, and a man who walks this road the second Man's way, covering his children, owning his part, refusing the exits, comes out a man other men will follow.

## The empty nest

The last one leaves, and two people who've spent twenty years talking about children look at each other and aren't sure what's left. The temptation is two strangers in a house, and the affair, because a man who feels invisible at home and visible somewhere else is in danger.

The assignment is to court her again. Walt did, after he sold off part of the business, and called it the best stretch of their marriage, "because for the first time since 1994 it was just us and I actually showed up for it." Then turn outward, because there are hills to give. If what you built was a co-parenting arrangement, God gives you this season to build the marriage.

## The retired man

You have Roy's rhythm already. Retirement in Scripture is a shift from carrying to assisting, not from working to sitting. The temptation is the drift with a fishing license and the anger that comes through the television. The assignment is to assist: the younger men, the widow's gutters, the wife who has waited forty years for you to be home. And to be Caleb, asking God for something hard and expecting Him to give it. "I kept the peace for thirty-one years," Roy said. "I've been making men for five. I know which one God's going to ask me about."

## The widower

Mr. Pruitt buried his wife of fifty-one years and planted forty pecan trees the next spring. That's the assignment in one sentence, and the year of grief before it was part of it. The temptation is to decide that life ended when hers did. Grieve as long as it takes, find one brother, because God said not alone and made no exception for men past seventy, and work whatever's left of the garden. And God made old men a promise.

:::margin
Isaiah 46:4 (NIV)
"Even to your old age and gray hairs I am he, I am he who will sustain you. I have made you, and I will carry you; I will sustain you and I will rescue you."
:::

I will carry you. It's the same word from Deuteronomy, a father carrying his son, said to the gray-haired. Somebody's grandson is going to eat those pecans.

## The man in weakness

Some of you are sick, or caring for someone who is; Mr. Alvarez has bathed his wife every morning for three years. If you've decided a man who can't work is done, remember Paul, who begged God to remove a thorn and was told no: my power is made perfect in weakness. The temptation is uselessness, and the assignment is to assist. Pray by name; a man in a chair with a list is a power in a church. A homebound man I know has prayed for every young man in his church by name for eleven years, and they know it. That's a work, and it counts.

## The last season

There's one more. Men don't like to talk about it; Scripture does, constantly. Number your days, the psalm says, and live like a man who'll give a completion report. The assignment is to finish well: say what needs saying, bless your children the way Jacob did from his bed, leave your plumb bob to someone with a story attached to it. Paul, from a cell, near the end:

:::margin
2 Timothy 4:7 (NIV)
"I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith."
:::

Finished, not won. Every man in this book will stand in this season, and the ones who've lived the four marks in every other one will find they already know how to do this one.

## The marks don't change

Passivity at twenty-five is a tailgate; at seventy-five it's a recliner. The weight a new father picks up is a two o'clock feeding; a widower's is a post-hole digger and forty bare-root trees. And God's reward is the same in every season, because it was never in the season.

The marks don't change; the garden does. A man who has learned to reject passivity, accept responsibility, lead courageously, and expect God's reward in one season already knows how in the next. There is no off-season. February counts. Whatever season you're in has an assignment with your name on it, and the Father who set the seasons is watching you work it.

## Before you turn the page

Close these pages without the ranking; it has cost men decades. May the Father who set the seasons give you the assignment of yours in one plain sentence with a verb in it, and may you find, at nineteen or seventy-nine, that the garden you're standing in has your name on it.
