# 5. Pick Up the Weight

There's a wall in your house you can't take out. It looks like the others, same paint, same nail holes where the school pictures hung. But the joists above it come to rest on that wall, and everything upstairs, the beds, the dressers, the kids asleep in them, is riding on it whether anybody thinks about it or not. A contractor finds it in a minute. He knocks, looks up, checks which way the floor runs, and says, this one's carrying the second floor.

You can open up the kitchen without it, but only if you put something else under the weight, because the weight doesn't go away when you'd like a bigger room. It lands somewhere. If nothing's under it, the floor above comes down slowly, a sag nobody notices until a bedroom door stops latching a year later.

Every house has weight that has to land somewhere. So does every family, and every job, and every life. Nobody asks whether the wall thinks it's fair. The weight is where the weight is.

The second mark of a man is that he accepts responsibility. A man looks at the weight in his life, the part that's his, and says, that lands on me. Not because he caused all of it. Not because the arrangement is fair. Because he's the wall, and if he steps back, everyone upstairs finds out.

## Not mine

The first words out of a man after the fall are the oldest sentence in the language of men. God asks what happened and Adam points: the woman You put here with me, she gave me some of the fruit. Two fingers in one breath, one at his wife and one at God, and none at himself. His son learned the grammar. When God comes asking after Abel, Cain hands back a question.

:::margin
Genesis 4:9 (NIV)
"Then the LORD said to Cain, 'Where is your brother Abel?' 'I don't know,' he replied. 'Am I my brother's keeper?'"
:::

Yes. That's the whole answer. It's the answer for every man who has ever said not my department, not my kid, she knew what she was getting into, nobody told me.

Notice what both men were protecting: a version of the story in which the weight belonged to someone else. That's all blame is. It's a story with the weight moved.

Good men get stuck here because they've confused responsibility with fault. Fault asks who caused it. Responsibility asks whose it is now. A man can be at no fault at all for the layoff, the diagnosis, his daughter's anxiety, the father who left him nothing but debts, and every one of those can still be his to carry. Fairness is a fine question for a courtroom. It's a useless one for a wall.

## Five things with your name on them

Here's what's yours. Not everything; the weight that isn't comes soon. But these five, without argument.

Your own life. Your body, your money, your word, your habits, the state of your soul. Nobody else is going to get you to the doctor or pay the card down or open your Bible. If you're grown, these left your mother's hands a long time ago, whether or not she's noticed.

Your house. If you're married, the temperature of that marriage is yours, not split down the middle. You answer for whether it warmed or drifted. If there are children, their forming is yours. The school and the church can help. The boy will still grow up sounding like whoever was in the house.

Your work. The job in front of you, done the way you'd do it if the owner were watching, because One is.

Your people. The brother who's struggling, the man at church whose wife just left, the kid down the street with no father in sight. Am I my brother's keeper? Yes. Not his savior. His keeper.

And the fifth weight, the heaviest and the first one men set down: what you've done wrong. Not what was done to you. What you did. The words at the dinner table, the years you were gone while you were home, the thing she doesn't know. That's yours. Setting it down doesn't make it lighter. It makes it heavier for everyone else in the house.

The weights shift with the season, but the name on them doesn't. A single man's load is mostly himself, and it's heavier than he thinks, because nobody's home to notice when he sets it down. A new husband learns that a second person's peace now rides on him. A man with a newborn carries a wife who hasn't slept; a man with a boy who just got his license carries his own tongue. A divorced man is still the floor of two houses, whatever the paperwork says. A man with grown children carries the fifth weight most of all, the things he got wrong when they were small, which he can still pick up with a phone call.

## Two words for weight

Paul says two things a few lines apart that sound like they cancel out.

:::margin
Galatians 6:2 (NIV)
"Carry each other's burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ."
:::

:::margin
Galatians 6:5 (NIV)
"for each one should carry their own load."
:::

Carry each other's. Carry your own. Both, because he's using two different words. The first means a crushing weight, more than one back was built for. The second is a soldier's pack, a ship's cargo, the load a man is issued and expected to carry himself. Jesus used that second word when He said His burden is light.

A man who accepts responsibility can tell the two apart. He shoulders his own load without a speech about it, and when a brother goes down under the crushing kind, he gets under it without being asked. Neither is optional, and neither is weakness.

## The weight that isn't yours

Some men would rather lose a finger than make two trips from the car, and they carry their lives the same way: a wife's happiness, the children's every choice, the church's health, their own salvation, all on one arm, because setting any of it down feels like failure.

Moses got there. A whole nation in the desert complaining about the menu, and he said to God what most men are too proud to say.

:::margin
Numbers 11:14 (NIV)
"I cannot carry all these people by myself; the burden is too heavy for me."
:::

God didn't rebuke him. He gave him seventy elders and said they'd share the burden so he wouldn't carry it alone. Read that as a man. There's weight God never assigned you, and picking it up isn't responsibility. It's pride in work boots. Your wife's joy is not your load. Your grown son's choices are not your load. Carry what's yours, and ask for the seventy on what isn't.

## Providing without disappearing

Most men know one verse about providing, and they've heard it preached as a paycheck.

:::margin
1 Timothy 5:8 (NIV)
"Anyone who does not provide for their relatives, and especially for their own household, has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever."
:::

Read what the sentence is about. Household. Not payroll. The word takes in everyone under the roof, and what a child needs from a father is not mostly money. A man can fund a house he's never in. He can pay for years of piano lessons and never once sit in a folding chair in a school cafeteria to hear his daughter play, and the empty chair undoes the lessons.

Feed your family; that's the floor. But it's only the floor. Presence is part of provision, and a man who's traded all of the second for more of the first hasn't provided. He's found a very expensive way to disappear.

What a household needs from the man carrying it is a floor that doesn't move. A teenager pushes because he's checking whether it holds. A wife brings the hard thing to a man she's sure won't buckle when she says it. You don't have to feel strong. You have to be there, and stay there, and not move when it gets loud. Upstairs, nobody thinks about the beam. They walk across the floor without testing it. That's what the beam is for.

## How to pick it up

Say, that's mine. Out loud, to the person it concerns. Not I can see how you'd feel that way. That's mine.

Stop at the period. That's mine, but is a different sentence, and everyone in the room can hear the difference. Adam's answer had a but in it before the word existed. Say the first part and close your mouth.

Own the outcome, not the effort. The roof doesn't grade on effort. Either the rain's coming in or it isn't. A man who says I tried to get home for dinner is describing his intentions. His kids are describing the chair.

Carry your load, then get under a burden. In that order. The man who's helping everyone else while his own pack sits in the driveway isn't generous. He's hiding, and the people who can see it are the ones at home.

And ask for the seventy. Name the weight God never assigned you and hand it back to Him. Find two or three men and hand them the part that's crushing. That isn't setting responsibility down. It's the most responsible thing you'll do this year.

## The one who took it up

Every carrying word in these pages was used first of someone else.

:::margin
Isaiah 53:4 (NIV)
"Surely he took up our pain and bore our suffering, yet we considered him punished by God, stricken by him, and afflicted."
:::

Took up. Bore. He picked up a weight He hadn't earned, over a wreck He didn't make, and He never once said the woman You put here with me. The cross is that's mine, spoken by the one man in history who could have said not mine and been right.

And why? Scripture gives the reason in a line: for the joy set before Him He endured the cross. He carried it with His eyes on a reward. That's the fourth mark living inside the second. You don't shoulder weight because you're strong or because it's fair. You shoulder it because the Father sees the beam nobody upstairs ever thanks, and He's the one who pays.

## Before you turn the page

If the weight is settling on you as you read, good. That's what it feels like to be the wall. But notice that it's settling, not crushing. God builds a man for the load he's given and sends seventy for the rest. You aren't asked to hold up the world. You're asked to hold up your corner of it and to stop pointing when it gets heavy. May He make you steady under what's yours, honest about what isn't, and may your house learn that the floor holds.
