# Proposal: 00-introduction (Aaron-locked)

## Panel touchpoints

- **Season tours.** Several readers felt each chapter stopped to run an age tour (twenties, forties, sixties). The revised chapters stop doing that. The introduction is the right place to tell the reader once how seasons are handled.
- **Closers.** Readers liked the four-line closers and wanted them kept. The introduction should tell a man how to use them, especially the "Tell a brother" line, since chapter 13 assumes some men have no brother yet.
- **Brothers at ~20 minutes a week.** The introduction can plant the promise that the book's brotherhood ask is small enough to actually happen.
- **Review-draft stamp language.** If the current introduction carries any "review draft," "working copy," or version wording in prose (not just footers), it needs to go.
- **Composites.** Chapters 3 through 35 use named men who are composites. The introduction is the natural place for the author's note on that.

## Proposed insertions (drop-in, original, Aaron to accept or rewrite)

### A. Season signpost (one paragraph)

> You'll notice I don't stop every chapter to sort you by age. I've read books that do that, and by the fourth "if you're in your twenties" I'd started skipping. Here's how this book works instead. When the counsel actually changes because of your season, I'll say so right there in the paragraph. The single man, the man with a newborn, the man who's divorced, the man whose kids are grown, the man who's buried his wife: you're all in the room, and I'll turn to you when it matters. Then in chapter 32 we'll sit down and talk about seasons on purpose, all of them, in one place.

### B. How to use the closers (one paragraph)

> Every chapter ends the same way, with four short lines. *Name it* asks you to say plainly what the chapter touched in you. *Do this week* gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday. *Tell a brother* asks you to say it out loud to another man. *Carry this* hands you one line to keep. If you don't have a brother yet, don't skip that line. Chapter 13 is for you, and until then, "tell a brother" can mean writing it down where you'll see it and telling God.

### C. The twenty-minute promise (two or three sentences)

> I'm not going to ask you to join a program. I'm going to ask you for about twenty minutes a week with one other man, and I'll show you exactly what to do with them. Most men can find twenty minutes. Most men have never been asked.

### D. Author's note on the men in the book (two sentences)

> The men you'll meet in these pages are composites. The conversations happened; the names and the details are mixed so no one can be picked out of a lineup.

### E. Four marks placement

If the introduction currently previews the four marks, keep the locked sentence exactly as written: *A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.* Chapter 3 explains each mark and addresses how these marks differ from the "wild heart" shelf of men's books, so the introduction doesn't need to do that work.

## Cuts proposed

- Any sentence that reads like a manuscript note ("in this draft," "reviewers will notice," "final version to include").
- Any version string, date stamp, or "review copy" line inside the prose.

## Continuity check

- Chapter 3 opens with "You made it through chapter 2 and you're not crazy," so the introduction should not promise a different order.
- Chapter 32 is referenced as the seasons chapter. Chapter 13 is referenced as the brothers chapter. Chapter 34 is referenced as the plan chapter.
