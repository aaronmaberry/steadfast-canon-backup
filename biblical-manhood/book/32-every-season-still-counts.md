# 32. Every Season Still Counts

February cotton looks finished. Bare dirt. Stubble. A fence with empty ground behind it.

July is already underway. Soil rests. Stubble goes back down. Winter rain sinks toward the depth roots will need in the heat. February has a job. Plant because the wait is unbearable, and the year is lost.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 3:1 (NIV)
"There is a time for everything, and a season for every activity under the heavens:"
:::

A season for every activity. I said early I would not sort this book by age on every page, and I have not. Here we sort once, on purpose. The culture ranks seasons. The ranking takes decades off men.

## The lie of the peak

Most men carry a private chart. Twenties for freedom. Thirties for building. Forties at the top. Fifties for coasting. Past sixty, a long exit with golf in it. A marriage that comes apart, an illness, a wife who dies, these get filed as detours on the way back to the chart.

Scripture has no chart like that. It knows a man serving God's purpose in the generation he actually lives. At eighty-five, Caleb asked for hill country.

:::margin
Joshua 14:12 (NIV)
"Now give me this hill country that the LORD promised me that day."
:::

Hardest ground on the map, asked for by an old man whose season still had work. I will walk the seasons. The pull. The assignment. Which man from these pages is standing there.

## The young single man

Strength and open hours, and the season men waste most. Micah sat six years at the back table and spent the first three waiting. The pull is to treat the years as a lobby. The other pull is the itch. Open hours and no one watching put you closer to that exit than you will be again.

The assignment is to build, and the list is already in your hands. Solomon, old, looking back, gave the young man one charge.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 12:1 (NIV)
"Remember your Creator in the days of your youth, before the days of trouble come and the years approach when you will say, 'I find no pleasure in them'"
:::

Find the Father at twenty-five and you skip thirty years of scanning the bleachers for a nod. Later seasons inherit the man you build in this one.

## The first year, and the first baby

A marriage starts getting decided the day after the honeymoon. Weddings are markers. Marriages are made of Tuesdays, and the first run of them lays a floor the next forty years will stand on. Know her. Lay your preferences down. Learn what she needs. Keep the twenty minutes with your brother. Drop the brothers and your wife is carrying a weight she was never meant to carry alone.

More young marriages break in the first year or two with a baby than in any other stretch. You met Tomás when his daughter was four months old. A third person has moved in and needs everything. Both of you fear this weather is permanent. It is this year. Inside the chaos, guard the marriage. Once the baby is down, ten minutes at the counter is the whole of the liturgy. This is the season. There is not a better one waiting. Watch for the darkness after a birth that has a medical name. A year in, Tomás said, "I found out I could be a man when I was too tired to be anything, and she found out she could count on me."

## The middle years

The culture names thirty-five to fifty-five the peak. It is where the career rises and the house gets lost. Anthony is here, in a doorway with the garage on one side and the kitchen on the other. The pulls wear decent clothes. Provision as a place to disappear. Drift dressed up as ordinary tired. The itch back, middle-aged, once the marriage has gone quiet.

Presence is the assignment. A floor that holds. A marriage tended, because the woman you married has changed and the woman here needs knowing. Brothers, since men this age have fewer friends than any other season. Hills for the children. A hard look at what the work is for, which is where the fourth mark gets tested. The children are watching that doorway.

## The season nobody plans

The man in my mind never wrote this season on a calendar. It has a job, and it lasts longer than people tell you.

Survival is all year one and year two can hold. Waves of grief show up at the child exchange. They show up again on Sunday nights. Anger aimed everywhere. The itch, because you are alone and you had not been. The pull is a turn. Move. Marry. Quit. Anything to stop the gauges screaming. Drive straight. Drive slow. The assignment is short. Grieve where someone can hear you. Own your part and leave hers off it. Children before any new woman. One brother, a phone call, every week. Stay in God's house even when the house is awkward about you.

Years three through five, the gauges settle. A real house, drawings on the refrigerator. You become the man other men on this road want to talk to. I will not promise another marriage. The season still counts. The Father who does not leave did not leave you in the courtroom. Walk it the second Man's way. Cover the children. Own your part. Refuse the exits. Men will follow the man who comes out the far side.

## The empty nest

The last child leaves. Twenty years of talk was about the children, and now the two of you are not sure what remains to say. Two strangers in one house is the pull. So is an affair. Invisible at home and visible somewhere else is a dangerous pairing.

Court her again. Walt did, once he had sold off part of the business. He called those years the best of the marriage, "because for the first time since 1994 it was just us and I actually showed up for it." After that, face outward. There are hills to give. A co-parenting arrangement, if that is what you built, gets these years from God for the marriage itself.

## The retired man

You already know Roy's rhythm. Scripture's retirement is a move from carrying to assisting. The pull is drift with a fishing license, and anger arriving through the television. Assist. Younger men. Gutters for a widow. The wife who waited forty years to have you home. Be Caleb. Ask for hard ground, and trust God to put it in your hands. "I kept the peace for thirty-one years," Roy said. "I've been making men for five. I know which one God's going to ask me about."

## The widower

Fifty-one years, and then Mr. Pruitt buried his wife. The following spring, forty pecan trees went into the ground. The picture is the assignment, grief-year included, before any tree went in the ground. The pull is to decide your life ended with hers. Grieve for as long as grief takes. Find one brother. God said alone is not good, and men past seventy were not excused. Work what garden is left. Old men were given a promise.

:::margin
Isaiah 46:4 (NIV)
"Even to your old age and gray hairs I am he, I am he who will sustain you. I have made you, and I will carry you; I will sustain you and I will rescue you."
:::

I will carry you. The same word Deuteronomy uses for a father carrying his son, here spoken over gray hair. A grandson who is not born yet will eat from those trees.

## The man in weakness

Sickness is some of your story. Caring for the sick is the story of others. Three years now, Mr. Alvarez has bathed his wife every morning. A man who cannot work is not finished. Paul begged to have a thorn taken, and the answer was no. Power made perfect in weakness. The pull is to feel useless. The assignment is to assist. Pray by name. A chair and a list make a man a power in a church. I know a homebound man who has prayed, by name, for every young man in his church, eleven years running. The young men know. That work counts.

## The last season

One season is left, and men dodge the subject. Scripture does not. Number your days. Live as a man who will hand in a completion report. Finish well. Say the things that need saying. Bless your children from the bed, as Jacob did. Leave the plumb bob to someone, and leave a story on it. Near the end, from a cell, Paul wrote this.

:::margin
2 Timothy 4:7 (NIV)
"I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith."
:::

Finished. Every man in this book stands here eventually. Live the four marks in the seasons before, and the moves are already learned.

## The marks don't change

At twenty-five, passivity looks like a tailgate. At seventy-five it looks like a recliner. A new father picks up a two o'clock feeding. A widower picks up a post-hole digger and forty bare-root trees. God's reward does not live inside the season. It is the same reward in all of them.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. Marks hold. Gardens change. Learn the motions once and the next season already knows your hands. No month is off. February counts. The season under your boots has work with your name on it. The Father who arranged the seasons is watching you do it.

## Before you turn the page

Put that ranking down. Men have paid for it in lost decades. May the Father who arranged the seasons hand you yours as one plain sentence with a verb in it. Nineteen or seventy-nine, may the garden under your boots turn out to have your name on it.
