# 12. Leave Without Slamming the Door

You can learn a lot about a grown man by watching him walk up to his parents' front door.

One man doesn't bother to knock. He has gray in his beard and a mortgage on the other side of town, and he has the refrigerator open before anybody says hello. His wife is still out on the step. Another man hasn't set foot on that porch in years. He can tell you word for word about the fight that closed it, and he calls what he has now freedom, but that house still has hold of him. A third man may even have a key. He knocks anyway, and he waits. When the door opens, he says, "Hey, Dad."

The third man has left home. The other two are still standing in the doorway, one with his hand on the knob and one with his back turned, and neither of them knows it. A knock is what God had in mind for a grown son.

:::margin
Genesis 2:24 (NIV)
"That is why a man leaves his father and mother and is united to his wife, and they become one flesh."
:::

The leaving belongs to the man. It comes before "is united to his wife," so a single man is already under it. And God put the other half where nobody could miss it, in the Ten Commandments.

:::margin
Exodus 20:12 (NIV)
"Honor your father and your mother, so that you may live long in the land the LORD your God is giving you."
:::

Leave, and honor. One move does both. A slammed door manages the leaving and fails the honor. A hand that won't let go of the knob manages the honor and fails the leaving. God is after a son who does both. A knock says two things at once. I live somewhere else now. And this is still your house, and I'm glad you opened the door.

## The man who never left

Start with the hand on the knob. He's sure this chapter was written for a louder man. I've been sure of that myself.

If your mother's opinion still outranks your wife's in your own kitchen, you haven't left. If one remark from your father can still make you twelve years old, and you arrange your decisions so he won't make it, you haven't left either. If you're single, well into your thirties, and every big choice still gets cleared at their table, it's the same answer. Moving your furniture into another building was a separate event.

A wise man keeps taking counsel his whole life, and good counsel is a gift. But a life whose center stays in someone else's kitchen never grows a center of its own. Your children need the man in their house to be their father, and to make his own calls.

Leaving well doesn't take a fight. Decide, and then tell them. If you ask first, you've handed the decision back. Say, "I love you, and we're going to do it this way." Stand between your wife and your mother's criticism, even on the days your mother is right. Keep your voice low and your back straight.

## The man who slammed it

Now the man with his back turned. Some of you left houses no boy should have had to live in. I won't send you back, and I won't send you toward a man who is still dangerous. But I've watched what a slam does to a man too many times to keep quiet about it.

A slam doesn't stay behind you. Anger is a kind of attention. The father you cut off comes with you into every room, and your days start arranging themselves around him. You're more tied to him than the son who calls every Sunday. You just can't see the rope, because anger made it.

Your kids are watching how you treat your father. That's how they'll learn to treat you. Contempt gets saved up for the year you're the old man. Honor, given to a man who didn't earn it, teaches them something no sermon can.

## Honor when he wasn't honorable

Honor is aimed at the office a father holds, the place God gave him in your life. Scripture never asks you to call a bad father good, and it never asks you to lie about him.

A soldier can salute a rank long after he's lost all respect for the man wearing it. The salute goes to the office, and it's still clean.

With a hard father, honor gets practical. Tell the truth about him, and leave out the mockery. Provide for him when he's old, even if he never provided for you. Pray for him, even if the whole prayer is "Father, I don't know how to pray for this man, but You know him."

Forgiveness lives inside that honor. What he did mattered, and God knows exactly how much. Trust is a separate choice. You can forgive him and still refuse to leave your children alone with him. Some tables aren't safe, and a restored one may never come. When you forgive, you decide that God will settle the debt, and you stop trying to collect it. Your peace quits waiting on an apology that may never come.

:::margin
Romans 12:19 (NIV)
"Do not take revenge, my dear friends, but leave room for God's wrath, for it is written: 'It is mine to avenge; I will repay,' says the Lord."
:::

Hear the relief in "leave room for God's wrath." Paying him back was never your job.

Joseph's brothers threw him into a pit and then sold him. Years later, when he held every advantage over them, he said the clearest thing I know about this kind of forgiveness.

:::margin
Genesis 50:20 (NIV)
"You intended to harm me, but God intended it for good to accomplish what is now being done, the saving of many lives."
:::

He named what they meant to do. You intended to harm me. Then he named what God did with it. He was honest about the wound and trusting about what God had made of it, and he didn't hand them a bill.

## The knock

If your father is alive and it's safe to go near him, a conversation may be waiting on his porch. Some men should leave that conversation alone, because some talks only tear open a wound God is already closing. But plenty of men have sat in the truck outside that house for years and never knocked. The knock has four parts.

First, say what you needed and didn't get. Keep it to one sentence. "Dad, I needed to hear you were proud of me, and I never did." One sentence can land. Ten will bounce off.

Second, say what you can thank him for. Even a hard father usually left you something. A way of working. A roof. One afternoon on the water. Gratitude is honor doing its work.

Third, say what you've decided. "I'm going to raise my kids differently, and I'm done carrying this against you." A decision sounds different from a threat.

Fourth, hold the door open yourself. "I'd like to know you as a man, if you're willing." His apology isn't the price of that offer. Whatever he does with it stays on his shoulders.

If you wrote the letter in "The Empty Chair," get it out now. The first two parts are probably already on the page.

Grant knocked on the door of a father who had never once had a kind word for him. Years earlier, Grant had left that house in a slam. He sat in his truck in the driveway for a long while before he got out. His father opened the door and said nothing. Grant said the four parts, standing on the porch, and then he stopped. No apology came. His father never said he was proud of him. The old man's eyes went past Grant to the field across the road and stayed there. Then he asked, "You eat yet?" and turned toward the kitchen, and he left the door open behind him. Grant followed him in. That supper, he told me later, was as close to a hug as the old man had in him. Grant took it. He had quit demanding more than his father had to give.

If your father is dead, or too dangerous to go near, he still gets the four parts. God hears them, and a brother can hear them. The words need to leave you even if they have no way to reach him.

## You'll leave more than once

The verse names father and mother, so a mother who raised you alone is in it too. In your house, your wife comes first. Men leave home more than once, and both kinds of failure keep coming back.

A single man is tempted either to cut his parents off or to call them before every big choice. Decide, then tell them, and let the telling carry your love. A young father has to leave again when the baby arrives with grandparents attached. He learns to say, in his mother's hearing, "We're doing it my wife's way." After a divorce, the pull is back into his mother's kitchen. She'll make room, because she loves him, and it's easy to let her kitchen become the place he hides from building a life of his own. He still has to build it. In the middle years his father starts to age, and every visit sends him home a boy again. The four-part talk isn't too late.

And some of you are on the other side of the door now, with a son who slammed it or drifted away. Be the father Jesus described, the one who went out to both of his sons. Make the call that doesn't get answered. If you failed him, say it once. "I wasn't the father you needed, and I'm sorry." If he knocks, set another plate.

## Knock, and wait

Look at both men through the four marks. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. Passivity is what keeps a hand on the knob for years, and it has to be rejected. What the slammed door left behind is honor still owed, and accepting responsibility means owning that debt. Both men have a walk to lead, up the steps to the door. And both are done treating their father's apology as the prize. There's a better one in God's hand.

Whatever kind of father he was, he was yours, and the way you treat him now shows on you. Leave gently. Pull the door shut behind you and don't let it make a sound. When you come back, knock. And build a house with a door your own son will be able to knock on when it's his turn.

## Before you turn the page

Tell God the truth about your father, and hand Him the debt. Honor the office on your way out, and let the door close quietly. May the Father who stayed with you make that leaving steady, give you grace for the knock that comes later, and teach your children, by watching you, how to leave you well.
