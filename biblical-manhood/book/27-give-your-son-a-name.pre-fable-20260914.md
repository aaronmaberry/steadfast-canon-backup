# 27. Give Your Son a Name

The first weight a boy ever carries is his father's name. He didn't pick it. It was on the mailbox before he was born. And for the rest of his life, when he says it, people who knew his father will hear the father in it, and the boy will feel that, and he'll learn early whether the name is a thing to stand on or a thing to live down. I've known men who spent forty years trying to make their last name mean something other than what their father made it mean. I've known others who could walk into a feed store two counties over and get credit on a handshake because of what their father's name meant there.

That's what this chapter is about, but it's about more than the last name. A boy needs his father to name him. Not only to hand him a word on a birth certificate, but to tell him, over and over, in a thousand ordinary moments, who he is. Because if his father doesn't, somebody else will, and the world is full of names for boys, and almost none of them are good.

## The names he's getting anyway

Before I tell you what to call your son, let me tell you what he's being called.

He's being called things at school. Some of them are fine, and some of them will stick for life if nobody gives him something heavier to stand on: lazy, stupid, weird, too small, too much.

He's being called things by the culture, which has decided that boys are a problem to be managed. He'll hear that his energy is a disorder, that his aggression is toxic, that his wanting to build and climb and fight and win is something to be medicated or lectured out of him. Some of that has a grain of truth in it. Most of it is a world that doesn't know what boys are for, naming them out of fear.

He's being called things by his own failures. Every boy fails, constantly, at everything, because that's what learning is, and a boy with no one to tell him otherwise will begin to believe the failures are his name, and the name sounds like this: I can't, I'm bad at this, I always mess it up.

And he may be getting called things at home, by you, that you don't hear yourself saying: the sigh, the eye roll, "Why can't you be more like your sister." "What is wrong with you." "You're just like your father," said by a mother who didn't mean it kindly, or "you're just like your mother," said by you in anger, which he'll carry. Nicknames that started as jokes and landed as verdicts. I sat with a man in his fifties who could still tell me the exact word his father used for him at nine, and his voice changed when he said it.

Chapter 18 said that God gave men the power to name and that the world listens when a man speaks. On a son, that power is nuclear. What you call him, he becomes. What you don't call him, he'll go looking for somebody to call him, and the somebodies who volunteer are coaches with agendas, older boys with worse names, and a screen that will call him anything he wants to hear.

## The three names

Here's what a boy needs to hear from his father: three names, given over and over, from the first week of his life to the last week of yours.

The first name is mine, and it's the name of belonging. It says: you are my son, and you're mine, and I'm not going anywhere. Before he's good at anything, before he's earned anything, before he's a man or a success or anything else, he's yours, and that's settled, and nothing he does can unsettle it. This is the name Jesus received first, at the Jordan, before He'd done a single thing.

:::margin
Mark 1:11 (NIV)
"And a voice came from heaven: 'You are my Son, whom I love; with you I am well pleased.'"
:::

You are my Son, whom I love, with you I am well pleased: three things in one sentence, and the first of them is possession. Every boy needs to hear "mine" from his father so many times that it stops being information and becomes the floor he stands on. A boy who knows he belongs can fail at anything and come home. A boy who isn't sure he belongs will spend his life auditioning, and we spent chapter 8 on what that does to a man.

The second name is what I see in you. That's the name of character, and it's about who he is rather than what he did. When your son tells the truth about the broken window, you say, "That's honesty. You're an honest man." When he stands up for the kid getting picked on, you say, "That's courage. You've got courage in you." When he helps his mother without being asked, you say, "You're a protector. That's what protectors do." When he keeps going on something hard, you say, "You don't quit. That's who you are."

You're doing two things when you say that. You're catching him being what God made him and naming it so he knows what it's called. And you're speaking the name a little ahead of the boy, prophetically, so that he has something to grow into. He isn't fully honest yet; no boy is. But a boy who has been called honest by his father a hundred times will feel a lie sit wrong in his mouth in a way that a boy who has only been called a liar never will. Paul described his own fathering in a sentence I'd put on every father's bathroom mirror.

:::margin
1 Thessalonians 2:11-12 (NIV)
"For you know that we dealt with each of you as a father deals with his own children, encouraging, comforting and urging you to live lives worthy of God, who calls you into his kingdom and glory."
:::

Encouraging, comforting, and urging. That's the second name in three verbs. Encouraging says, I see it in you. Comforting says, when you fail, you're still mine. Urging says, now live up to what I see. A father who only urges, without encouraging and comforting, produces a boy who can never rest. A father who only comforts, without urging, produces a boy who never grows. All three, over and over, is how a son gets named.

The third name is man. That's the handoff word, and it's the one chapter 11 said most men never got. At some point, and in a way he'll remember, your son needs to hear his father say: you are becoming a man, and here is what that means. Not "you're the man of the house now" tossed over a shoulder on the way out the door. A deliberate, weighted, spoken word from a father to a son that says, I see you crossing over, and I'm going to tell you what's on the other side, and I'm going to walk you across.

The next two chapters are about how to do that with a hill and a day. Here I only want to say that the word itself is a name, and the boy needs it from you. Every boy is asking whether he's a man yet. He'll ask it of a girl, a fight, a paycheck, a truck, if his father doesn't answer it first. Answer it, on purpose and out loud.

## How to give a name

Naming isn't a speech. It's a habit, and here's the habit. Every day, catch him and call it. Once a day, find one thing your son did that a man does and name it. "That's what a man does." Roy said that sentence to a thirty-year-old at a breakfast table and the man came back every week for a year. Imagine what it does to a nine-year-old from his own father. It takes five seconds, and it's the highest-return five seconds in fathering.

Every week, give the blessing. Pick a night; Sunday works. Put your hand on his head and say three sentences. Who he is: "You're my son and I love you and I'm not going anywhere." What you see: "This week I saw you tell the truth when it cost you. That's courage." What God says: "God made you on purpose and He's got work for you, and I'm going to help you find it." Then a short prayer. It will feel strange the first three times, and you keep going anyway, because boys who get blessed weekly grow up with a floor under them that nothing can take out.

:::margin
Deuteronomy 6:6-7 (NIV)
"These commandments that I give you today are to be on your hearts. Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up."
:::

When you lie down and when you get up. The blessing isn't a ceremony. It's what you say at bedtime, in the truck, at breakfast. Impress them, the text says, and that's a word about pressure over time, like a mark pressed into leather. That's how names get given.

Every year, write the letter. On his birthday, write your son a letter of one page. Who he is this year, what you've watched him become, what you see coming, and that you love him. Put it in a box. Give him the box when he's eighteen, or at the day we'll talk about in chapter 29. A man with eighteen letters from his father, in his father's handwriting, telling him who he is, can walk into any room on earth.

In public, praise him; in private, correct him. When he's done well, tell people in front of him: his grandparents, his coach, your friends at church. "This is my son. He finished the whole fence with me Saturday and didn't quit." Let him hear his father brag. When he's done wrong, take him aside. Never name his failure in front of others. Shame in public is a name that sticks, and praise in public is a name that lifts.

When he fails, separate the act from the name. This is the one that saves boys. Suppose he told a lie. You say, "That was a lie. You are not a liar. You're my son, who told a lie, and here's what my son does next: he goes and tells the truth to the person he lied to." The act gets named accurately, and the boy gets named accurately, and they aren't the same name. A father who says "you're a liar" has handed his son a name he'll either fight or fulfill. A father who says "you're my honest son who did a dishonest thing" has handed him a way back.

And always, tell him whose he is. Under your name is a bigger one. Tell your son he was made by God on purpose. Tell him the Father who does not leave is his Father too. Tell him what God calls him: made in the image, loved, called. Teach him to pray "Father" the way you learned in chapter 10. Your name over him is good and it isn't enough; you'll fail him and you'll die. God's name over him is the one that holds, and the best thing your name can do is point at that one.

## What a boy does with a name

Cole's father never named him, not once in thirty-three years of criticism, and the boy grew into a man who was competent and hollow, always waiting for a sentence that didn't come. Then a seventy-year-old deacon said "you're a good man" by a truck, and Cole sat in the cab and cried for twenty minutes. It was one sentence, one name from a man who wasn't even his father, and it fixed something that thirty-three years hadn't.

That's how hungry boys are for this, and that's how much power your words have over your son. If a stranger's sentence can do that to a grown man, think what a father's daily naming does to a boy who is still soft enough to take the impression.

Cole has a son now, three years old. He names him every night. "I'm not going to make him wait thirty-three years to hear it from a stranger," he said.

## Naming in every season

If you have a baby boy, start now. He doesn't understand the words yet, but he understands the voice. Hold him and say "you're my son" over him every day, and talk to him, and pray over him out loud. New fathers often feel useless with an infant because they can't feed him and they don't know what to do, and here's what to do: name him. The words seep in before comprehension does, and a boy who has heard his father's voice say "mine" ten thousand times before he's two has been named in a way he will never be able to un-hear.

If this week is diapers and you feel useless, here is the infant move: every day before Tuesday, hold him for ten quiet minutes with your phone in another room and say his name and "you're my son" out loud, even if he only sleeps on your chest. That is not waiting for sports. That is naming him now, on a Tuesday, while his whole world is still your voice.

If you're divorced, your son may be getting other names at another house, from a stepfather, or from his mother's grief, or from the silence about you. You can't control that. You can control the names he gets from you, and consistency is everything. The Tuesday call is where you name him. The weekend is where you name him. Never make him a messenger or a spy between houses; that gives him a name nobody wants. Tell him he's not in the middle. Tell him he's yours and that isn't up for negotiation no matter where he sleeps on Wednesday. Kenny's son said "nothing" for eight months on the Tuesday calls, and Kenny ended every single one of them with the same sentence: "You're my son. I love you. I'll call you Tuesday." That's naming, and it worked.

If you're an older man, you have grandsons and you have grown sons, and both need naming. A grandfather's "that's what a man does" carries a weight that a father's sometimes can't, because the boy knows Grandpa has seen a lot of men, so use it. And if your grown son never got the third name from you, if you never told him he was a man and what that meant, it isn't too late. Roy told his son at forty-four, on a porch, that he was proud of the man he'd become and that he was sorry he'd never said so when it would have been easier to hear. His son didn't say much. Three weeks later he called and asked Roy to come teach his grandson to fish.

And if you're a single man, or a man with no sons, there are boys in your church and on your street with no man to name them. Coach them, teach them, show up for them, and when you see one of them do what a man does, say so, out loud, in front of the others. You have no idea what one sentence from an unrelated man can do to a fatherless boy, and if you doubt it, ask Cole.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A son of fifteen needs the third name most, the one that says he's becoming a man and here's what that means, and he needs the second name spoken in front of other men. Cole's son is three, but Cole told me he's already planning the sentence for fifteen. The takeaway for the father of a teenager is that a boy who's called honest by his father a hundred times feels a lie sit wrong in his mouth, and that the naming has to be louder in the years when the world's names get loudest.

A man of forty-five with a son of seventeen is often locked in a standoff, and the takeaway for him is to separate the act from the name: the boy did a dishonest thing, and he is not a liar, and here is what my son does next. That sentence has saved more teenage boys than any lecture I know.

## The Name that holds

Every name you give your son is a small copy of the Name God gives His sons: mine, what I see in you, man. God says all three over every man who's in Christ, and He said them over you, whether or not your father ever did.

:::margin
Psalm 127:3 (NIV)
"Children are a heritage from the LORD, offspring a reward from him."
:::

A reward is what the psalm calls him. Your son is God's reward to you, and you're God's gift to your son, and the gift you give him back is his name. There's a boy in your house who's asking who he is. He asks every day, in every mess and every win, in the way he watches your face, and you get to answer him. Give him the first weight worth carrying: a name, from his father, that he can stand on for the rest of his life.

## Before you turn the page

If you're the son who never got a name, and you've read this chapter with a stone in your chest, I want you to know that you can give what you didn't get, and that giving it does something in you that receiving it would have. Cole didn't wait for his father to change before he started naming his own boy. And if you've been giving your son the sigh and the eye roll without hearing yourself, the fix starts tonight with a hand on his head and three sentences. May the Father who called His Son beloved before He'd done a thing put the same words in your mouth, and may your son grow up with a floor under him that nothing can take out.

**Name it.** Because a boy hears what his father says without meaning to as clearly as what he says on purpose: say what your son has actually been hearing from you, the three names or the sigh and the eye roll and the verdicts; and if you're the son who never got a name, say that too.

**Do this week.** Because naming is a daily habit before it's anything else: every day before Tuesday, catch your son doing one thing a man does and name it, "that's honesty," "that's courage"; then pick a night and give the three-sentence blessing with your hand on his head. If you have no son, find one fatherless boy and name one thing you saw him do.

**Tell a brother.** Because fathers need to say out loud what they've been calling their sons: tell him the names you've been giving without meaning to, ask what his father called him, and ask what he's calling his own son.

**Carry this.** What you call him, he becomes. If you don't name your son, the world will, and the world has no good names for boys.
