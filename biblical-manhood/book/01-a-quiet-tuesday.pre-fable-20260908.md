# 1. A Quiet Tuesday

If you had asked me at twenty-five to name the days that would decide what kind of man I became, I would have given you a list of big ones: the wedding, the day the first child came, the funeral of a father, the job I took or the job I turned down, the morning I stood in a river and let a pastor put me under. I would have told you those were the hinges, and I would have believed it, and I would have been mostly wrong.

I've had all of those days now, and I can tell you that not one of them decided much of anything. They marked things and they announced things, and they were stones on a riverbank that I can point to and say, that's when. But the deciding happened somewhere else, in a long string of days I couldn't tell apart if you paid me, and the more men I sit with, the more I'm convinced it works that way for every one of us.

A man's life is decided on Tuesdays. I mean that almost literally. Not on the Sunday when he stands up in church, and not on the Saturday when he takes his son to the lease, and not even on the Friday when the paycheck lands or the Monday when the week's trouble starts. On the day in the middle, with nothing special in it. The day nobody remembers. The day he comes home tired and the house is loud and there's a conversation he could have or not have, a call he could make or not make, a battery he could change or walk under one more time. That's where it happens. That's where the marks get made.

## What a Tuesday looks like

Let me show you one, because a claim like that ought to be able to survive a specific day.

A man I'll call Anthony left for work at 6:10 on a Tuesday in October, having slept badly, because his wife had said something the night before about money that wasn't quite fair and he'd let it sit, and it was still sitting there in the truck with him as he drove. He got to the shop having promised a customer the truck would be ready by lunch, then found the critical part still sitting unordered on the counter where he'd meant to call it in yesterday, and had to phone the owner and push the job while the shop lead overheard from two bays over. Around ten a customer called to complain about something that wasn't Anthony's fault, and Anthony was polite to him in a way that cost something. At lunch he ate in the truck and looked at his phone for forty minutes and couldn't have told you afterward one thing he'd read.

In the afternoon his father called. His father calls maybe twice a year, and every time the phone shows that name Anthony feels nine years old for a second, and this time he let it ring through to voicemail and told himself he'd call back later. He finished the day's work well. He is good at his job. He drove home listening to nothing, pulled into the garage, and stood in the doorway between the garage and the kitchen for a minute the way he does most nights, hearing his kids be loud and his wife tell somebody to sit down, not sure why he wasn't walking in.

Then he walked in. He kissed his wife on the side of her head without looking at her, and he ate, and he got the kids to bed, more or less, and by 8:40 he was in the recliner with the game on and his phone in his hand, and his wife was in the kitchen, and neither of them said anything about the money, and around ten he went to bed and lay there a while before he slept.

That's a Tuesday, and there's nothing in it you'd put in a book, and I've just put it in a book, because I want you to see how much of a man's life was actually decided in those sixteen hours.

The thing he let sit from the night before was a decision. So was the unordered part, the call that pushed the job, and the voicemail. The doorway, the kiss without looking, the phone at lunch and again at 8:40: none of them felt like decisions. That's the whole point. They felt like a day. And a man who has ten thousand days like that will become a particular kind of man without ever once choosing to, because the choosing was happening the whole time, in pieces too small to notice.

Now, Anthony's a good man. I want to be clear about that, because the point of this book isn't to make good men feel like failures. He didn't sin against anybody on that Tuesday, not in any way you could name. He worked hard and provided and came home and was gentle enough. But he is becoming something, one Tuesday at a time, and if you asked his wife what he's becoming she could tell you, and it isn't what he'd choose if he thought he was choosing.

## Why the big days can't do it

Here's why I've stopped trusting the big days to make men. A big day is a burst. It gathers up everything you feel and believe and want to be, and it puts it all on the table at once, in front of witnesses, with music playing, and that's a good thing, because God builds days like that into a man's life on purpose, and later in this book I'm going to tell you to make one for your son. But a burst is not a shape. It doesn't leave a mark by itself, because a mark is what gets left on you by what you do over and over, and a big day, by definition, is something you do once.

Think about your hands. If you've worked with them, they have marks on them: a ridge of callus across the palm, a finger that doesn't straighten all the way, a scar from a fence stretcher that slipped. Nobody got those from one hard day. You got them from a thousand ordinary ones, none of which you remember. The big days of your working life, the first day and the last and the one when you got hurt, didn't shape your hands. The ordinary Tuesdays did.

The men who go to a conference and come home on fire and are back in the recliner within two weeks aren't weak men. They've just misunderstood where a life gets made. They put everything into the burst and nothing into the shape, and the shape is where the marks come from.

And there's another reason the big days can't do it, which is that on the big days you're watched. At a wedding, at a baptism, at a ceremony, at a funeral, you know what a man is supposed to do and everyone's looking, and most men do it. That doesn't tell you much about the man. What tells you about the man is what he does when the room is empty, when nobody would know if he made the call or didn't, opened the drawer or didn't, said the true thing or let it sit. Tuesday is when nobody's watching. That's exactly why it counts.

## What God does with Tuesdays

If you've been in church a while, you've probably absorbed the idea that the spiritual life happens in the big moments too, at the retreat and the revival and the altar, and God does meet men in those places; I'm not going to pretend He doesn't. But look at how the Bible actually talks about the way a man is formed.

:::margin
Luke 16:10 (NIV)
"Whoever can be trusted with very little can also be trusted with much, and whoever is dishonest with very little will also be dishonest with much."
:::

Very little is a Tuesday phrase if there ever was one. Jesus is saying that the man who can be trusted in the small unwatched things is the man who can be trusted in the large watched ones, and that you don't get the second man without the first. He is saying that the recliner and the voicemail and the kiss without looking are not neutral. They're the training ground. They are where the man who'll one day stand in a hard room gets made, or doesn't. And when Jesus tells a story about a man who built a house that held, notice what He says about how the man built it.

:::margin
Matthew 7:24 (NIV)
"Therefore everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man who built his house on the rock."
:::

Puts them into practice. Not hears them and is moved by them. Practice is a Tuesday word too. It's what you do again and again until it's how you do things. The wise man in that story didn't build his house in a burst on a big day. He built it the way houses get built, one course at a time, on days nobody photographed, and when the rain came the house held because of all those days and not because of any one of them.

Most of a man's walk with God is Tuesdays. Most of his marriage is Tuesdays. Most of his fathering is Tuesdays. If you want to know what kind of man you're becoming, don't look at how you did at the retreat. Look at last Tuesday.

## The other Tuesday

Let me give you Anthony's Tuesday again, a year later, because I want you to see that I'm not describing a trap. I'm describing a place where a man has more power than he thinks.

He left at 6:10 again, and he'd slept badly again, and there was something from the night before sitting in the truck with him again. But somewhere on the drive he said, out loud, "Father, I don't want to carry this all day," and then he pulled over at a gas station and sent his wife a text that said he was sorry he'd gone quiet, and he'd like to talk about it after the kids were down. It took him ninety seconds. He was still tired. But the thing wasn't sitting in the truck with him anymore.

At the shop he had another truck promised by lunch. This time he'd ordered the part the afternoon before and confirmed the ticket on the counter before he clocked in. When a different job still went sideways mid-morning, he walked out to the truck, waited a minute and a half for his body to come down, then went to the shop lead himself, told him the truth, and called the customer before anyone had to overhear a scramble. His father called in the afternoon, and Anthony felt nine years old for a second, and then he answered, and it was an awkward six minutes about nothing, and it was the first time in two years he hadn't let it go to voicemail.

When he came home he stood in the doorway for a second out of habit and then he noticed he was doing it and walked in. He put his phone on the counter face down, which he'd started doing a few months before after another man told him to, and he found his wife and looked at her when he kissed her. After the kids were down they sat at the kitchen counter for ten minutes and talked about the money, and it wasn't a good conversation exactly, but it was one, and he laid down something he'd wanted to say and said something truer instead. Then he did the dishes. Then he prayed, badly and briefly, with his hand on her back. Then he went to bed and slept.

Nothing about that day would make a book either. His daughter, who is seven and who watches his face when her mother walks in, would not be able to tell you what was different. But something was different, and it was different in about six places, each of which took less than two minutes, and if you gave that man ten thousand of those Tuesdays instead of the other kind, you would end up with a different man, and a different marriage, and a different girl watching a different face.

That's the claim of this book. Not that you need a big day. That you need to know what a man does on a Tuesday, and then do it, and then do it again.

## The same Tuesday at every age

A Tuesday doesn't look the same at nineteen as it does at seventy, but it decides the same things, and I want you to see yourself in one of these four men before we go on.

A nineteen-year-old I'll call Eli works the counter at a parts store and lives with two roommates. His Tuesday has a moment in it around ten at night when the apartment goes quiet and the phone is in his hand, and what he does in that moment is deciding what kind of man he'll be at thirty far more than anything he'll say at church on Sunday. He told me once that he thought his real life would start when something big happened to him. His real life is the ten o'clock moment, and it's happening now.

Tomás, who you'll meet more later, is twenty-nine with a baby who doesn't sleep. His Tuesday has a moment in it at two in the morning when he hears the crying and hears his wife start to move and could simply lie there, and he's so tired that lying there feels like the only reasonable thing a man could do. Whether he gets up is deciding the marriage. Not the anniversary trip they can't afford; the two o'clock.

Walt is fifty-five and built a business from one truck to eleven, and his Tuesday has a moment in it at 6:15 in the evening when he could leave the shop and be home for dinner or could stay another hour because the shop is quieter than the house. He stayed for twenty years, one Tuesday at a time, and never once decided to. The staying decided itself, in pieces too small to notice, until his wife said a sentence in the kitchen that I'll tell you about later in this book.

And Roy is seventy-two, retired from thirty-one years as a state trooper, and his Tuesday has a moment in it at 5:30 in the morning when the alarm goes off and there's no badge to put on and no reason anyone would notice if he slept until nine. What he does at 5:30 is deciding whether his last twenty years will be a long slow exit or the most important stretch of his life. He gets up and makes the coffee. You'll see why that matters.

Four men, four ages, four moments that don't feel like decisions. That's the whole book, and that's why it starts here.

## What comes next

Soon I will give you a sentence with four marks in it, and then I'm going to spend the rest of the book showing you what those marks look like on a Tuesday, in every season a man can be in, at every table I've sat at. But before I do that I need to say one thing to the man who's reading this and suspecting that everything he's been told about being a man is either contradictory or thin or both. That man is not crazy. That's the next chapter.

For now, do one thing. Think about last Tuesday. Not the highlight of your week. The ordinary day in the middle of it. Walk through it hour by hour in your head, the way I walked you through Anthony's, and notice how many small decisions were in it that didn't feel like decisions. That's your life, and that's where the marks are being made. The good news, and it is very good news, is that it's also where they can be remade, starting tomorrow, which is probably a Tuesday.

## Before you turn the page

I don't know which of those four you are, or whether you're somewhere in between, but I know this about your week. It has a Tuesday in it, and the Tuesday has a moment in it, probably several, where you'll choose without feeling like you're choosing. I'm not going to ask you to be a different man by Sunday. I'm going to ask you to see the moment, this once, and to do one small thing in it differently, and to trust that the Father who made you sees the moment too and is glad when you meet it. May He give you eyes for the ordinary day, and the small strength it takes to walk through a doorway instead of standing in it.
