# 7. Live for a Reward That Lasts

Mr. Pruitt planted forty pecan trees the spring after he buried his wife. He was seventy-one years old.

I helped him set a few of them, bare-root trees not much thicker than a broom handle, each one going into a hole he'd dug himself with a post-hole digger and a great deal of stopping to breathe. When I asked him why forty, he said that was how many the orchard company had on the truck. When I asked him why at all, he stood up straight and looked down the row for a long moment before he answered.

"I won't eat one of these," he said. "You know that. My grandson might. Somebody's grandson will." Then he bent back down and went on tamping.

I have thought about that afternoon more than I've thought about most sermons, because Mr. Pruitt was doing something that made no sense on any scoreboard he would live to see. He was spending his strength, which was running out, on a harvest he would never taste, and he wasn't sad about it. As far as I could tell he was the most settled man in the county.

That's the fourth mark. A real man expects the greater reward, God's reward. I saved it for last in this part of the book because it's the mark that makes the other three hold. You can reject passivity for a while on willpower. You can carry weight for a while out of pride. You can go first for a while because you enjoy being seen going first. But every one of those runs out of fuel eventually, and the only thing I have ever seen keep a man doing the right thing for forty years when nobody claps is that he's working for Somebody who is.

## Every man lives for a reward

Let me begin by taking away an excuse, because some men hear "live for a reward" and feel a little uneasy, as if it were beneath them. Shouldn't a good man do good for its own sake? Isn't wanting a reward a bit mercenary?

Here is the plain truth. You already live for a reward, because every man does, and nobody gets up at five in the morning for nothing, and the only real question is which reward you've chosen.

Some men live for applause. The crowd, the promotion, the compliment from the boss, the pastor noticing, the text from a buddy that says you crushed it. It pays out fast and it feels wonderful and it's gone by Wednesday.

Some men live for money, and most of them aren't greedy. Money is simply the one scoreboard that never lies about whether you won, and it pays on a schedule. It also never says "well done." It only ever says "more."

Some men live for being wanted, by women mostly. A single man can shape his whole life around the next woman noticing him, and a married man can shape his around the next woman noticing him too, and both will tell you it isn't about sex, and both will be about half right.

Some men live for being right: the argument won, the point proved, the doubter shown up.

Some men live for their father's approval, and they'll go on living for it twenty years after the man is in the ground. I've watched sixty-year-old men still trying to earn a nod from a grave.

Some men live for comfort, which isn't a large reward but is paid out daily. The recliner, the routine, the not being bothered.

And some men live for being remembered. The building with their name on it, the town that will tell stories, the children who'll speak of them.

Every one of those rewards is real and every one of them pays. And every one of them has the same two problems. It runs out, and it's controlled by someone other than God. The crowd turns, the money goes, the woman leaves, the father dies without saying it, and the town forgets faster than you'd believe. A man who lives for any of them has handed the wheel of his life to something that doesn't love him. Jesus didn't tell men to stop living for a reward. He told them to pick a better one.

:::margin
Matthew 6:19-21 (NIV)
"Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moths and vermin destroy, and where thieves break in and steal. But store up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where moths and vermin do not destroy, and where thieves do not break in and steal. For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also."
:::

Store up is an accumulating word. He's telling you to build a fortune. He's only telling you to build it somewhere the weather can't get to it.

## The Father who sees in secret

The heart of this mark is in a sentence Jesus said three times in a row in one sermon, about giving and praying and fasting.

:::margin
Matthew 6:3-4 (NIV)
"But when you give to the needy, do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing, so that your giving may be in secret. Then your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you."
:::

Do it in secret, He says, and your Father sees, and He will reward you. Sit with that for a moment, because it's stranger than it sounds at first. The most important audience in your life is invisible, and He's watching the things nobody else sees. The tithe check nobody knows about. The two o'clock feeding. The lust you turned away from in a hotel room in Odessa with no one to catch you. The kindness to your ex-wife at the exchange that your buddies would have called weakness. The fifteen-year-old you keep showing up for who hasn't said thank you once. Nobody saw any of it. He saw all of it.

And He isn't indifferent, which is the word I want to press on, because the text says He rewards. Most Christian men, if you asked them, would say God forgives and God loves and God is patient. Fewer would say God rewards, and fewer still would say it like they meant it. But Scripture says it flatly and repeatedly, and the man who doesn't believe it will go looking for his reward from an audience that can see him.

:::margin
Colossians 3:23-24 (NIV)
"Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord, not for human masters, since you know that you will receive an inheritance from the Lord as a reward. It is the Lord Christ you are serving."
:::

Whatever you do, Paul says, and he doesn't mean church work. He means the pipeline, the classroom, the truck, the spreadsheet, the diaper. Work at it with your whole heart because you're working for Him, and He pays.

## Is that mercenary?

I promised to come back to this, so let me tell you why wanting God's reward isn't the kind of wanting a man should be ashamed of.

There's a kind of man who marries a woman for her money, and we call that mercenary and we're right to. There's another kind who marries a woman because he wants her, wants the life with her, wants to wake up beside her for fifty years, and nobody calls him mercenary for wanting his reward. The reward is the thing itself. Wanting it is simply what love looks like.

The reward God offers isn't a payment separate from Him. It is Him: His "well done," His pleasure, a place at His table, more of Him to enjoy forever. When Jesus tells the story of the servants and their master, the reward for faithfulness isn't a bonus check.

:::margin
Matthew 25:21 (NIV)
"His master replied, 'Well done, good and faithful servant! You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things. Come and share your master's happiness!'"
:::

Come and share your master's happiness. That's the reward: to hear Him say it, and then to be let into His joy. Wanting that isn't mercenary. Not wanting it would be strange. A son who works his father's land all day and doesn't want to hear "well done" at the end of it isn't humble. He's either lying or he's given up on the father. Moses understood this better than most. He had Egypt, the palace and the title and everything a man could live for on this side, and Hebrews says he did the arithmetic.

:::margin
Hebrews 11:24-26 (NIV)
"By faith Moses, when he had grown up, refused to be known as the son of Pharaoh's daughter. He chose to be mistreated along with the people of God rather than to enjoy the fleeting pleasures of sin. He regarded disgrace for the sake of Christ as of greater value than the treasures of Egypt, because he was looking ahead to his reward."
:::

He was looking ahead to his reward. That isn't a man who didn't care about rewards. That's a man who cared about them so much he refused to settle for a small one, and the Bible calls his refusal faith.

## What the reward does to a Tuesday

I want to be practical here, because this mark can sound like theology when it's actually the most practical thing in the book. Here is what happens to a man who really does expect the greater reward.

He can do good that nobody sees and never need to mention it, which means he can be generous with his money without anyone knowing, patient with his kids when no one's watching, and faithful in a hotel room a thousand miles from anyone who'd find out.

He can lose an argument. If your reward is being right, every argument in your house is a matter of life and death. If your reward is the Father's "well done," you can let your wife have the last word and go to bed at peace, and He counts it.

He can stay, in a hard marriage or a hard job or a hard church or a hard season with a hard father. Men leave when the local scoreboard says they're losing. A man with his eyes on a different scoreboard can stay through a losing decade because he knows the game isn't over.

He can take the low place. He can hold the baby while the other men watch the game. He can sit with the old man nobody visits. He can coach the team that's going to go two and nine.

He can forgive, which is expensive, because it means giving up the reward of seeing someone pay. A man who's expecting a greater reward can afford to give that one up, because he was never counting on it for his happiness.

And he can plant pecan trees at seventy-one.

## What it isn't

I have to say this, because some men have been sold a counterfeit and it has cost them their faith.

The greater reward is not a guarantee that this life goes well. It isn't health, and it isn't wealth, and it isn't a bigger truck. Moses, after everything, died on a mountain looking at a land he never entered. Paul, who wrote more about the reward than anyone, wrote a good deal of it from a jail cell. Jesus, who lived every second for the joy set before Him, got a cross.

So if you're expecting God's reward to arrive as things working out on your schedule, you'll be disappointed, and you'll be tempted to quit, and I've watched men do exactly that. The reward is real and it's coming, and some of it you'll taste here: peace you can't explain, a marriage that heals, a son who comes back. But most of it is stored where the weather can't reach it, and you're going to have to trust Him with the timing.

:::margin
2 Corinthians 4:17-18 (NIV)
"For our light and momentary troubles are achieving for us an eternal glory that far outweighs them all. So we fix our eyes not on what is seen, but on what is unseen, since what is seen is temporary, but what is unseen is eternal."
:::

Achieving is the word to hold onto. Your troubles are producing something. They aren't wasted, and that's why you can fix your eyes past them.

## Where the small rewards find you

The small rewards come for men differently in different seasons, and I want you to see which one is calling loudest in yours.

If you're young and single, the reward you're most tempted by is being wanted, and the second is being impressive, and both of them are loud. I'm not asking you to stop wanting a wife or a good career. I'm asking you to check, on a Tuesday, who you're performing for. If you'd stop doing the right thing tomorrow because no woman ever noticed, you aren't living for the greater reward yet.

If there's a baby in your house, nobody's clapping, and nobody's going to clap for a while. Your wife is too tired, your friends can't see it, and your own parents think they did it harder. This is the season where the fourth mark stops being an idea and becomes the only thing that gets you up at three. He sees the three o'clock, and He counts it.

If you're divorced, you may be waiting for vindication. You want people to finally understand what happened, and her to admit her part, and your kids to see it clearly. That may never come on this side, and I'd ask you to let it go as your reward, because it's too small for you anyway. The Father who sees in secret knows exactly what happened in that house, and He's the only judge whose verdict will last.

And if you're older, watch out for the reward of being remembered. It comes for good men late and it's subtle. You'll want your kids to be grateful, and the town to know what you built, and the church to name a room after you. None of that is wrong to want, and all of it is too small to live for. Mr. Pruitt didn't put a sign on the orchard. He didn't need one. He knew who was watching him dig.

One more man belongs in this section, because he's the one a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A man in his forties is most tempted by the scoreboard and by being right, which is the reward Anthony had to lay down in his kitchen. He's measuring himself against every man at church and every argument in his house, and every comparison is a small reward cashed early. The takeaway for him is that a man who can lose an argument and sleep well has found a better reward than winning.

## How to expect it

Think about it on purpose. Paul said fix your eyes, and fixing takes deciding. Once a day, in the truck or on the porch, tell yourself the truth: what I did today that nobody saw, He saw. Say it until you believe it.

Do one secret good thing a week. Give something, fix something, pray for someone, serve someone, and tell nobody, not even your wife, not even your brother. Let it stay between you and the Father. You'll be surprised how it recalibrates you.

Rehearse the sentence you're hoping to hear: well done, good and faithful servant. Imagine hearing it, not in a proud way but the way a boy imagines his father watching from the stands. Let it pull you.

Notice when you're performing. Every time you catch yourself telling a story so someone will think well of you, that's a small reward you just cashed early. Jesus said men who do that have already received their reward in full, and He meant that's all they get. Don't spend it here.

And pray with heaven in view. When you pray, don't only ask for the week to go well. Ask for grace to be faithful whether it does or not. Ask for eyes that see what's unseen. Ask to want the right reward, because wanting it is itself a gift.

## The whole picture

So here's a man with all four marks on him. He's off the tailgate. He's set at the corner post. He's the first door open. And he's doing all of it for a Father who sees in secret and rewards in the open, on a day that hasn't come yet.

That's a steadfast man. Not a man who never fails; there's no such man. A man who knows what he's working for and who he's working for, and who doesn't need the local scoreboard to tell him how he's doing.

Mr. Pruitt's trees are about knee-high on me now. They'll bear in another eight or ten years, and he won't be here for it. Somebody will be, standing under a tree they didn't plant and eating a harvest they didn't earn, and if they think about it at all they'll think about an old man who spent his last strength on people he would never meet.

He wasn't spending it on them, though. He was spending it on the One who was watching him dig.

Live for a reward that lasts. Then get back to work.

## Before you turn the page

I know this chapter asks you to want something you can't see, and I know how hard that is on a Tuesday when the visible rewards are right there. So I'll leave you with the thing that has kept me at it. Every good thing you've done that nobody saw, He saw. Every one. He isn't indifferent to any of it, and He isn't going to lose track, and the day is coming when He'll say the sentence you've been hoping to hear from someone your whole life. May the Father fix your eyes on what's unseen, and may He let you taste, even this week, a little of the joy that's set before you.
