# 3. The Four Marks

Last chapter we spread the maps out on the hood and watched them argue with each other. That leaves you standing in the lot with a sharper question than the one you drove in with. All right, then. What is a man?

I want to hand you the answer as one sentence that fits in a shirt pocket. We'll open it up afterward, a piece at a time, at the size of an ordinary week. But a picture first, because a sentence without one goes right back on the shelf.

## What a mark is

Walk into a shop where a man has worked the same bench for twenty years and the room tells you about him before he does. There's a groove worn into the wood where the same cut has been made a few thousand times. There's a brown ring where his coffee cup always lands. The wrench hooks sit right where his hand already reaches. My own bench has a ring like that from a mug I reheated twice and still walked off without. Wanting to be a craftsman didn't cut those grooves. A Saturday class didn't either. Work did, on a long run of days that all looked about the same.

That's a mark. Thinking well of yourself leaves nothing anybody can see. Neither does a title on a business card. Work, kept up for years, presses something into a man that shows. The fire you feel at six in the morning is mostly gone by lunch. The mark comes from the hour you walk back in the door.

So here's the sentence. It's how this book defines a man, and it's ours to carry.

**A steadfast man stops waiting, picks up the weight, goes first, and works for God's pay, not the room's.**

Read it again, slower. Four marks, and every one of them is something you do. Keep doing them and they stop being words on a page. They become things people can see on you, plain as the groove in that bench.

Paul gave the church one line about where all of it starts.

:::margin
**Philippians 2:5 NIV**
In your relationships with one another, have the same mindset as Christ Jesus:
:::

The mind of Christ, worn by an ordinary man, looks like those four marks. In his house. With his crew. On a week where nothing special happens.

## Stop Waiting

Every town has a tailgate with a decent man sitting on it. He'll fill you in on the weather, the school board, and the city water while the job he drove out there to do sits behind him in the truck bed. It's not the right time. Or the right price. Or he's not in the mood. He's waited so long he's started to trust the waiting.

Left alone, a man drifts toward waiting, and it rarely looks lazy. It looks like patience, or keeping the peace. Its favorite line is "I didn't want to overstep," and the man saying it usually thinks he's being humble. I've said it in my own house. The first man in the Bible did his own version. The serpent slid into the garden and started bending the one command God had given, and the man stood right there next to the woman and said nothing. The world broke on his silence.

James doesn't leave much room to hide.

:::margin
**James 4:17 NIV**
If anyone, then, knows the good they ought to do and doesn't do it, it is sin for them.
:::

Stop Waiting isn't about being loud or pushing people around. It's about moving. When it's yours to do, start now, before you feel like it. Get down off the tailgate. Open the hard conversation while it's still yours to open. The chapter called **Stop Waiting** stays here until you can spot it in the week you're actually living.

## Pick Up the Weight

One August I helped a friend move a gun safe, four hundred pounds of it, down a hallway that barely cleared our shoulders, in air thick enough to lean on. Lift something like that and there comes a moment when backing out is gone. You've both got it off the floor. There's nowhere in that hallway to set it down. Halfway along, you don't get to announce that the safe is somebody else's problem. You finish the carry.

Paul says it in eight words.

:::margin
**Galatians 6:5 NIV**
for each one should carry their own load.
:::

A boy handles weight by waiting to be assigned something and then arguing that it isn't fair. A man looks at what's in front of him and says, "That's mine." Somebody else may have dropped it. Nobody may be blaming him. He picks it up anyway. The stack of unopened bills. The kid nobody in the house is talking to. The apology he still owes. Carry what's yours, even when somebody else dropped it. A load on a young man's shoulders is a gift, because carrying is what makes the carrier. **Pick Up the Weight** shows you how to take what's yours and put down what was never handed to you.

## Go First

Picture a stock tank on a cold morning and a row of boys on the bank. The water is deeper and colder than any of them guessed, and every one of them is waiting on somebody else. The boy who goes in first is the one leading, because now the rest know which way to go. He didn't need a title. He needed wet boots.

Jesus described Himself that way.

:::margin
**John 10:4 NIV**
When he has brought out all his own, he goes on ahead of them, and his sheep follow him because they know his voice.
:::

Homes and crews and churches stall until somebody moves. Three days of silence in a marriage, and somebody has to say "I was wrong" before anybody else will. Somebody has to pray out loud at a table that's never heard it. A drifting brother needs somebody to make the first call. I've held back on every one of those and called it being careful. Go First means you step out ahead of your people, scared or not. The fear can stay in your chest while your boots go on. **Go First** opens that up.

## Work for God's Pay

This last mark is where good men and godly men part ways. I put it last because it's the one men step right over.

A pecan tree keeps its own clock. Plant one this fall and you'll water it for years before it gives you a nut worth cracking. Pecan orchards have always been gifts to people who show up later. The man with the shovel is spending his back on a harvest he may never hold.

Paul wrote this to people doing hard work for masters who never thanked them.

:::margin
**Colossians 3:23-24 NIV**
Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord, not for human masters, since you know that you will receive an inheritance from the Lord as a reward. It is the Lord Christ you are serving.
:::

Notice the word. Inheritance. That's what God's pay is. It isn't a wage, and it isn't a deal where you work hard enough and God lets you into the family. Nobody earns an inheritance. You get one because you're a son, and in Christ you're a son by grace before you've lifted a finger. The pay is the Father's gift to His children, and the best of it is the Father Himself saying "well done." Work for God's Pay means you do it for the Lord's reward, not the room's applause.

That changes ordinary days more than anything else in this book. Obedience nobody sees becomes possible. So does a hard decade in a marriage, and money you'll never get back, and hours nobody thanks you for, because a Father sees what's hidden and keeps the record. Look to Him for nothing and you'll go shopping somewhere else. A crowd. A bigger number. A newer truck. A father who finally says it. **Live for a Reward That Lasts** follows that hunt.

## Why these four

Why four, and why in this order? Because they're one life seen from four sides, and each one leans on the one before it.

You can't carry what you never started, so the first mark comes first. You can't lead anybody while you refuse your own load, because people follow a man with his hands on something, not a man explaining why it isn't his. And you won't keep going first, Tuesday after Tuesday, when it costs you and nobody notices, unless you know who's paying and what the pay is. Take away the last one and the first three are a list of good habits that decent men with no faith can manage for a while. The fourth ties the other three to Christ, and it keeps a man going after the applause stops. No fifth. Four is plenty to spend a life on.

The seasons will shift under your feet. The marks won't. A young man can get serious now, without a wife to force it. A young father in the noise can turn toward a worn-out wife and actually see her. A man in his middle years can catch himself drifting toward applause and point the truck home. An older man with nothing left to pin on his chest can still get up and find the work. Four marks. Another Tuesday.

## Before you turn the page

The sentence is yours now. This week, don't try to work all four. Shelve three and stay with the one that's faintest on you. Every man I've watched change did it one mark at a time, on one plain day, and then did the same mark again the next day. May the Father who made you in His image show you which mark He wants to press in first, and may His patience with your slow start outlast it, the way it has outlasted mine.
