# 8. The Empty Chair

The boy had owned his learner's permit for about three hours. On a quiet Saturday, Curtis drove him to the empty high school lot, got out, walked around, and lowered himself into the passenger side of his own car. He had no idea where to put his hands.

His son was good. A year of watching from that seat had taught him plenty. He eased into every stop and circled the light poles twice, slow and careful. Curtis gripped the door handle until his knuckles went pale, and his left foot kept stamping the mat for a brake that wasn't there. Every touch of the gas pulled a tight "easy" out of him in a voice he'd never have claimed. Halfway through the third lap, his son stopped, shifted into park, and said, "Dad. You're doing the thing."

That night, alone at the sink with the house asleep, Curtis finally saw it. When he was sixteen, it was his mom in that right-hand seat, both palms pressed against the dash, scared right along with him. His dad had left years before, and there was no other man to ask. Curtis had never seen a calm father beside a boy at the wheel. All he'd been handed was a door handle and a single word, and even the word came out crooked.

Before the hard part, know where this road ends, because some of you won't make it through the middle otherwise. There's a Father who doesn't leave. The next chapter belongs to him. For now, hold on to how the psalm names him.

:::margin
**Psalm 68:5-6 NIV**
A father to the fatherless, a defender of widows, is God in his holy dwelling. God sets the lonely in families, he leads out the prisoners with singing; but the rebellious live in a sun-scorched land.
:::

That's who's waiting at the end of this. Now let's look at the chair honestly.

## The first classroom

Every man learned about manhood long before anyone gave a talk on it. The classroom was one seat at the end of a kitchen table, filled or empty. You were taking notes before you could spell. When he got home. What his silence was saying. Whether his hands could be trusted. Whether your mom's words reached him at all. Whether Saturday's promise survived to Saturday. You'd graduated from that school before you were trusted with a key to the front door.

My aim in this chapter is to help you separate two piles. One pile was set on you. The other is now yours to answer for.

Some of you grew up around a vacant seat. Divorce, a funeral, a prison sentence, or a man who drove off and never explained. His absence still taught. Men go. When Mom is exhausted, the boy fixes things. You figure life out alone.

Some of you had a man sitting there who never really showed up. His job swallowed him, or the bottle did, or the TV got what you never got. Ask what he thought of you and you hit a wall. Relatives make allowances for him. I won't. That wound cuts as deep as a packed bag at the door, and it hides better, because the neighbors saw him come home every night and figured you had it fine.

Some of you had a chair you feared. The way the keys hit the counter told you what kind of evening it would be. You learned to shrink, or to pick the fight yourself so the storm would land on you instead of your mom.

Some of you had a good, steady father. Thank God, and keep it. Even good men have sharp corners, and you carried a few out of the house. Nobody gets through unmarked, though some of you will need a while to find yours.

## What sits down in the empty place

No boy can live around a hole that big. Something takes the seat, and whatever takes it does the rest of the raising.

Often the boy takes it. He fathers himself, sets his own rules, coaches himself through the fear until it's small enough to carry. Some genuine toughness comes from that. So does a man nobody can father anymore, including God. He's carried the load solo for so long that a second pair of hands feels like drowning.

For other boys, a rule takes the seat. Need people and they'll hurt you. So he quits needing, and grows into a husband who never asks for a thing. His wife could spend two decades beside him and not know what he longs for. He'll tell you he longs for nothing, and mean it.

Some boys give the seat to whoever's in the room. He knows how everybody feels. He has no clue how he feels.

Some hand it to an audience. He earns scraps of approval with a report card, a punch line, a curveball. The beard's going gray, the show is still running, and he couldn't say who he's playing to.

And for some, what fills the seat is the man he promised never to be. Curtis made that promise as a kid. No child of his would be left, and none has been. That's good, and I'd never take it from him. But that promise is the whole of his idea of a dad. He'd memorized everything not to do. Nobody had shown him how to sit still in the passenger seat while his son figured it out. The door handle was all he knew to grab.

## Yours to answer for

You didn't choose who was in that chair. Nobody hands a seven-year-old a ballot on whether his father stays. It was done to you, and it formed you. God saw all of it. He keeps an honest record, and it does matter to him.

What you do with it from today on, though, is yours. When Curtis snaps at his kids, the snap has the shape of a missing father, and it's still his snap. His wife meets it in a real kitchen. She never met the years that left. I've mistaken a good diagram of my wound for a healed one. Plenty of men describe their damage perfectly and then settle in to live there, because describing started to feel like getting somewhere.

Scripture is straight with men like this. Joseph had a father who loved him, and the pit happened anyway. David was out with the flock when the prophet came to look at Jesse's sons. Jesus was raised by a man who wasn't His father, and Joseph quietly exits before Jesus' public years. Each carried a mark from the chair. None was excused by it. That's a kind of honor. God treats you as a man who can respond to what happened to you. He's talking to the right man.

## What to do with it

Say it first. I've asked grown men about their dads, big men who fill a doorway, and watched them freeze. "I don't really think about him." That answer is the thinking. Say your sentence once, out loud, in your own hearing. My dad left. My dad was home and I never knew him. My dad frightened me. My dad was a good man, and some part of me never reached him.

Then grieve it. Men dodge this one every chance they get. Grief lets a loss be its true size. A seat that stayed empty. A shoulder you never leaned on. Words a fifteen-year-old needed, words some of you are still waiting for. Give it the weight you'd give a funeral. Managing it won't do your grieving for you. I keep thinking of David, the son his own father didn't bother to call in from the field, and the line he wrote without any polish on it.

:::margin
**Psalm 27:10 NIV**
Though my father and mother forsake me, the LORD will receive me.
:::

He doesn't act like nobody left. He names the hurt, and he names who took him in.

Then let the empty chair be your first word about fathers, never your last. That's where the Father from the start of this chapter comes in. If the chair gets the final say, you've believed a lie, and the next pages are here to put the truth back in the seat.

## It comes with you

The chair doesn't stay put in one house, and it doesn't age out.

Maybe your father's alive and still absent. Don't wait for his permission before you live like a man. Put your weight on the Father who's already here.

If you're young and single, don't shelve this for later. Somebody's already watching the seat you sit in: a kid brother, a nephew, the new hire.

The first time you hold your own son, the chair returns as a catch in your throat. What if this comes through me into him? That fear means you're awake. The men who repeat it are the ones who never turned to face it.

If you're divorced, I'll say it plainly and leave it alone. You are somebody's empty chair now. Being there is how you begin to answer that. The kid exchange. The ballgame. The call you keep making, through months when all you hear back is "nothing."

If you've built a big career, a corner office doesn't fill a chair. Men keep trying anyway. And if you're sixty with the seat still empty, it isn't too late to name it.

Sometimes a death closes the conversation before it opened. I'm sorry. Say it to God, who knew your father as you never could, and to a brother.

## The next Saturday

The two of them went back to the lot the following weekend. Before his son pulled out, Curtis told him the truth. "Nobody ever sat in this seat for me. I'm learning it while you learn this." He let go of the door handle, rested his hand on his knee, and kept it there. The boy swung wide on the first corner. Curtis said, "You've got it." It was the first fatherly thing he'd ever said on purpose.

Years from now, the boy won't remember the laps. He'll remember who stayed beside him.

The end of your old table was handed to you. The seats you hold now are your choice. Take them and stay, so your kids study a different man than you did. And step out of the job of fathering yourself long enough for a real Father to get to you. There was more in the seat next to you than you were ever taught to notice.

## Before you turn the page

Bring God the chair exactly as it was. No touch-ups. May the One who sets the lonely in families bring you into his. And tonight, at your own table, take your seat and stay in it.
