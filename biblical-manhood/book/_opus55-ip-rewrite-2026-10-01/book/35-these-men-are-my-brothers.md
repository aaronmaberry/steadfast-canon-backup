# 35. These Men Are My Brothers

Overcast today, a breeze, nothing on the calendar, the kind of day nobody's journal remembers. That's why I chose it. Before I let you go, I want you to know what became of the men you've met in these pages.

None of this is meant to wow you. They aren't famous. You'd stand behind any of them in line at a gas station and never give him a second glance. That's my whole case: the four marks were made for plain men on plain days, in every season, and I've seen them hold.

## Born for a hard day

I've used the word brother since chapter one, and I've meant it. These men and I are in the same work. We've said hard, true things over coffee, in truck cabs, on nine o'clock phone calls. We've walked fence before sunup, prayed over each other's boys, buried wives, rocked newborns, and dialed each other with nothing useful to say. Solomon put the whole thing in one proverb.

:::margin
**Proverbs 17:17 NIV**
A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for a time of adversity.
:::

That's no hunting buddy, and it's not a name lost somewhere in three hundred phone contacts. A brother is the man who picks up. Every man below has one now, and most had none the day I met them. Of everything that changed them, that changed them most.

## Where they are tonight

Mr. Alvarez bathed his wife this morning. Her mind has let go of his name. He hasn't let go of hers. He promised "in sickness" in 1968, and he's keeping all of it.

Mr. Pruitt, seventy-six, has pecans above his knee now. Ed came for coffee this morning, a Wednesday, same as every week for four years, the best twenty minutes of the old man's week. He won't live to eat from those trees. No man in the county is more at rest.

Hollis, seventy-four, wrote tomorrow's three things before bed. One reads "Call Roy re: roof."

Cole is thirty-seven and done waiting. Every night his hand rests on his boy's head for a short blessing and the boy's name. Sunday he and his dad stood over a well pump at the home place and barely spoke. That silence is the nearest his father gets to a hug, and Cole took it.

Anthony made it into the kitchen tonight. His daughter glanced up from her homework to read his face when her mom came in, and his face got it right. He still prays "Father" in the truck each morning, and the word still feels clumsy.

Walt was home for supper. Part of the business is sold, and a fall trip is booked, her pick. He has a brother now. He still remembers that bank parking lot: three hundred contacts, nobody to call.

Micah's married. A younger couple ate at their table tonight. Dale still buys him breakfast every other week, and a twenty-four-year-old gets Micah's Thursdays. He wrote the date in his Bible the day a man told him to quit asking permission.

Kenny's kids are with their mom this week. It's Tuesday, though, so at 7:30 he called his son and got twenty minutes on the smoker they built over eleven weekends. Three years back Kenny was a ghost in a one-bedroom apartment, until an older man called every week for a year and actually waited to hear how he was. Now divorced men at church seek Kenny out. What he thought disqualified him is what God is using.

Tomás, twenty-nine, walked his two-year-old around the block at six. His wife slept five straight hours because he took first shift. They learned trust in their worst year. Most days he doesn't feel like a man. He is one.

And Roy, seventy-two. Coffee at 5:30, the porch, his wife telling her dream, a chapter of Proverbs read aloud. Then off to look at a church roof leak nobody asked him about. Five years ago he turned in his badge and thought he was finished. He'll tell you these five years mattered most. Nobody's clapped once.

## What they share

Go back over that list. Single, married, divorced, widowed. A crib in one house, an empty bedroom in another. Retired. Sick. You won't find a remarkable man in the bunch, which is my point. Each of them, in his season, on his ordinary days, is living the four marks.

Nobody's sitting on the tailgate. Roy hunts up work. Tomás leaned into his marriage in the middle of the mess. Walt let trucks go so he could make supper, and Mr. Alvarez lifts a washcloth at dawn, carrying a load nobody would blame him for setting down. Anthony led with the apology. Hollis walked across the lot to a stranger by a truck. And every one of them is working for an audience of One, the Father who sees in secret. That's Work for God's Pay, and it's what keeps the rest standing. Not one of them is trying to earn heaven. What they're working toward is an inheritance, given by grace to sons the Father already calls His own, never wages that buy salvation.

:::margin
**Psalm 112:7 NIV**
They will have no fear of bad news; their hearts are steadfast, trusting in the LORD.
:::

That word is the title of this book. Steadfast, on a man, is a heart lashed to something that won't shift. Bad news lands and he holds together. He reads the gauge, tells God and his brother the truth, and grips the wheel again.

## The door's open

Nobody here showed up finished, and this was never a club for men who have it handled. Walt came from a parking lot, Roy from a recliner, Anthony from a doorway, Kenny from an apartment where he'd been haunting his own life. Someone asked, or they finally asked. The door stood open for each of them. It's open for you.

:::margin
**Hebrews 10:24 NIV**
And let us consider how we may spur one another on toward love and good deeds,
:::

Spurring takes somebody close enough to reach you, and a crowd never is. If you've read this far, named your thing, taken the step, and opened your mouth to a brother, you're in this company whether we ever meet. Behind us, a garden. Ahead, a table still waiting on its plates. Plain men, made steadfast, held together by the same four marks.

## Tomorrow is ordinary

Your life won't be settled at an altar, a fence line, or the last page of a book. It'll be settled on days like this one, overcast and breezy, nobody watching, nothing seemingly riding on it. Which is exactly why everything is.

Tomorrow will be another. A smoke alarm with a dying battery is chirping in somebody's hallway, maybe yours. Your wife wants to be known. Your kid needs a name and a hill to climb. Your own dad is waiting on a call. Some man you know needs you to ask. And the Father is standing out on the road, watching for you to come over the rise. You know what to do. What's left is getting up and doing it.

## Once more

Once more, and then I'll step aside. **A steadfast man stops waiting, picks up the weight, goes first, and works for God's pay, not the room's.**

Those four show up on a man like the callus a fence stretcher left on my grandfather's hand. Nobody grows them by gritting his teeth. They're the family resemblance, and it comes from the Son who already did everything this book has asked of you, and did it for you. Live even one of them on a plain day and somebody is going to notice the likeness.

You will fall. What matters is whose man you're standing in, the first Adam's or the second's. Stand in the second and there's one question left, which is tomorrow morning. Get up. Turn back toward a Father who has never once walked off. Then do it again, until the word you hear is "well done."

:::margin
**1 Corinthians 15:58 NIV**
Therefore, my dear brothers and sisters, stand firm. Let nothing move you. Always give yourselves fully to the work of the Lord, because you know that your labor in the Lord is not in vain.
:::

Not in vain. He saw every slab, every pot of coffee, every call, every pecan tree. It's held with Him and won't fade, because what He's keeping for you is a son's inheritance, not a laborer's wage.

## The open chair

Israel's priests didn't send people home without a word from God over them. Neither will I.

:::margin
**Numbers 6:24-26 NIV**
The LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face shine on you and be gracious to you; the LORD turn his face toward you and give you peace.
:::

Keep was Adam's second job in the garden, guarding the ground he'd been handed. Here the word turns around and lands on the man: the One he answers to is guarding him. So work your ground, and keep it. These men are my brothers. There's an open chair at the table. Pull it out and sit.

## Before you close the book

You've made it to the end, with a written page, a man you asked, and one mark already in your hands. The Father who watches in secret missed none of it. May He guard you the way He told Adam to guard Eden. May His face shine on your plain days. And when we finally sit down at a table still unset, may you discover you had brothers all along.
