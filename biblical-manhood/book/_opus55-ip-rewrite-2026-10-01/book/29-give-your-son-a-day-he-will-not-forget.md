# 29. Give Your Son a Day He Will Not Forget

There's a boy I know who keeps a brass plumb bob in his pocket. It's small and surprisingly heavy, with his name and a date cut into the side. His dad says he'll pull it out in a grocery store parking lot for no reason, just to hold it. A few miles from his house, on a gravel bar under a county bridge, sits a little pile of creek rocks nobody has moved. Some afternoons he rides his bike down there to look at them.

I was there the morning both of those things happened. Before I tell you about it, here's why it matters.

## Why give him a day

Most of us never got one. We kept waiting for somebody to tell us we'd arrived, and the waiting quietly turned into a whole life. Your son doesn't have to wait like that.

God has always given His people things to bump into. When Israel crossed the Jordan, Joshua had men haul stones out of the riverbed and stack them on the bank, and he said why.

:::margin
**Joshua 4:6-7 NIV**
to serve as a sign among you. In the future, when your children ask you, "What do these stones mean?" tell them that the flow of the Jordan was cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD. When it crossed the Jordan, the waters of the Jordan were cut off. These stones are to be a memorial to the people of Israel forever.
:::

A pile of rocks built to start a conversation with a kid who hadn't been born yet. Passover had a child's question built right into the meal. And Jesus heard His Father speak over Him, out loud, at a river, before His work began.

Most Christian homes have nothing like this for a son. Birthdays, graduation, sure. But no morning when the men in his life look at him and say plainly, you're a man now, and you're one of us. So he grabs a marker from whatever's lying around. A girlfriend. A drunk night. A fight. Hand him the real thing instead.

## The creek

His voice had started to crack that year, and he'd taken to studying grown men the way younger boys study trucks. His dad told him to be dressed by five, and wouldn't say why. We drove out in the dark and parked by the bridge where he'd been baptized two summers before. Nobody talked on the walk down.

Five of us stepped into the creek. Alton, his grandfather, went in ahead. His dad. His dad's brother. The friend his dad meets with every week over coffee. And me. The water barely covered our ankles and it was cold enough to knock the air out of you. Each of us bent, came up with a stone, waded back, and set it at the boy's feet.

Alton read the Joshua passage. Then the men talked, one at a time. His uncle told him the worst thing he'd ever done, and what he did the morning after. When it came around to his dad, he laid his hand on the boy's shoulder and said the name he'd been saying over him since the hospital: my son. Then something new. "You're becoming a man. That's what this morning is about." Then what he meant by it, in his own plain words. "A man does what's his to do, even on the days nobody's looking. That's most of it." He'd written it all out by hand on a card so the boy could keep it.

Alton reached into his coat, came out with the plumb bob, put it in the boy's palm, and told him what it was for.

## A line that doesn't lie

Alton had hung a plumb bob against every wall of the house the boy's father grew up in. Here's what he told his grandson.

You let it hang beside whatever you're building, and you wait. It swings a while. Then it settles, and when it settles it points straight down, every time. It doesn't care what you think straight is. Gravity doesn't take suggestions. Crooked work can look fine, he said, when everything around it is crooked too. You need something outside yourself to check against. God gave the prophet Amos that exact picture: the Lord standing beside a wall with a plumb line in His hand, measuring His own people.

Scripture is the line that holds still. Hang your life beside it, wait out the swing, and look honestly at the gap. And the weight is on purpose, Alton told him. A light bob drifts in any breeze. A heavy one settles. A man is supposed to be the heavy kind, hard to push off true. The name on the side is for the day he forgets who he is.

His dad says it's already come out of that pocket a few times, once over going along with the crowd at school, once over the truth about a grade. Your boy's keepsake doesn't have to be brass. A Bible with the date inside, a great-grandfather's watch, a good tool. Pick weight over price, and say what it means when you hand it over.

## Putting the morning together

Watch for the turn in him: the voice, the shoulders, the way he's started watching men. That tells you when. If you've already missed it, give him the morning anyway.

Keep it small. You, and a few men he already looks up to who'll still be around long after breakfast. A crowd turns everybody into a performer. Don't have those men yet? Start looking now.

Go outside, early, somewhere that means something. His baptism creek. The hill he climbed. Make him work a little to get there.

Tell him something's coming, not what. Meet with the men once ahead of time. Ask each to bring something true, five minutes at most: what being a man has meant in his life, something he got wrong and what came after, and a charge for this boy.

When the morning comes, show up quietly and let it land on him before anybody speaks. Then somebody reads. I keep coming back to one short verse.

:::margin
**1 Corinthians 16:13 NIV**
Be on your guard; stand firm in the faith; be courageous; be strong.
:::

Then the men take turns, and his father goes last. Hand on his shoulder, he says the name the boy already knows, then the new word, then what a man is in words that sound like him and nobody else. If he chokes up, let it show. A boy who watches his dad cry while blessing him learns what no program could teach him. Then the keepsake. Then every man lays a hand on him and prays, his dad last. Then one sentence: you're one of us. Then breakfast, and every man at that table talks to him like a man. He'll hear the difference.

## Every kind of house

Divorced? You can still do this, and you should. Tell his mom what you're aiming for and keep it about the boy. A good stepdad might be worth inviting. If she pushes back, use your own time and your own men, and keep the old fight out of that morning. Single? You can be one of the men at another boy's creek, or gather a few for a boy whose mom would be grateful. Talk to her before you plan anything. Granddads, offer. You've got standing, maybe a keepsake in a drawer, and the years to make the weeks afterward real. And daughters deserve a marked day too, shaped differently: her mom and grandmother there, supper instead of sunrise, who she is and what she's worth and how a man ought to treat her. Mark your son and leave your daughter guessing, and you've only done half.

## The Monday after

The morning doesn't finish anything. I've watched a family pour everything into one beautiful day and go right back to treating him like a little kid by Monday. What makes it true is how he's treated afterward. Bigger hills. His opinion asked for and then actually used. Grown-up work, grown-up correction, a grown man's standard, and mercy when he blows it. The day marks the spot. The weeks after it do the handing over.

Alton worked for the county most of his life. He says his retirement dinner was the best night of his working life, and the morning after was the worst. Applause, then nobody needing him. So the very next week he called his grandson to come help pour a footing. The creek told the boy he was a man. The footing started making him one.

## If no one gave you one

Maybe nobody ever did this for you, and you can feel it in your throat. You may think you can't give what you never got. That's fair to feel, and it's wrong. Giving it heals the same place receiving it would have. I've watched fathers who never heard a word from their own dads stand by the water, come apart halfway through blessing their boys, finish anyway, and drive home changed. Want it closed out? Afterward, ask a man further down the road to lay a hand on you and say it. You're a man. You did right by that boy. It isn't too late for you.

## What do these stones mean

Someday a grandson will pick up that brass bob, or ask about the rocks under the bridge. The answer will be a gray morning, a cold creek and the men who waded it, a card in a father's handwriting, and the bob a grandfather hung beside every wall he built. And if we've done our part, whoever's telling it will go set up a morning for his own son.

The day is where it starts. Every week after it is the rest.

## Before you turn the page

Even if no one ever gave you a morning like this, there's a word I'd want said over you, and it isn't too late. May the Father who stacked stones beside a river give you the men, the morning, and the nerve to say the new word out loud. May your boy carry something heavy in his pocket that keeps reminding him whose he is.
