# 27. Give Your Son a Name

The hospital gives you a clipboard and a cheap pen on a chain. You fill in the lines while the baby sleeps through his own paperwork, and it feels like the big moment. It's the smallest one. The real naming starts once you get him home, and it runs for years, mostly in sentences you won't remember saying.

A boy grows toward whatever he's called. Call him trouble and he'll find ways to prove you right. Call him nothing and he'll go door to door until somebody fills in the blank for him. Plenty of people are lined up to do it. Most of them don't love him.

## Four seconds of video

Ray didn't think he had a naming problem. Then his wife pulled up a clip from their son's birthday. Candles, a pile of kids, everybody off-key, and under the noise, a little left of the camera, a voice Ray knew was his. "What is wrong with you, bud?"

He couldn't place the moment. That scared him more than the line did. A man forgets what he says on autopilot. On the screen his boy flinched, then his face went smooth again, like he'd filed it for later. Ray ran those four seconds back so many times he quit seeing the cake.

Most of us have a version of that, and it isn't always words. A long breath out through the nose. The look over the top of the phone. Men sit across from me at fifty and can still repeat the exact thing, and their voice goes quiet when they get to it.

He's getting named everywhere else too. Kids have handed out labels since recess was invented: slow, weird, scrawny, too much. And failure talks to him all day, because boys miss far more than they make. If no man interrupts that voice, he'll take its word for it. I'm just bad at stuff.

I'm not above any of this. I've got a sigh that comes out before I've decided anything, and I've watched it land on people.

## The name under all the others

Under everything you call him, there's one name, and it sounds different on different days.

Most days it sounds like belonging. You're mine. He hasn't earned it and he can't lose it, and you aren't leaving. Before Jesus had done one public thing, before a miracle or a sermon, a voice came down over the river.

:::margin
**Mark 1:11 NIV**
And a voice came from heaven: "You are my Son, whom I love; with you I am well pleased."
:::

The Father said whose He was before anybody had seen what He could do. A boy who's sure he's wanted can strike out, flunk, back the car into the mailbox, and still come home. A boy who isn't sure turns his whole life into a tryout.

Some days the name sounds like I see you. He sends a ball through the neighbor's window, then walks over and knocks on the neighbor's door. "That was honest. You're an honest guy." Now he's got a word for what he just did and something to grow into. Hear it often enough and lying starts to feel like a shoe on the wrong foot. Paul talked to his churches the way a good dad talks, comfort and push in the same hand. Push alone wears a boy out. Comfort alone leaves him soft. You're still mine after that mess. And I know there's more in you than that.

And at some point the name has to say man. Most of us never heard this one from our fathers. Your son needs you to tell him, at an age he'll remember, that he's becoming a man, and what you mean by it. Don't recite anything. Talk like you do on a long drive. Maybe, "A man doesn't sit around waiting to be told. He sees what's his and gets after it. And when he blows it, he owns it and fixes what he can." That's Stop Waiting in a dad's voice. The question is already in him. Am I there yet? A girlfriend, a fistfight, a paycheck, or a lifted truck will answer it if you don't. Get there first.

## Saying it until it sticks

You can't hand this over in one talk. It gets in by repetition.

Daily is the small stuff. He carries his grandmother's groceries without being asked, and you tell him, "That's what a man does." Five seconds. Over a few years they pile up into a floor.

Weekly is a blessing. Pick a night and protect it. Hand on his head, a few honest words in your own voice. It might come out, "You're my son, and I'm not going anywhere. I watched you own up to that grade on Wednesday, and I was proud of you. God made you for something, and I'm going to help you find it. Now get some sleep." You'll feel silly. Say it anyway. Moses told Israel's fathers to keep God's words in front of their kids at every hour.

:::margin
**Deuteronomy 6:6-7 NIV**
These commandments that I give you today are to be on your hearts. Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up.
:::

Impress is a stamp pushed into leather, again and again, until the shape holds.

Yearly is a letter. His birthday, one sheet, your handwriting. What he's like right now, what changed in him this year, where you think he's headed. Put it in a shoebox and keep going, and someday you'll hand him the box.

Brag on him in public. "My son helped me hang the gate Saturday. Stuck with it till dark." Keep the correcting behind a closed door, because shame in front of people turns into a label, and so does honor.

When he fails, pull apart what he did from who he is. Don't shrug off the lie. Call it what it was, then show him the way back. "You're my son, and that was a lie. My son goes and makes it right. Come on, I'll drive." Tell a boy he's a liar and he'll either fight the word for years or become it. Tell him he's honest and just did a dishonest thing, and he can see the road home.

Then point past yourself. Your name over him is good shade, but it isn't the tree. You'll let him down, and one day you'll be gone. Tell him God made him on purpose, and that the Father who never leaves claims him too.

Ray started late and awkward, with nobody to copy. Once he went looking for things to name, he found plenty. A few months in, his daughter dropped a lamp and burst into tears, and Ray heard his boy tell her, "You're not bad. You did a bad thing. Go tell Mom." The boy had started naming people himself.

## In every kind of house

A newborn can't follow the words, but he knows your voice. Leave the phone on the counter and say it over him: you're my son. Dads of infants often feel like spare parts. This is the job.

If you're divorced, your boy is hearing names in a house you don't run. Maybe a stepdad's. Maybe your silence. You can't fix the other address. You can own the Tuesday night call. Never use him to carry messages or report back on his mom. Go-between isn't a name he should have to wear. He's yours wherever he sleeps. Close every call the same way: you're my son, I love you, I'll call next week.

If your kids are grown, they still have ears. A grandson can hear "that's what a man does" from Grandpa and believe it in a way he won't from his worn-out dad. And if your grown son never got the word man from you, it isn't too late. Tell him you're proud of the man he is.

No son in your house? Look around the church or the ball field. Some boy there hears nothing from anybody. Catch him doing one thing right and say it where his friends can hear.

## His name over you

Everything you say over your boy is borrowed. God said it first, over His own sons, and He hasn't stopped saying it: you're Mine, and I'm glad you're home. He says it over every man in Christ, including you, whether your father ever got a sentence out or not.

:::margin
**Psalm 127:3 NIV**
Children are a heritage from the LORD, offspring a reward from him.
:::

Your son is a gift the Lord put at your kitchen table. He's asking who he is all day, mostly without words. You get to answer. Give him a name sturdy enough to hold him when he's old.

## Before you turn the page

Nobody named you? You can still name him. Ray didn't feel qualified the night he first put his hand on that boy's head. Putting it there is part of how Ray grew up. The sigh and the "bud" can be swapped tonight for a few plain words. May the Father who called Jesus beloved before He'd done anything give you the nerve to say the same over your son, and may your boy stand his whole life on a floor that won't give way.
