# 12. Leave Without Slamming the Door

Watch three grown sons walk up to the house they were raised in, and you'll learn most of what you need to know.

The first one doesn't bother knocking. He's got gray coming in on his chin and a mortgage on the other side of town, and he's got the fridge open before anybody says hello, while his wife is still standing out on the step. The second one hasn't come up those steps in years. He can still quote the argument that ended it, and he calls the distance freedom. The house still owns him. The third one might have a key on his ring. He knocks anyway, waits, and when it swings open he says, "Hey, Dad."

Only that third man has gone. The other two are stuck at the threshold, one gripping the knob and one with his back to it, and neither can see it. Knuckles on wood, then waiting: that's what God had in mind for a grown son.

He wrote half of it on stone at Sinai, where no son could miss it.

:::margin
**Exodus 20:12 NIV**
Honor your father and your mother, so that you may live long in the land the LORD your God is giving you.
:::

But the other half goes back further, to the first pages, and God says it about a grown son.

:::margin
**Genesis 2:24 NIV**
That is why a man leaves his father and mother and is united to his wife, and they become one flesh.
:::

Notice that the going comes before the wedding. A single guy is under this verse too. He doesn't need a ring to be told it's time.

Side by side, it's one motion with two hands. Slam it and you're out, but the honor got dropped on the porch. Grip the knob forever and you've kept something like honor, but you never went anywhere. God wants a son who does both at once. A knock says it without a speech: I live somewhere else now, and I'm glad you answered.

## The slam

Start with the man whose back is turned. Some of you got out of houses no kid should have grown up in. I won't send you back there, and I'll never point you toward someone who's still dangerous. But I've seen what years of a slam do to a man.

That door doesn't stay behind you. Rage is its own kind of attention. The dad you cut off rides along into every room, and pretty soon your whole life is arranged around not being him. You're more tied to him than any Sunday phone call would have made you. You just can't see the rope, because anger braided it.

Your kids are taking notes on how a son treats his dad. That's the playbook they'll grab later. Contempt you show now gets stored up for when you're the one in the recliner. Respect given to a father who never earned it teaches them something no pastor can.

## Honor without pretending

Honor goes to the office, not the report card. Scripture never asks you to call a bad dad good, and it never asks you to lie about him.

Picture a lieutenant saluting a colonel he can't stand. He salutes the eagles on the collar, and the salute is still clean.

With a hard dad, honor gets practical. You tell the truth about him without making him a joke. You help look after him when he's old, even if he never looked after you. And you pray, even if all that comes out is, "Lord, I don't even know what to ask for him. You know him."

Forgiveness lives in there too. What he did was real, and God knows exactly how heavy it was. Trust is a separate call. You can forgive him and still never leave your kids alone in his house. Some tables aren't safe, and some never will be. Forgiving means handing the debt to God and quitting the collection business. Your peace stops hanging on a sorry that may never come.

:::margin
**Romans 12:19 NIV**
Do not take revenge, my dear friends, but leave room for God's wrath, for it is written: "It is mine to avenge; I will repay," says the Lord.
:::

There's relief in that verse. Getting even was never your job.

Joseph's brothers threw him in a pit and sold him to strangers. Years later he held every card, and what he told them is the cleanest picture I know of this kind of forgiveness.

:::margin
**Genesis 50:20 NIV**
You intended to harm me, but God intended it for good to accomplish what is now being done, the saving of many lives.
:::

He didn't soften it. You meant this for evil. Then he pointed to what God had built out of it. He told the truth about the hurt, trusted God with the rest, and never slid an invoice across the table.

## The grip

Now the other man, who's sure all this is for somebody louder. If your mom's opinion still beats your wife's in your own kitchen, you're still holding the knob. If one remark from your dad can shrink you back to twelve, and you plan your choices to dodge it, you're holding the knob. Thirty-five, single, and every big decision still clears Sunday dinner? Same. Moving across town didn't change it.

Their advice is a gift your whole life, and a wise man keeps asking. But a man whose center of gravity sits in somebody else's kitchen never grows his own, and his kids need a dad whose direction starts with him.

You don't need a fight to do this well. Decide, then tell them. Ask first and you've handed it back. "I love you, and this is how we're doing it." If Mom has a comment about your wife, you stand in that gap, even on days she's partly right. Keep your voice low and your back straight.

## Grant's porch

Grant's father never once said anything kind to his son, and Grant had slammed that door hard years before. When he finally went back, the engine ticked in the driveway a long time before he got out. His dad opened up and didn't speak. Grant said what he'd come to say and then went quiet. There was no apology. Nothing about being proud of him. The old man stared out over the road at the pasture for a while. Then: "You eat yet?" He turned and headed in, and didn't shut the door. Grant went in after him. That supper, he told me, was about as close to a hug as his dad could manage. Grant was grateful for it. He'd quit asking the old man for what wasn't in him.

What did Grant say? It wasn't long. He named the one thing he'd needed and never gotten, in a single sentence, because one sentence lands and ten bounce off: "Dad, I always wanted you to be proud of me, and I never heard it." He thanked his dad for something true. Even a hard father usually hands down something, a work ethic, a roof that didn't leak, one afternoon on the lake, and gratitude is honor with its sleeves rolled up. He told him what he'd decided, and it came out as a decision, not a threat: "My kids are getting something different, and I'm letting this go." Then he held the door open from his side: "I'd like to know you, man to man, if you want that." He didn't make an apology the price of the offer. What his dad did with it was his dad's business.

If your father's alive and it's safe, there may be a porch like that waiting for you. Some men should leave it alone, because a few conversations only rip open what God is already closing. But a lot of men have stood out there for years without lifting a hand. And if he's gone, or too dangerous to go near, say it anyway. God hears it. So does a brother. It needs to come out of you even if it can't get to him.

## Again, and again

Leaving isn't one trip. The verse names a mother too, including one who raised you by herself, and under your roof your wife now comes ahead of both.

A single man swings between cutting his folks off and calling them before every decision; decide, tell them afterward, and let them hear love in it. A new dad leaves all over again when a newborn shows up and the grandparents show up with it, and he learns to say, where his mother can hear, "We're going with my wife on this one." Divorce makes the pull strongest, straight back to Mom's table, where her kindness can become his hiding place. She'll make room because she loves him. Building a life is still on him. A man in his fifties with a failing father can come home from every visit feeling twelve, and it's not too late for his porch either. And if you're the dad inside, whose boy stormed off or just faded out, be the father in Jesus' story who went out after both boys. Make the call he doesn't return. If you let him down, say so once: "I let you down when you needed me. I'm sorry." When he shows up, pull out a chair.

## Knock on the way home

This door asks something of both men. The one gripping the knob has waited years for letting go to feel right, and it won't, so he opens his hand anyway. The one who slammed it left behind the honor he owed. His dad dropped plenty, but that piece is still his to carry. That's Pick Up the Weight, on your parents' front steps. Neither one should wait for the other side to move. And both can quit holding out for his apology to make them whole. What you needed from a father, you already have from the One who adopted you.

He was your dad, whatever kind he was, and the way you treat him now shows on you. Go gently. Pull it closed so it barely clicks. Come back and knock. Then build a house with a door your boy can knock on someday.

## Before you turn the page

Be honest with God about your dad, and give Him what's owed. Honor the office as you go, and close the door quietly. May the Father who never walked out on you steady your steps, give you grace for whatever knock comes later, and let your kids learn from you, someday, how to go from your house well.
