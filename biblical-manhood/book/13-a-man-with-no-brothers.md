# 13. A Man With No Brothers

The form on the clipboard wanted two names.

Dale got to that line a little after two in the morning, under hospital lights. For three hours the pressure in his chest had felt like the end of everything, and then it turned out to be nothing serious. A nurse handed him the intake form and went to find him a gown. He wrote his wife's name on the first line. The second line asked for an emergency contact who wasn't a spouse. His pen hung over the paper. No name came.

His phone held hundreds of them. The crew from the county barn. Men he'd hunted with for twenty years. Men from church. A brother-in-law two states over. His pastor. Any one of them would have helped him move a couch. Not one of them was a man he could call at that hour and say, "I'm scared."

When he told me about it years later, his voice went flat. "I didn't know I didn't have anybody until I needed somebody."

## Most men have no brothers

The man who needs this chapter is usually sure it's for somebody else, so I'll say it plainly. Most men don't have brothers, not in the sense that matters. They have buddies, and a buddy is something else. He's the man next to you while something gets done. A hunt, a river, a round of golf, a game on a screen. And in a lot of marriages, one wife is carrying the whole weight of being the person who knows him. She was never built to carry that alone.

Ask a man a few questions. Who can call him at two in the morning, and who can he call? Who's allowed to tell him to his face that he's wrong? Who knows what he's actually afraid of? Then watch the quiet spread across the faces of good men with full phones. I've had a phone full of names and nobody to call myself.

We weren't built for this. Before sin ever entered the story, the first thing God called not good was a man by himself. A wife was the first answer He gave, and all through the rest of the story, the company of men stands beside her. David had Jonathan. Jesus had twelve, and three of them sat closer in.

:::margin
Ecclesiastes 4:9-10 (NIV)
"Two are better than one, because they have a good return for their labor: If either of them falls down, one can help the other up. But pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up."
:::

Pity the man who falls with nobody to help him up. Dale was that man, on a gurney, with a pen in his hand.

## Why we don't have them

I've asked a lot of men about that blank line. "I'm fine," they say. That's pride. Nobody is fine. "I don't have time." The time is there. Nobody has asked them for a piece small enough. "I don't want to be a burden." Under that, there's usually a boy who learned that needing someone was dangerous, and who grew into a man who would rather go under than reach out. "Men don't do this." Men did it for most of history. When they quit, they started dying quietly, and we called it independence. "I got burned." Some of them really did. One bad meal is a poor reason to quit eating.

"I have a wife." Keep her. Her calling is real, and it was never to be the only person who knows you. Over the years that weight bends a woman. Ask her if it has.

## What a brother is

A brother knows which exit you take when a day goes bad, whether it's anger, a screen, or going quiet. He asks, and you tell him the truth. No room is closed to his questions. Money. Your marriage. The bedroom. What crossed your eyes this week. Whether you've prayed. If you're in a hospital at two in the morning, he comes, because to him brother means getting in the truck. He tells you the truth back, with love, and he says it once. He doesn't water it down.

:::margin
Proverbs 27:17 (NIV)
"As iron sharpens iron, so one person sharpens another."
:::

Iron on iron throws sparks. Some of what he says will sting, and you'll walk away sharper.

He also helps you find God on the days you can't find Him yourself. David was on the run with a king hunting him, worn out and hiding in the woods, when a friend came looking for him.

:::margin
1 Samuel 23:16 (NIV)
"And Saul's son Jonathan went to David at Horesh and helped him find strength in God."
:::

Jonathan helped him find strength in God. He didn't bring any extra strength of his own, and he couldn't stop the hunt, because his own father was the hunter. He went into the woods anyway. That's the job.

You need one man like that, two at most. He might tell you to go see a counselor, and he'll still be your brother. And the man you enjoy most may be the wrong man to trust with the truth.

## Twenty minutes

Most men already want this. What they don't have is a picture small enough to fit the week they're actually living. The pictures they've been handed are too big. A six o'clock breakfast every week. A group with homework. A weekend away. A man with a newborn at home or a business going under looks at those, knows he can't do them, and does nothing.

Take a smaller picture. It's the one that finally worked for me. Twenty minutes. One man. Once a week, at the same time. The same time matters, because a meeting that floats from week to week dies.

Spend it on three questions and a prayer.

What happened this week? Skip the report. Name the two or three things that actually counted.

What's heavy? This is the question that earns the twenty minutes. Name the load, and name the fear under the week. You both tell the truth. The man listening has one job, and it's listening. Fixing things can wait for another day.

What's next? Each of you names one move before the next call and says it out loud, so another man hears it. Next week starts with whether it got done.

Save the last two minutes for prayer. Out loud, each of you for the other.

Some weeks one of you is a wreck, and his answer to what's heavy takes the whole twenty minutes. Weeks like that are why you set a floor. Twenty minutes, every week, the same hour. The floor doesn't move.

Twenty minutes survives tax season. It survives a twelve-hour shift, chemo, and grief. I've never met a man who couldn't find twenty minutes. I've met plenty who couldn't find two hours, and so they found nothing. The questions keep you on track. Without them, you'll be talking about trucks and football by minute eleven. Twenty minutes is enough to start.

## How to find him

Look for a man who is living the four marks, most of the time. A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward. He's probably already at your church or on your crew, and nobody has ever asked him a real question. He can be your age. Older is often better. Don't pick your boss, someone who works for you, or your brother-in-law. You have to be able to tell him the whole truth without a job or a Thanksgiving table riding on it.

Ask him in plain words. "I'm trying to be a better man, and I've realized I don't have a brother. Would you be willing to talk with me for twenty minutes a week for the next three months and see how it goes?" Three months makes it easy for him to say yes. It also makes it easy to stop if it doesn't work. Most of the time it holds, and by the end neither of you remembers you were allowed to quit.

It starts out awkward, and it gets easier. He's been waiting too. Men are lonely, and most of them won't go first. So you go first.

## Brothers in every season

The shape changes with the season. The need doesn't.

A single man with roommates may already have a brother and just need to add the twenty minutes. Or he may share a house with three men and still have no one who knows him. A father with a newborn has twenty minutes but not thirty, which is why the number is twenty. He can make the call with the dishwater running. When a marriage ends, friends often leave with it. They were mostly hers, or they didn't know what to do with him. Of everyone reading this, he needs one man the most. The big group can wait. Get one voice on the phone. Retirement, or the death of a wife, takes away the company that came built in with the job or with her. He still needs one man.

## The garden

On the night before He died, Jesus took three men with Him into a garden. He knew they would fall asleep. He knew one of them would deny Him before morning. He took them anyway, and He asked them for something.

:::margin
Mark 14:34 (NIV)
"'My soul is overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death,' he said to them. 'Stay here and keep watch.'"
:::

The Son of God, who lacked nothing, asked three men to stay with Him through His worst hour. He wouldn't face that hour alone. You don't get to skip what He refused to skip.

Dale has a brother now. It took him most of a year to ask. The man sat a few rows behind him at church. They'd known each other for twenty years and never had one real conversation. Now they talk every Thursday at noon, on the phone, while Dale eats lunch in the cab. Three questions and a prayer. For the first month, Dale was sure he'd signed up for the dumbest arrangement of his life. Last spring he went in for a scheduled surgery, and a nurse handed him another clipboard. When the form asked for the second name, his pen kept moving.

Find the man. Give him twenty minutes at the same hour every week. Open with the sentence. You're the one who goes first.

## Before you turn the page

The sentence may be the most awkward one you'll ever say. Nearly every man who has said it got the same answer back. "I've been wanting somebody to ask me that." May the Father give you the nerve for a twenty-minute ask. And may the man who hears it turn out to be a brother born for a time of adversity.
