# 17. No North Star

An old rancher taught me to find the North Star when I was a boy, standing in a pasture outside of Post with no lights for twenty miles in any direction. He showed me the Big Dipper first, and then how to follow the two stars on the end of the cup up and out until you landed on a star that wasn't very bright and didn't look like much.

"That one doesn't move," he said. "Everything else up there goes around all night. That one stays put. You get lost, you find that one, and you know which way's which."

I asked him why it didn't move. He said he didn't know and didn't need to. "It just doesn't. That's what makes it useful."

Years later a mason taught me something similar with a plumb line, which is nothing more than a string with a weight on the end of it. You hang it next to a wall and the weight settles and the string points straight down, every time, no matter how the wall looks, because gravity doesn't have opinions. If the wall's off, the string tells you. The wall doesn't get to argue with the string.

Two things that don't move. That's what this chapter is about, because most men are measuring their lives against things that do.

## Everyone did as they saw fit

There's a sentence near the end of the book of Judges that I think is the saddest summary of a people I know.

:::margin
Judges 21:25 (NIV)
"In those days Israel had no king; everyone did as they saw fit."
:::

Everyone did as they saw fit. Not everyone did evil; that's what makes it sad. Some of what they did was probably decent. But nobody was measuring by anything outside himself. Each man's own judgment was his north star, and each man's north star moved with him, so nobody could tell they were drifting because everybody was drifting together.

That describes most men I know. Not bad men; drifting men. They're measuring themselves against a reference that moves, so they don't feel the drift, and one day they're forty miles off course in a marriage or a career or a faith and they honestly can't tell you how they got there. Nobody can, if the thing they were steering by was moving too.

Here is what men use as a north star, and why every one of them moves.

Most men's first fixed point was their father. What he did was what a man does. Then you grew up and found out he was a moving star too, sometimes wildly, and if he was the empty chair you never even had a star to fix on. A father is a good thing to learn from and a terrible thing to steer by.

Then there are other men, and this is the big one. "I'm doing better than him." I make more than my brother-in-law. I'm home more than the guys on the crew. I've never cheated, like the deacon did. I go to church more than my neighbor. Every one of those comparisons feels like a measurement, and every one is a man holding his wall up against another crooked wall. You can be the straightest wall in a crooked town and still be off plumb.

There's the culture, which is what everybody knows a man should be. The trouble is that what everybody knows changes about every eight years now, and a man steering by it is steering by a star that jumps. What made a man in your grandfather's day and what makes one on the screens your son watches are not the same star, and chapter 2 already told you that you aren't crazy for noticing.

There are feelings, which sound like this: "I just don't feel like this is right anymore." "I feel like God's leading me to." "I don't feel anything when I pray." Feelings are real and they matter, and Part 5 gives them a whole chapter. But a feeling is the most moving star there is. It changes with sleep and blood sugar and the weather and what you ate for lunch. A man who steers by his feelings has decided to navigate by the clouds.

And there's success, which reasons that if it's working, it must be right. The business grew, so I must be doing something right with my life. The marriage hasn't blown up, so it must be fine. David ran a kingdom very well for a year with a murdered man in his drawer. Success isn't a plumb line. It's a wall that hasn't fallen yet.

:::margin
Proverbs 14:12 (NIV)
"There is a way that appears to be right, but in the end it leads to death."
:::

Appears to be right. That's what drift feels like from inside. It never feels like drifting. It feels like a reasonable way to go, and you only find out at the end.

## The one that doesn't move

God has a plumb line, and He told Amos about it.

:::margin
Amos 7:7-8 (NIV)
"This is what he showed me: The Lord was standing by a wall that had been built true to plumb, with a plumb line in his hand. And the LORD asked me, 'What do you see, Amos?' 'A plumb line,' I replied. Then the Lord said, 'Look, I am setting a plumb line among my people Israel; I will spare them no longer.'"
:::

I am setting a plumb line among my people. God holds a string with a weight on it against the wall of a man's life, and the string points true, and the wall doesn't get to argue. That's what His Word is. Not a set of rules, though it has rules in it. Not a book of inspiring thoughts, though it will inspire you. It's the fixed reference, the thing that doesn't move so that you can tell what does.

:::margin
Psalm 119:105 (NIV)
"Your word is a lamp for my feet, a light on my path."
:::

A lamp for my feet, and not a floodlight for the whole valley. Enough light for the next step, which is what a man on a Tuesday actually needs, and the reason it can be that is that it's the one thing in your life that isn't drifting along with you. Paul said the same thing to Timothy near the end of his own life, when Timothy was surrounded by people doing as they saw fit.

:::margin
2 Timothy 3:16-17 (NIV)
"All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training in righteousness, so that the servant of God may be thoroughly equipped for every good work."
:::

Teaching, rebuking, correcting, and training. Look at that list. It teaches you what's true. It rebukes you when you're off, which is the string against the wall. It corrects you, which means it shows you how to get back to plumb. And it trains you, over years, until your hands begin to build true without checking every course. Thoroughly equipped, Paul says. That's a man with a north star.

## Why men don't use it

I've asked, and here's what men say. They say they don't know how, because nobody taught them. Their father didn't read it, or read it in a way that made it look like homework. They tried starting at Genesis in January and were in Leviticus by February and that was that. That isn't a moral failure; it's a training failure, and it can be fixed in about a month.

They say it feels like homework. It does, if you read it like a textbook. Read it instead like a man listening to his Father talk. Read it out loud in the truck. Read it with one question in your hand: what does this say a man does?

They say they don't have time. You have twenty minutes for a brother, and you have ten for this. Chapter 34 will put it in your week for you. A man who says he doesn't have ten minutes for the only thing in his life that doesn't move has just told you what he's steering by.

And they say they don't feel anything when they read it, and I tell them that's fine, because you aren't reading it to feel something. You're reading it to find out which way is north. The rancher didn't feel anything about that dim star either. He only knew it didn't move.

## How a man reads

Here's how to begin, if you never have, or if you tried and quit. Start with one Gospel. Mark is the shortest and John is the deepest. Read a chapter a day, and you'll be done in two or three weeks, and you'll have watched the Man you're following do everything He did, and you'll know Him better than most men who've been in church for thirty years.

Then read Proverbs, a chapter a day, for a month. Dale, the man who took Micah to breakfast every other Tuesday for two years, started doing that at forty, on the day he realized he'd spent his whole adult life steering by his father's opinion of him and his father had been dead for six years. He read a chapter of Proverbs out loud in his truck on the way to work, every morning, and he's done it for fifteen years now, which means he's read the book through something like a hundred and eighty times. When Micah asked him once how he seemed to know what to do about money and women and anger and friends, Dale looked a little puzzled and said, "Chapter a day." There are thirty-one chapters, one for each day. It's a father talking to his son about money and women and work and anger and friends, and it's the most practical thing ever written for men, and you can read it for fifty years and it will still be telling you where the wall's off.

Pray the Psalms, because when you don't know how to pray, which is most mornings for most men, open to a Psalm and read it out loud to God as your own. David was a man with every exit you have, and he prayed his way through all of them, and he left you the words.

Ask one question every time: what does this say a man does? Not what does it mean in the original language, and not what a scholar would say. What does this say a man does, today, on a Tuesday? Then go and do it.

Read it with your brother. In the twenty minutes, one of you reads the verse you've been sitting on that week and says why. Iron sharpens iron better with a plumb line in the room.

And read it out loud when you can. In the truck, on the porch, at the table with your kids. There's something about hearing the Word in your own voice that makes it stop being homework and start being a Father talking.

## A story bigger than your story

Now I want to turn toward the next part of the book, because the north star is more than a rule book, and if you take it as only that you'll miss most of what it's for.

We've spent this whole second part on your story. The chair, the exits, the handoff, the brothers you didn't have, the problem inside and how it looks on you. All of that is your story, and it's real, and God knows every line of it.

But your story is not the master story. It's a chapter inside something much older and much larger, and if all you know is your own chapter, you'll read it wrong. You'll think the empty chair is the beginning, and it isn't. You'll think your sin is the end, and it isn't. You'll think the point of your life is to fix what was done to you, and it isn't that either.

The master story starts before the chair, in a garden, with a man who was built for something. It turns on two men and two ways of being a man. And it ends with a destiny that isn't a feeling. That's the next three chapters. I'm going to hold the plumb line up against the whole story of manhood, from the first man to the last, and I want you to see where you are in it.

Because a man with no north star isn't only lost on Tuesday. He's lost in the story. He doesn't know what he was made for, so he can't tell whether he's doing it. The fixed reference isn't only a set of instructions. It's a story that tells you who you are.

## The star at every age

Men steer by moving stars at every age, but the stars change, and I want you to see which one is likely to be moving under you now.

A young man of twenty is usually steering by other young men, and the crooked walls he measures against are the ones his friends have built. Eli told me his standard for how to treat a woman had been, for most of his teens, whatever the guys in the truck laughed at. The takeaway for him is that a young man who reads a chapter of Mark in the truck each morning has picked up a string that doesn't move, and that it will save him from measuring by walls that are also on sand.

A young father is usually steering by his feelings, because a man on four hours of sleep has almost nothing else to steer by, and his feelings are telling him the marriage is in trouble when the marriage is only tired. The takeaway for him is that the Word is the one thing in his life not moving this year, and that ten minutes with it before the phone is worth more than an hour of evaluating his marriage at three in the morning.

A man of fifty is usually steering by his success, which is the wall that hasn't fallen yet. Walt measured his life by eleven trucks for twenty years, and the trucks told him he was doing fine while his wife built a life without him. The takeaway for him is that success is not a plumb line, and that a man who's never held the string against his marriage doesn't know whether it's straight.

An older man has often steered by the culture he grew up in, and he's watching that culture get mocked and wondering whether everything was wrong. Hollis, who has read Proverbs through more times than he can count, told me that the Word had corrected his generation's picture in some places and confirmed it in most, and that this was the difference between a man who was shaken by the times and a man who wasn't. The takeaway for him is that the string doesn't care which decade built the wall.

## The house that held

Jesus ended the longest sermon He ever gave with a picture that every man in Texas understands.

:::margin
Matthew 7:24-25 (NIV)
"Therefore everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man who built his house on the rock. The rain came down, the streams rose, and the winds blew and beat against that house; yet it did not fall, because it had its foundation on the rock."
:::

Hears these words and puts them into practice. Not hears them; hears them and does them. That's the wise man. He's building on something that doesn't move, and when the weather comes, and it comes for every house, his house holds.

The other man in that story built on sand, and he probably built a fine-looking house. It probably looked fine right up until the rain. He was doing as he saw fit, measuring by walls that were also on sand, and he never knew until the water rose.

The rancher is dead now. The pasture outside of Post is still there and so is the star. I've found it on a lot of nights since, from a lot of places, and it has never once moved, and every time I look at it I think about a man who told a boy that you don't have to understand why a thing doesn't move to be saved by it.

Find the one that doesn't move. Hold the string against your wall. Then turn the page and let me show you the story it measures you by.

## Before you turn the page

I've told you about a rancher and a star, and a mason and a string, and I want to leave you with the thing the rancher said that I've never forgotten: you don't have to understand why a thing doesn't move to be saved by it. You don't have to be a scholar to read a chapter of Mark out loud in your truck. You only have to hold the string against the wall and let it settle. May the Father give you a lamp for your feet this week, enough light for the next step and no more, and may you find, as men have for three thousand years, that the one who built the wall true to plumb is patient with the ones who are learning to.
