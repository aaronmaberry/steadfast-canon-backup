# 8. The Empty Chair

Anthony's family had a kitchen table with four chairs around it, and for two years after his father left, nobody moved the fourth one.

He was nine years old, and his mother set three plates every night, and the fourth chair sat there at the end of the table with nothing in front of it, and every night Anthony looked at it while he ate. Nobody talked about it. His mother didn't cry in front of him; she cried in the laundry room with the dryer running, and he knew, and he never said. When he was eleven she finally carried the chair out to the garage and set a box of Christmas lights on it, and that was the end of that, as far as anyone in the house was willing to say.

He's thirty-eight now, with three kids and a wife who loves him and a job that pays, and he told me that some nights he stands in the doorway between the garage and the kitchen for a minute before he goes in. He just stands there. He couldn't tell me why. His kids are loud at the table and his wife is asking somebody to sit down and it's everything he ever wanted, and he stands in the doorway like a man who isn't sure he's allowed to come in.

He knows why, of course; he simply hasn't said it yet. There's a chair in his chest with a box of Christmas lights on it.

## Every man inherited something

This second part of the book is about what you inherited and what is yours, and those are two different things, and most men have never separated them, which is what we're going to do together over the next ten chapters.

Start with this: every man learned what a man is from the chair at the end of the table, not from a book or a sermon or a movie, though all of those added to the picture later, but from whoever sat in that chair, or didn't. Before you could read, you were reading him, learning how he came through the door in the evening, what his silence meant, whether his hands were safe, whether he looked at your mother when she talked or past her, whether he came to the game, whether he came home. You learned more about manhood before you were ten than you've learned in all the years since, and you learned it from that chair.

For some of you the chair was empty, through divorce or death or prison or a man who was never there to begin with, and that was Anthony's kind of chair. You grew up with a shape at the table where a man was supposed to be, and the shape taught you things. It taught you that men leave. It taught you that your mother's tiredness was somehow yours to fix. It taught you to figure it out, whatever it was, because there was nobody to ask.

For some of you the chair was full, and the man in it was gone anyway. He worked, or he drank, or he watched the television with a kind of attention he never gave you. He was in the house every night of your childhood and you couldn't tell anyone one thing he thought about you. I want to say this carefully, because these men often get a pass they didn't earn. A father who is present and absent at the same time can leave a hole as deep as one who packed a bag. Deeper, sometimes, because a boy can't even name it; after all, Dad was there, so what's your problem?

For some of you the chair was full and it was dangerous. The man in it was a weather system. You learned to read the forecast from the sound of the truck in the drive. You learned that being noticed was worse than being ignored, and so you learned to be small. Some of you learned instead to be big and loud so the storm would land on you and not on your mother or your little brother. Either way you learned your lessons from fear, and you're still living some of them.

And some of you had a good man in that chair. Present, kind, showed up. I want you to know I'm glad, and I want you to know two things. The first is that even the best father had limits, and you inherited those too, and there's a gap in you shaped exactly like the places he couldn't reach. The second is that you shouldn't skip this part of the book because you think it's for men with worse fathers. Every man in the room inherited something. You'll just have to look a little harder to find yours.

## What the empty chair teaches

Let me tell you what I've heard from men over a lot of years of this, because a wound you can name is a wound you can take somewhere, and a wound you can't name just keeps running the house.

The empty chair teaches a boy to father himself. He learns to make his own rules, hold his own line, comfort himself when he's scared. That sounds like strength, and some of it is. It also means he grows into a man who can't be fathered by anyone, including God, because he's been doing it alone so long that letting someone else do it feels like drowning.

It teaches him that needing things is dangerous. If you needed your father and he wasn't there, the lesson your body learned was that needing gets you hurt, so you stopped needing, and you became a man who doesn't ask for anything. Your wife will tell you she can never tell what you want, and you'll say you don't want anything, and you'll believe it.

It teaches him to read rooms. Boys with an empty or dangerous chair become experts on other people's moods, and it makes them good salesmen and good pastors and good at avoiding conflict, and it makes them terrible at knowing what they themselves feel, because they've spent thirty years pointed outward.

It teaches him to perform. If a man's approval was scarce, a boy learns to earn it. Grades, sports, jokes, whatever worked in that house. He grows into a man who's still auditioning and doesn't know for whom.

And it teaches him what he's not going to be, which sounds good and is a trap. Anthony swore he would never leave, and he never has, and he's proud of that, and it is also the only picture of fatherhood he has, which is a picture of a man he refuses to be. But you can't build a house out of what you're not. A man who has only a negative picture of fatherhood ends up standing in a doorway, sure of what he won't do and with no idea what to do instead.

## The chair explains you, and it doesn't excuse you

Here's where I have to make the turn, and I want to make it gently, because I know some of what you've carried.

What sat in that chair is what you inherited. You didn't pick it. Nobody asked a nine-year-old whether he wanted his father to leave. It happened to you, and it shaped you, and God knows every bit of it and isn't shrugging at any of it.

But what you do with it now is yours. Anthony's temper with his kids is his. It's shaped like his father's absence, and I understand that, and it's still his. His wife doesn't live with his father's absence. She lives with his temper. And the thing about a wound is that it can explain a man's behavior forever and never once change it. Plenty of men have worked out exactly why they are the way they are and have stayed exactly that way, because understanding felt like progress and it wasn't.

The Bible is full of men with empty chairs. Joseph's father loved him and still couldn't protect him, and his brothers threw him in a pit. David's father didn't bother to bring him in from the field when the prophet came looking at his sons. Timothy's father gets half a sentence in the whole New Testament, and it's his mother and grandmother who get the credit for his faith. Jesus Himself was raised by a man who wasn't His father and who disappears from the story before He's grown.

Not one of them is excused by it. They're shaped by it, and God works in them through it, and they are still held responsible for the men they became. That isn't harsh; it's dignity, because God treats you like a man who can do something with what happened to him, because you are one.

## Where the chair goes

So what does a man do with an empty chair? You start by admitting it's there. I've watched grown men, big men, go quiet and say "I don't really think about it" when I ask about their fathers, and I've learned that sentence means the exact opposite. You think about it every day. You think about it in the doorway. Say it out loud, once, in words. "My father left." "My father was there and I never knew him." "My father scared me." "My father was good and there's still a place he didn't reach." Whatever it is, it needs to be said by you.

Then you grieve it, which is the thing men don't do. We get angry about it, or we get numb about it, or we get busy, and we call all three of those "dealing with it." Grief is different. Grief is letting yourself feel the actual size of what you didn't get: the father who wasn't at the game, the hand that wasn't on your shoulder, the words you needed at fourteen and are still waiting for at forty. That's a real loss, as real as a death, and it deserves to be mourned rather than managed. David wrote a line about this that I come back to more than almost any other.

:::margin
Psalm 27:10 (NIV)
"Though my father and mother forsake me, the LORD will receive me."
:::

David wrote that, the man his father left in the field. He doesn't pretend the forsaking didn't happen. He names it, and then he says where he went with it.

That's the third thing, and it's the whole of chapter 10, so I'll only point at it now. There is a Father who does not leave. The empty chair is not the last word about fatherhood. It's the first word, and it's a lie, and the rest of this part of the book is about replacing it with the truth.

:::margin
Psalm 68:5-6 (NIV)
"A father to the fatherless, a defender of widows, is God in his holy dwelling. God sets the lonely in families, he leads out the prisoners with singing; but the rebellious live in a sun-scorched land."
:::

A father to the fatherless. That isn't a metaphor. It's a description of who He is.

## The chair in different rooms

The chair comes back differently at different points in a man's life, and I want to speak to a few of you directly.

If you're young and your father is still alive and still absent, you're probably still hoping. Hoping he'll call, hoping he'll come to the thing, hoping the next conversation is the one where he finally says it. I'm not going to tell you to stop hoping. I am going to tell you to stop waiting on him to become a man before you become one, because you can't build your manhood on a foundation that was never poured. Build it on the Father who's here. If your dad shows up later, good; you'll have something to offer him.

If you've just become a father yourself, this is the season when the chair comes back hardest. You'll hold your son and feel a fear so specific it takes your breath: what if I do to him what was done to me? Hear this: the fear means you're paying attention. Men who repeat the chair are almost always the ones who never looked at it, and you're looking, and that isn't a guarantee, but it's a start, and you can do the rest with help.

If you're divorced, I have to say a hard thing. You may be the empty chair now. Your kids eat at a table where you don't sit. I'm not saying that to shame you; some of you fought like lions to stay and couldn't. I'm saying it because a man who's honest about being the empty chair can do something about it, and a man who's defensive about it can't. Kenny told me the day he stopped explaining the divorce to his kids and started simply showing up was the day things turned: every exchange, every game, every Tuesday call at 7:30, including the months when his son said "nothing" to every question. He couldn't fill the chair at their mother's table. He could make sure there was one at his that was never empty when they came.

And if you're older and your father died before the conversation ever happened, I'm sorry, and I mean that. There's a particular grief in a door that closed before you reached it. You can still say what you needed to say. Say it to God, who knew your father better than you did, and say it to a brother, and write it down. The conversation you never got to have can still be had, just not with him, and it will still do something in you.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A young man of eighteen feels the chair on the day he moves out, when there's nobody to help him carry the boxes and nobody to tell him what a man does with a first apartment. He'll feel it again at his first real job when the other men talk about what their fathers taught them, and he has nothing to say. The takeaway for him is that the ache is accurate and not shameful, and that the Father who does not leave is willing to teach him the things nobody taught him, mostly through older men he'll have to go find.

A man in his forties feels the chair at his father's funeral, or at his father's bedside, when the conversation he'd been waiting for thirty years is suddenly never going to happen. Anthony's father is still alive, and Anthony feels the chair every time the phone shows his father's name and he's nine years old for a second. The takeaway for him is that the conversation can still be had, with God and with a brother, even when it can't be had with the man.

## Anthony's doorway

I asked Anthony what he thought his father would say if he could see the kitchen Anthony stands outside of every night: three kids, a wife, and a table where every chair is full.

He was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "I don't know. I don't know what he'd say. I never knew what he'd say."

And that's the inheritance, right there: not the leaving so much as the not knowing. A boy who never learned what his father thought of him grows into a man who doesn't know what any father thinks of him, including the one in heaven, and so he stands in doorways.

Here is what is yours, Anthony. You get to walk in. You get to sit down in a chair that's yours and stay in it. You get to be the man your kids read before they can read, and you get to make sure what they read is different from what you read. You didn't choose the empty chair. But you choose, every night, whether to stand in the doorway or come to the table.

Come to the table. The Father who does not leave is already sitting there.

## Before you turn the page

Some of you have read this chapter with a tightness in your chest that you've carried since you were a boy, and I want to say something to that boy before we go on. What happened to you happened, and God knows every line of it, and He isn't shrugging. He isn't going to ask you to pretend the chair was full. He's going to ask you to bring the empty one to Him, and then He's going to do something with it that I've watched Him do in men of every age in this book. May the Father who sets the lonely in families receive you the way David said He would, and may you find the courage to walk into your own kitchen tonight and sit down.
