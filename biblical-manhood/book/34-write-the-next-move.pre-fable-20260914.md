# 34. Write the Next Move

Hollis carries a notebook in his shirt pocket. It's about the size of a deck of cards, spiral-bound at the top, and he's carried one like it since he was nineteen. Every night before bed he writes down tomorrow's first three jobs, and that's all: not a to-do list, three things, in order, that he's going to do before anything else.

I asked him once why he still does it at seventy-four, when he has nowhere to be and nobody to answer to.

"Because if it isn't written down, it isn't going to happen," he said. "I learned that at nineteen and it hasn't stopped being true. A man who knows what he's going to do tomorrow gets up and does it. A man who's going to figure it out in the morning figures out that he'd rather have another cup of coffee."

He flipped the notebook open and showed me: fix gate latch, call Cole, read Proverbs 14. Then he closed it and put it back in his pocket, and I thought: that's fifty-five years of the next thing, written down the night before, and it's why he's Hollis.

You've read thirty-three chapters. If you close this book with a feeling and nothing written down, you'll have a marker with no Tuesday after it, and by Christmas you won't be able to tell anyone what the book said. So before the last chapter, I want you to write the next move, and I mean the next move rather than a life plan.

## Make it plain

God tells men to write things down. He told Habakkuk to, in the middle of a vision that was too big to carry in his head.

:::margin
Habakkuk 2:2 (NIV)
"Then the LORD replied: 'Write down the revelation and make it plain on tablets so that a herald may run with it.'"
:::

Write it down, make it plain, and make it so that someone can run with it: that's a plan in one sentence. Not a feeling in your chest that you'll act on when the time is right, but a thing written on something, plain enough that a man could pick it up and run. Men fail at plans in two directions. Some write nothing, and they leave the retreat or the conference or the book or the sermon with tears in their eyes and resolve in their gut, and they go home, and the resolve lasts until the first hard Tuesday, because nothing was ever written and so nothing was ever real. Others write too much. A forty-page life vision with values and mission statements and ten-year goals, and it goes in a drawer and dies of its own weight, because no man can run with forty pages.

The Bible's version is a tablet. One surface, plain words. Nehemiah, who rebuilt a wall in fifty-two days, did it because he'd prayed for months and then walked the wall at night and looked at exactly what was broken and then asked the king for exactly what he needed: timber, letters, safe passage, all of it specific and written. Then he organized every family by its section of the wall and went to work, and when men tried to pull him off it, he had a sentence ready.

:::margin
Nehemiah 6:3 (NIV)
"so I sent messengers to them with this reply: 'I am carrying on a great project and cannot go down. Why should the work stop while I leave it and go down to you?'"
:::

I am carrying on a great project. A man who has written his plan knows what the project is, and knows what's a distraction from it, and can say so.

## One page

Here's what I want you to write. One page, not a notebook; a page, or an index card if you write small. It pulls the whole book onto a single surface you can run with, and every line of it is something you've already been asked to name in a closer somewhere in these thirty-three chapters. You've done the thinking, and now you make it plain.

Start with the mark you're faintest on, from chapter 3, in one line. "Of the four, the one I'm missing most is ___." Rejecting passivity, accepting responsibility, leading courageously, or expecting the greater reward. Pick one, because you can't fix four at once.

Then your exit, from chapter 9, in one word: anger, the itch, or the drift. Written down where you'll see it, so that when you reach for it you'll know what you're reaching for.

Then your season and its assignment, from chapter 32, in one sentence with a verb in it. "I'm a new father under a baby, and my assignment is to protect the marriage inside the chaos." "I'm retired, and my assignment is to assist: two younger men and a grandson." "I'm single again in year one, and my assignment is to drive straight and slow and call my kids every Tuesday."

Then your brother, from chapter 13, as a name and a day and a time: "Dale, Thursdays at noon, by phone." If you don't have a name yet, the line reads, "Ask ___ by Tuesday." That's the move.

Then your man ahead, from chapter 14, as a name and the first question: "Hollis, how did you handle it when your kids stopped talking to you?" If you're the older man, this line flips: the younger man's name and what you'll offer him.

Then your Tuesday rhythm, from chapter 33: five to ten unwitnessed things that don't move, scaled to your season: the alarm, the first hour, the floor, the twenty minutes, the secret good thing, the job by noon, the blessing, the notebook, sundown, Sunday. This is the longest part of the page, and it's still short.

Then your house's next move, from chapters 21 through 29. One thing for her, or for the wife who isn't here yet, or for the woman you're building a covenant toward in your singleness. One thing for each child, or for a fatherless kid if you have none. And a date, if you have a son or daughter approaching the crossing, for the day.

Then your work's next move, from chapter 30, in one line. "Do the plant with all my heart for the Lord." "Tell my wife the truth about my hours." "Name my job for tomorrow before bed." "Take the bridge job."

Then the drawer you're opening, from chapter 16, in one line with a name attached. What's hidden, and which Nathan you're telling. This is the line men skip, and you mustn't.

And then the next move. One thing, before next Tuesday. The single first action from everything above that you're going to do this week. Not the most important thing; the first. "Ask Dale." "Put the phone in the kitchen tonight." "Say the eleven-second prayer at dinner." "Call my father and pick a date."

At the bottom, sign it and date it. Chapter 11 said men need markers, and this is one. The day you wrote the next move is a day you can point to.

## What a move is

I keep saying "move" rather than "goal," and the difference matters. A goal is a destination. "Be a better husband." "Get closer to God." "Fix my relationship with my dad." Goals are fine and they're useless, because you can't do a goal. You can only do a move.

A move has three parts: a verb, a person, and a day. "Ask my wife what she most needs from me, Thursday, after the kids are down." "Call my father Sunday and tell him one thing I'm grateful for." "Pray out loud with my roommate tonight." "Text Dale before noon tomorrow and ask about twenty minutes a week." Every one of those you either did or you didn't, and there's no arguing with it. That's what makes it a move.

Hollis's notebook is all moves: fix gate latch, call Cole, read Proverbs 14, each one a verb and an object with the day always tomorrow. He has never written "be a better man" in fifty-five years. He has written something like twenty thousand small moves, and the better man is what they added up to. Solomon knew the difference too.

:::margin
Proverbs 21:5 (NIV)
"The plans of the diligent lead to profit as surely as haste leads to poverty."
:::

The plans of the diligent, and not the dreams of the inspired. Plans, made by men who show up. Diligence is a Tuesday word.

## Why plans die

I've watched a lot of men write a page like this, and I've watched what kills it. Here's the list, so that you can build against it.

Plans die when they're too big. He wrote forty things and did none. Write ten, or write five. The page should be something you can read in ninety seconds in a truck.

Plans die when nobody sees them. A plan that lives in a drawer is a wish. Habakkuk's was written so that a herald could run with it. Yours needs to be read by one other man. Show it to your brother and give him the right to ask you about it.

Plans die when there's no date. "Soon" is not a day. "This week" is barely a day. Tuesday is a day. Put dates on the moves.

Plans die when they were made in a feeling. The retreat high, the last chapter of a book. Chapter 31: the gauges were pegged when you wrote it and you steered by them. That's all right, as long as you review the page on a plain Tuesday when the feeling has gone and see what still holds. Most of it will. Some of it was the feeling. Cross that part out.

Plans die when there's no floor, when everything was aspiration and nothing was non-negotiable. The Tuesday rhythm is the floor. Without it the rest floats.

Plans die when nobody reviews them. A page written once and never read again was written for the feeling of writing it. Every ninety days, sit down with the page and your brother and ask what got done, what's changed, and what the next move is now. Then write a fresh page. Hollis writes a fresh three every night. You can manage a fresh page every season. And plans die when God isn't in them, which is the one that matters most and the one men miss most. James had a word for men who plan without Him.

:::margin
James 4:13-15 (NIV)
"Now listen, you who say, 'Today or tomorrow we will go to this or that city, spend a year there, carry on business and make money.' Why, you do not even know what will happen tomorrow. What is your life? You are a mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes. Instead, you ought to say, 'If it is the Lord's will, we will live and do this or that.'"
:::

If it is the Lord's will. That isn't a disclaimer to tack onto the bottom of the page. It's the posture of the whole page. You're writing moves, and you're handing the page to God, and He's allowed to change it. Nehemiah planned everything and prayed through all of it and still watched God rearrange his days. A plan held loosely in an open hand is a plan God can use. A plan gripped in a fist is an idol with bullet points.

:::margin
Proverbs 16:9 (NIV)
"In their hearts humans plan their course, but the LORD establishes their steps."
:::

Humans plan, and the LORD establishes. Both halves are true. You plan, on paper, in plain words. He establishes the steps, which means He decides which of your moves actually land and when. Write the page, and then pray over it and let Him hold it.

## The page in every season

If you're single, your page leans on chapter 21's list: the house, the table, the company, the devotion, the fatherhood, the purity. Pick one from each for the season. And if you want a wife, one move: a specific ask of a specific woman on a specific day, or a specific question to a specific older man about how to find one.

If there's a baby in your house, your page is three lines, and that isn't failure; it's wisdom. The counter time with her, the shift you take, the brother on the phone at nine, and everything else waits until you've slept. A young father who writes a ten-point plan in the first year is a young father who'll feel like a failure by February. Three lines, done every week, is the whole plan for this season.

If you're single again, your page for year one has a rule at the top: no turns. Then the floor: the Tuesday call to the kids, the every-other-weekend that never gets traded, the brother on the phone, church even when it's awkward. Then the drawer: your part, named, to your brother. That's all for year one. Year two's page can have the rebuild on it.

If you're the older man, your page is the rhythm from chapter 30 and then three lines a younger man's page can't have: the hill you're asking God for, like Caleb; the younger man you're walking across the parking lot toward; and the conversation you've been putting off with a grown child, or the blessing you've never written, with a date. Roy's page had "Say it to my son on the porch" on it for two months before he did it. Then he did it, because it was written and it had a date and Hollis asked him about it every Thursday.

And if you're a widower, your page may be the shortest of all, and the most important: a brother's name, a job for each morning, and one thing for the garden that's left. Mr. Pruitt's first page after his wife died said, in its entirety, "Coffee with Ed Wednesdays. Trees."

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A young man of twenty writes a page that leans on the house he's building alone: one line each for the apartment, the table, the brother, the fatherless kid, and the phone in the kitchen, and one move toward a woman if there's a woman. Eli's first page had five lines and a date, and he told me the date was the first time his life had ever felt like it belonged to him.

A man of fifty writes the drawer line first, because it's the one his age skips, and then the brother line, because he has three hundred contacts and no name to put there. Walt's first page had "Ask Dale about Thursdays" at the bottom, and it sat there for three weeks before he did it.

## As for me and my household

Joshua, at the end of his life, gathered the whole nation and made them choose, and then told them what he'd chosen, in a sentence men have been putting over their doors for three thousand years.

:::margin
Joshua 24:15 (NIV)
"But if serving the LORD seems undesirable to you, then choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve, whether the gods your ancestors served beyond the Euphrates, or the gods of the Amorites, in whose land you are living. But as for me and my household, we will serve the LORD."
:::

Choose for yourselves this day, he says, and not this season or when you're ready, but this day. And then: as for me and my household. That's a man writing his next move in front of witnesses. He isn't describing a feeling. He's declaring a direction, for himself and for everyone under his roof, and he's doing it out loud where men can hear it and hold him to it.

Your page is your version of that sentence. It doesn't need to be eloquent. It needs to be plain, dated, signed, shown to a brother, and held loosely in an open hand before God. Then it needs to be run with.

## Tomorrow's three

Hollis put the notebook back in his pocket, and we sat on his porch a while longer, and I asked him what the hardest move he'd ever written down was.

He thought about it. "Probably 'Tell Cole he's a good man.' That was on the page for about three weeks before I did it. Kept moving it to the next day. Finally I thought, if it isn't written down it isn't going to happen, and it was written down, so I'd better." He shrugged. "Took thirty seconds. He sat in his truck and cried for twenty minutes. I'd have put it off another year if I hadn't had to look at it every night."

That's the whole method: write the move, look at it every night, do it because you wrote it, and let God establish the step.

You have one page to write and one move to make before Tuesday. Go make it plain.

## Before you turn the page

I've given you a page to write and I know that writing it will feel, for some of you, like the most exposed thing in this book, because a written move can be checked. That's the point of it, and it's also a mercy, because a man who writes his move down and hands it to a brother has stopped carrying his intentions alone. Hold the page loosely, in an open hand, and let God rearrange it. May the Father who establishes a man's steps establish yours this week, and may you do the move at the bottom before Tuesday and discover, as Hollis has for fifty-five years, that the next thing was always enough.
