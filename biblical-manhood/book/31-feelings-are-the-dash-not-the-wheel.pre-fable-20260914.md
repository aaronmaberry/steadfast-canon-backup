# 31. Feelings Are the Dash, Not the Wheel

The old Ford with the cracked block had a temperature gauge, and it worked. That's the part of the story I left out in chapter 15. All summer, while I was replacing hoses and thermostats, the gauge was telling me the truth, which was that the engine was running hot. Every time I put the hood down and told myself I'd fixed it, the needle would climb again on the next long grade, and I'd look at it and decide the gauge must be off.

The gauge was the only honest thing on that truck. But here's the other side of it. I've known men who drove into a ditch staring at the dash. A gauge tells you something true about the state of the machine. It doesn't tell you where the road goes. A man who ignores his gauges blows an engine, and a man who steers by them drives off the road. You need both hands on the wheel and one eye on the dash, and you need to know which is which.

That's feelings, and it's this whole chapter in a sentence. Your feelings are the dash and not the wheel. They're real, and they're telling you something, and you should read them. And they don't get to steer.

## Two ways men wreck

There are two kinds of men who get this wrong, and most of us are one or the other, and some of us switch depending on the week.

The first is the man who ignores the dash. He'd tell you he doesn't do feelings. He isn't angry; he's fine. He isn't sad; he's tired. He isn't lonely; he's busy. Walt was that man for the entire year before the bank parking lot in Abilene. The fear gauge had been climbing since the spring, every time he looked at the receivables, and every time he looked at it he told himself the gauge was off, the way I told myself the Ford's temperature gauge was off, and he went on driving. By the time he finally read it honestly, sitting in his truck with three hundred contacts and nobody to call, the fear had been running his sleep and his temper and his silence at the dinner table for eleven months, and his wife could have told him so at any point if he'd asked. He was probably raised by a man who did the same thing, and he's proud of it, and he believes it's strength, and it isn't. It's a man driving with tape over his gauges. The feelings don't go away because he won't look at them; they go somewhere else, and chapter 9 told you where: out as anger, down as appetite, away as absence. Every one of the three exits is a gauge that got ignored until the engine did something about it. The man who says he doesn't have feelings has feelings running his life from under the hood where he can't see them.

The second is the man who steers by the dash. He's the opposite and he's just as wrecked. "I don't feel in love anymore." "I don't feel like God's here." "I feel like this job isn't my calling." "I feel like I need to be happy, and I'm not happy here." He reads a gauge, which is fine, and then he turns the wheel toward whatever the gauge says, which is a ditch. He leaves marriages because a feeling changed. He leaves churches because the feeling left. He makes his whole life a series of course corrections toward whatever the needle is doing this week, and he ends up nowhere, and he can't understand why, because he was so honest about his feelings.

The Bible's way is neither. It's a man who reads every gauge honestly, says out loud what it says, brings it to God and to a brother, and then steers by something that doesn't move.

## What the dash is telling you

Let me give you a vocabulary, because a lot of men can't read their own dash for the simple reason that nobody ever taught them the names of the gauges.

Fear says that something matters to you and you aren't sure you can protect it. Read it, and then ask what you're actually afraid of losing.

Anger says that something is wrong, or that you think it is. Read it, and then ask what's under it, because it's almost always fear or shame or helplessness wearing a hard face, as chapter 9 said.

Sadness says that something is lost. Read it, and grieve the thing. Men skip this gauge more than any other, and the skipping is what turns sadness into the drift.

Loneliness says not-good-alone. It's the most accurate gauge on the dash, because God Himself said it in Genesis 2. Read it, and don't read it as "something's wrong with me." Read it as "go find a brother."

Desire says you're alive. It isn't sin to want. What you do with the wanting is where sin comes in.

Numbness says you've been running hot so long that the gauge burned out, and that isn't peace. It's a warning light that has stopped working, and it's the most dangerous reading on the dash.

Shame says "I am a wrong thing." Read it, and then check it against the plumb line, because this gauge lies more than any other. Chapter 15: guilt is a working gauge and shame is a broken one.

And joy says you're where you were made to be, doing what you were made to do. Read it, and thank God, and notice what you were doing when it came.

The Psalms are a book of men reading their gauges out loud to God. David, who was as hard a man as ever lived, said things to God that most men wouldn't say to their wives. Read this one and tell me he was ignoring the dash.

:::margin
Psalm 42:5 (NIV)
"Why, my soul, are you downcast? Why so disturbed within me? Put your hope in God, for I will yet praise him, my Savior and my God."
:::

Why are you downcast. He's reading the gauge and naming it. And then, in the same breath: put your hope in God. He's turning the wheel, not toward the feeling but toward God. That's the whole method in two sentences, and it's why the Psalms are the best book a man could read who has never learned to do this.

## What the dash is not telling you

Now the other half, for the man who steers by it. A feeling is not the truth about God. "I don't feel forgiven" is a gauge reading and not a verdict. God's forgiveness is a legal fact established at a cross, and it doesn't fluctuate with your sleep. "I don't feel like God is here" is a reading too, and it's often accurate about you and never accurate about Him. He said He'd never leave. The gauge describes your perception; the wheel is His word.

A feeling is not the truth about your marriage. "I don't feel in love anymore" is a reading. Every marriage that has lasted forty years has had stretches, sometimes long ones, when that gauge read empty. The men who steered by it are divorced. The men who kept the covenant with both hands and let the gauge do what gauges do woke up, often years later, with the needle back up and a marriage that had gone somewhere the feeling could never have taken it.

A feeling is not a command. "I feel like God wants me to" is the most dangerous sentence in a Christian man's mouth, because it takes a gauge and gives it God's authority, so test it by holding it against the plumb line. Does the Word say it? Do your brothers see it? Does it look like the four marks, or like an exit? God doesn't usually lead by feelings, and when He leads, what He leads toward looks like Scripture and not like relief.

:::margin
Proverbs 28:26 (NIV)
"Those who trust in themselves are fools, but those who walk in wisdom are kept safe."
:::

Trust in themselves is a man steering by his dash. Walk in wisdom is a man with his hands on a wheel that's fixed to something outside him. And a feeling is not permanent. Every gauge on your dash moves with sleep, food, blood sugar, hormones, weather, the last conversation you had, and what you watched last night. A man who makes a permanent decision on a Tuesday-night reading has handed his life to the weather, so wait. Read it again on Thursday, and again after you've slept, and again with a brother in the room.

## What the wheel is

So what does a man steer by, if not the dash? Four things, and you've met all of them in this book.

The first is the Word, from chapter 17, which doesn't move. When your feelings say one thing and the Bible says another, the Bible is describing reality and your feelings are describing you.

The second is the covenant, meaning what you promised before God and with witnesses, to her and to the church and to your children and to the men you gave your word to. Promises are made precisely so that they'll hold when the feeling doesn't; otherwise you wouldn't need to make them.

The third is the four marks: reject passivity, accept responsibility, lead courageously, expect the greater reward. When you don't know what to do, do whichever of those four is in front of you. None of them requires a feeling.

And the fourth is the next assigned thing, from chapter 20, which is not the thing you feel like doing but the thing God has put in your hands. Do that thing, and the feeling will follow, or it won't, and either way you did the right thing.

Here's how the two work together, and I'll use the truck. You're driving up a long grade and the temperature gauge climbs. You don't ignore it, because you'll blow the engine. You don't steer toward it, because there's nowhere to steer toward a gauge. You read it, and you name it, "running hot," and then you make a wise decision with the wheel. You pull over, you let it cool, you check the coolant, you tell somebody, and then you get back on the road toward where you were already going. The gauge changed how you drove. It didn't change where.

That's a man reading his dash and keeping his wheel.

## Elijah under the broom bush

I want to spend some time here on the heavy end of the dash, because men die of it and the church has been too quiet about it.

Some of you aren't only sad. You're somewhere darker than that, and you've been there a while, and you aren't sure how to say it, and you're a little afraid of what saying it means about you. I want to show you a man of God who was there, and what God did with him. Elijah had just seen the greatest victory of his life, fire from heaven on a mountain, and a day later he was alone in the desert asking to die.

:::margin
1 Kings 19:4 (NIV)
"while he himself went a day's journey into the desert. He came to a broom bush, sat down under it and prayed that he might die. 'I have had enough, LORD,' he said. 'Take my life; I am no better than my ancestors.'"
:::

I have had enough, take my life: that's a prophet of God, one of the greatest who ever lived, reading his dash out loud, and the dash said "I want to die." He didn't hide it. He said it to God. Now watch what God did. He didn't rebuke him. He didn't tell him to have more faith. He sent an angel with bread and water and let him sleep. Then He fed him again. Then, after the man had eaten and slept, God spoke, and then God gave him a job and a companion. Food, sleep, presence, a word, work, a friend, in that order.

That is the most practical passage on depression in the Bible, and here is what it means for you.

Read the gauge and say it out loud, to God first, the way Elijah did, and then to a man. If the gauge says "I've had enough," that sentence needs to leave your mouth in front of another human being this week. Not because saying it fixes it, but because a man carrying that alone is in more danger than he knows.

Take care of the body. God's first two moves with a suicidal prophet were a meal and a nap. Sleep, food, sunlight, movement: those aren't spiritual shortcuts, because they're the way God made you, and He knows how we're formed.

:::margin
Psalm 103:14 (NIV)
"for he knows how we are formed, he remembers that we are dust."
:::

He remembers that we are dust. Your body isn't separate from your soul, and a man who hasn't slept in a month is going to read every gauge on his dash wrong. Fix what you can fix in the body first.

Get help, and hear me on this: getting help is not weakness. A doctor is not a failure of faith. A counselor is not a substitute for God; he's one of the ways God sends bread and water. If your gauges have read dark for more than a few weeks, if the numbness has settled in, if you aren't sleeping when you could, if you've thought about ending it, you see a doctor and you tell him the truth. If he says medication, you take it, the way you'd take insulin. That isn't steering by your feelings. That's repairing a broken gauge so that you can read the dash again.

And if you're thinking about ending it tonight, or this week, call a man now, your brother from the twenty minutes or your pastor, and if there's no one, call 988 and talk to someone whose whole job is to sit with you in the dark. Elijah asked God to kill him and God fed him breakfast. There's no shame in the broom bush. There's only the question of whether you'll let somebody find you there.

Then, when you've eaten and slept and been found, the wheel. Elijah got up and went back to work with a new companion beside him. The feeling didn't leave first. He moved first, in small ways, with help, and the feeling followed over time. That's how it works for most men. Not a lightning bolt, but a meal, a nap, a brother, and the next small assigned thing, repeated until the gauge comes back into range.

## The dash in every season

If there's a baby in your house, every gauge on your dash is pegged, and none of them are accurate. You're sleep-deprived in a way that changes brain chemistry, and so is she. Do not make decisions about your marriage at three in the morning. Do not evaluate your life at three in the morning. Sleep is a spiritual discipline this year, and so is grace, for her and for yourself and for the gauges that lie loudest exactly when you're most tempted to believe them. Say to each other, out loud, when it's bad: "This is the sleep talking. We're fine. We'll talk about it after a nap."

If you're divorced, your dash is going to do things for a year or two that you've never seen it do. Anger, grief, relief, guilt, loneliness, in rotation, sometimes all in one afternoon. Waves at odd times: the exchange in the parking lot, Sunday night in an empty house, a song. Read every one of them. Say them to God and to your man. And don't turn the wheel for a year. No moving, no marrying, no quitting the job, no leaving the church. Kenny's older man told him, "Your gauges are broken right now. You don't make turns with broken gauges. You drive straight and slow until they settle." He did. He said the second year the needle started making sense again, and he was glad of every turn he hadn't made.

If you're an older man, you may have been raised in a generation that taped over the dash and called it manhood. Roy said he didn't know he had feelings until he retired and there was nothing left to drown them out. "I sat on the porch and all of a sudden I was sad about things from 1975." That's fifty years of gauge readings coming due. It isn't too late to learn to read them; the Psalms are the manual. And the older man has gauges the younger ones don't: grief that accumulates as friends die, fear of decline, the feeling of being useless. Read them out loud to a brother. Widowers especially: the loneliness gauge isn't lying to you, and it isn't a sign that you're weak. It's a sign that God made you for company and you've lost some. Go find the rest.

And if you're single, watch the loneliness gauge and read it right. It isn't telling you something's wrong with you. It's telling you the truth Genesis 2:18 told: not good alone. Steer toward brothers, and not toward whatever screen or woman promises to quiet the needle fastest.

Two more men belong in this section, because they're the ones a chapter like this most often leaves out.

A young man of twenty is most likely to steer by the dash, because his culture has taught him that his feelings are the truest thing about him. Eli left a good church because he "didn't feel it anymore," and came back a year later when an older man asked him what he'd been steering by. The takeaway for him is that a feeling is not a command and not a verdict, and that the Word is the only instrument on the dash that doesn't move.

A man of fifty is most likely to have tape over the dash, which was Walt for eleven months before the parking lot, and the takeaway for him is that the feelings he won't read are running his house from under the hood, and that reading them out loud to one man is the first move.

## The Man who read every gauge

Jesus felt everything. He wept at a grave, and He was troubled in spirit, He was overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death, and He said so. Hebrews says He prayed with loud cries and tears. There has never been a man who read his dash more honestly than the Son of God.

And He never once let a gauge turn the wheel. Not as I will, but as You will. He read the gauge that said "let this cup pass," out loud, three times, and then He steered by the Father's will straight into the thing the gauge was begging Him to avoid.

That's the model: not a man with no feelings, and not a man run by them, but a Man who felt all of it, said all of it, and kept both hands on a wheel that was fixed to something that didn't move.

So read your dash, and tell the truth about what it says, to God and to a brother. Then put your hands back on the wheel and drive where you were already going.

## Before you turn the page

If any gauge on your dash has been reading dark for a long time, I want to say the plainest thing I know before you close this chapter: tell one man today. Not this week; today. Elijah asked God to kill him and God fed him breakfast, and there is no shame in the broom bush, only the question of whether you'll let somebody find you there. For the rest of you, read your dash tomorrow morning in one honest sentence to God, and then put your hands back on the wheel. May the Father who knows how we're formed and remembers we are dust give you rest for your body and truth for your gauges, and may He steer you where you were already going.
