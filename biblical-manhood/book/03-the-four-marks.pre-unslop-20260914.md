# 3. The Four Marks

If the maps don't hold, you've got a harder question than the one you started with. If that isn't what a man is, what is?

I want to answer that in one sentence you can carry in your pocket. Then we'll take it apart, one mark at a time, so you can see how each piece looks on a man in the middle of an ordinary week. But first a picture, because the picture is what keeps the sentence from becoming one more thing on the shelf.

## What a mark is

Walk into a shop where a man has worked for twenty years and you can read him without asking a question. The workbench has grooves from the same cuts, repeated until the wood remembers the job. There's a ring where the coffee cup always sat. The wrench hooks hang in the order his hand reaches for them. He didn't get those grooves from wanting to be a craftsman, or from a weekend about tools. He got them from the work, on ten thousand days he couldn't tell apart.

That's what a mark is. Not a feeling you have about yourself. Not a title somebody gives you. A mark is what gets left on you by what you've done, over and over, for a long time. Feeling wild at dawn doesn't leave a groove on a life. What you do when you get home does.

So here's the sentence. I didn't invent it. Men have been handing it to one another for years because it holds. I've held it up against my own life and against the lives of men in every season I can name, and I've never found a place where it lies.

**A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.**

Read it again, slower. It names four marks. Not four moods, and not four qualities you either have or don't. Four things a man does, which over time leave four things you can see on him, the way you can see the grooves on that bench.

The four marks are what that mind looks like on a man, in a house, on a crew, in an ordinary week.

## He rejects passivity

Every town has a man on a tailgate. He isn't a bad man. He has opinions about the weather and the school board and the way the city runs the water department, and he'll share every one of them while the work he came to do waits behind him. He's waiting for the right time, or the right price, or the right feeling, and he's waited so long that the waiting has started to look like wisdom.

Passivity is a man's default setting. It almost never looks like laziness. It looks like patience. It looks like keeping the peace. It sounds like "I didn't want to overstep," and the man saying it usually believes he's being humble. The first man in the Bible was passive in exactly this way. When the serpent came into the garden and started twisting the one command God had given, Genesis says the man was with her. He didn't grab the fruit and he didn't argue for it. He stood there, and his standing there was enough to break the world.

Rejecting passivity doesn't mean getting loud, or pushy, or mistaking volume for strength. It means that when something needs doing, and you know it, and you're the one standing there, you get off the tailgate. You bring up the hard conversation instead of hoping it gets brought up. You don't wait for a feeling before you start. **Stop Waiting** looks at this closely.

## He accepts responsibility

The second mark is weight. I helped a friend move a gun safe one August, four hundred pounds of steel down a narrow hallway in heat that made the air thick. There's a moment with a load like that when you're committed. You've lifted it, he's lifted it, and the only way through is through. You can't set it down in the hallway. You can't decide halfway along that this isn't really your safe. You carry it to where it's going, and then you set it down.

That's what responsibility feels like on a man. A boy waits for responsibility to be assigned, then argues about whether the assignment was fair. A man looks at what's in front of him and says "that's mine," even when it isn't his fault and nobody would blame him for walking past. The bill nobody wants to open is his. The kid nobody's talking to is his. The apology nobody's making is his. Lamentations says it's good for a man to bear the yoke while he's young. Good, not merely tolerable. The weight shapes him. **Pick Up the Weight** shows how to take it without being crushed by it.

## He leads courageously

Somewhere there's a cold stock tank and a bunch of boys on the bank. The water's colder than any of them expected, and everybody's waiting for somebody to go first. That's leadership. Not the title, not the corner office. Being the one who goes into the cold water first so the others can follow.

Somebody has to go first, in every house and every crew and every church. Somebody has to be the first to say "I was wrong" in a marriage that's been quiet for three days. Somebody has to be the first to pray out loud at a table where nobody does, the first to call the brother who's been drifting, the first to say the true thing in the meeting where everybody's nodding. Courage isn't the absence of fear. If you weren't afraid, it wouldn't take courage to move. Courage is a man who's afraid putting his boots on anyway and walking. When God told Joshua to be strong and courageous, He didn't tell him to stop being afraid. He told him what to do while he was afraid, and why he could do it. **Go First** is about that.

## He expects the greater reward

The last mark is the one that separates a good man from a godly one. It's the one most men skip, so I've put it last on purpose.

Pecan trees don't hurry. Plant one this fall and you'll water it for years before it gives you a single nut worth cracking. Every man who ever planted a pecan orchard was planting for somebody else. You don't plant pecans because you intend to eat pecans. You plant them because you believe in a harvest you won't hold in your own hands, and you're willing to spend your strength on it anyway.

That's the fourth mark. A real man expects the greater reward, God's reward, and that expectation changes what he does with ordinary days more than anything else in this book. He can do the right thing when nobody's looking. He can stay married through a hard decade. He can give money that won't come back and time that won't be thanked, because he believes a Father sees what's done in secret. Hebrews says anyone who comes to God must believe that He exists and that He rewards those who earnestly seek Him. He rewards, and God isn't embarrassed by that word. The man who doesn't expect a reward from God will go looking for one somewhere else: applause, money, women, a bigger truck, a boy's approval, his own father's approval. **Live for a Reward That Lasts** is about that, and I've come to believe it's what makes the other three possible.

## Why four, and why these

Each mark answers a specific way men break. Passivity is how a man breaks by doing nothing. Blame and evasion are how he breaks by refusing weight. Cowardice, which almost always travels under a nicer name, is how he breaks by hanging back. Living for the wrong reward is how he breaks slowly, across a whole working life, while looking successful. I've never met a man in trouble whose trouble didn't trace back to one of those four.

Notice the shape. The first three are things a good man of any faith or none might do. The fourth is what makes the other three Christian. It's why a man can keep doing the first three when they cost him and nobody claps. Take the fourth out and you have a self-improvement list. Leave it in and you have a way of life that outlasts you.

Same four marks in every season. The ground changes. The marks don't. A young man gets serious now. A young father turns toward his worn-out wife in the middle of the chaos. A man in midlife notices he's drifted toward whatever claps for him and comes home. An older man gets up with no badge to put on and finds the work anyway.

## Before you turn the page

You've got the sentence. Don't try to fix all four this week. Find the mark that's faintest on you and hold that one for a while. The men I've watched change didn't change all at once. They worked one mark on one ordinary day, then did it again. May the Father who made you in His image show you which mark He wants to press into you first, and may He be patient with you while it takes, the way He's been patient with me.
