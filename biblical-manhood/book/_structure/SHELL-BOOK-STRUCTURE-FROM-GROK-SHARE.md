# Shell Book Structure Guide

## Conversation prompt

> What’s the common structure of a best selling shell book? Title page, table of contents, preface, introduction, about the author/book, continent, backend, etc?

## Response

Assuming you mean a self-help book (and “continent / backend” as content + back matter): bestsellers are surprisingly formulaic. They look professional in the front/back pages and follow a **problem → framework → action** arc in the middle.

## The three layers of the book

| Layer | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Front matter | Identify the book, prove legitimacy, orient the reader |
| Body | Deliver the transformation promise |
| Back matter | Help the reader keep going + connect with you |

Typical length for a commercial self-help book is about **45,000–80,000 words**, often **10–20 chapters**. ([Spines](https://spines.com/genres/self-help/))

## Front matter (common order)

Not every book uses every item. This is the usual professional sequence:

1. **Half-title (title only)** — optional
2. **Title page** — title, subtitle, author, publisher
3. **Copyright page** — © year, ISBN, edition, disclaimers
4. **Dedication** — optional, short
5. **Epigraph** — optional quote
6. **Table of contents** — expected in nonfiction
7. **Foreword** — written by someone else (credibility). Optional but useful.
8. **Preface** — you explain why you wrote it / how you arrived at the method
9. **Acknowledgments** — sometimes here, sometimes in back
10. **Introduction** — often treated as the real start of the book

### Preface vs. introduction

- **Preface:** about the author and the making of the book
- **Introduction:** about the reader’s problem, the promise, and how to use the book

Many bestsellers fold “how I came to this” into the introduction (Atomic Habits opens with the author’s injury story, then the thesis). ([Asja](https://www.asja.org/structuring_a_self-help_book/))

A strong introduction usually does four jobs:

- Name the reader’s urgent problem
- Show you understand it (you’ve been there or you’ve seen it)
- State the premise (your fresh take) and the promise (what will change)
- Tell them how the book is organized / how to use it

## Body: the structure that actually sells

The most common content architecture is a five-part map: ([Asja](https://www.asja.org/structuring_a_self-help_book/))

1. **Problem, premise, promise** — connect with the reader
2. **History of the problem** — how they got here (without shaming)
3. **What they need before they act** — mindset, language, framework
4. **The action plan** — principles, steps, exercises
5. **Maintenance / hard cases / looking forward** — how to keep it when life gets messy

### Two popular ways to slice the middle

- **System-led:** one named framework, one piece per chapter
  - Example: *Atomic Habits* — Fundamentals → 4 Laws (Obvious / Attractive / Easy / Satisfying) → Advanced tactics → Conclusion. About 20 chapters plus intro/conclusion. ([Ursummary](https://ursummary.com/atomic-habits-summary-by-chapter-james-clear/))
- **Principles-led:** standalone principles that still build (Covey’s 7 Habits)

### Chapter-level pattern most bestsellers repeat

**Hook / story → name the principle → explain it plainly → example or case → exercise / prompt → short takeaway → bridge to next chapter**

That “story → principle → application → exercise” loop is the genre’s engine. ([Squibler](https://www.squibler.io/learn/writing/how-to-write-self-help-book/))

## Back matter (the “backend”)

Common pieces, in roughly this order:

1. **Conclusion / closing charge** (sometimes still counted as body)
2. **Acknowledgments** (if not in front)
3. **Appendix / worksheets / scripts**
4. **Notes or sources**
5. **Recommended reading / resources**
6. **About the author**
7. **Also by the author / next book**
8. **Index** (more common in print/reference-heavy titles)
9. **Call to action** — newsletter, community, course, app

“About the author” almost always lives in the back, not the front. Front is for the reader’s problem; back is for who you are and what to do next.

## What “bestselling” usually adds on top of structure

- A short, sticky title plus a clarifying subtitle (“what + how”)
- A memorable named system (4 laws, 7 habits, 5 languages) readers can repeat
- Exercises they can do this week, not just ideas
- A last-page invitation into your world (list, program, community)

## Faith-based training/devotional adaptation

For a faith-based training/devotional book, the same skeleton works: keep religious content in the body and exercises, keep front/back matter looking like a serious trade book—not a church bulletin. Title page, TOC, preface/intro, framework chapters, then about the author + next step is the standard professional shape.
