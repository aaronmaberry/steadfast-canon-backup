# Introduction

I have spent most of my adult life sitting across tables from men. Kitchen tables, mostly, and the ones at the back of a church fellowship hall, and the tailgates and truck cabs and breakfast counters that serve the same purpose in this part of the country. Some of the men were young enough to be my sons and some were old enough to be my father, and I have come to believe that underneath the different jobs and different decades and different troubles, they were all asking me the same thing. They wanted to know what a man is, and whether they were one, and if not, whether it was too late.

This book is my long answer to that question. It is not the only answer and I will not pretend it is, but it is the one I have watched hold up in the lives of real men over a lot of years, and I have tried to write it the way I would say it to you if you were sitting across from me with your hands around a cup of coffee going cold.

## Who I'm writing to

I am writing to a man in every season of life. Find your seat.

You may be single and still waiting for someone to call you a man out loud, newly married or deep in little-ones years, providing everything except yourself, divorced and still a father, in the quiet house after the kids left, retired and wondering what a man does with his mornings, or burying a wife. You are all in this room.

I am not going to write only to the man with the mortgage and the minivan. The counsel changes as your season changes, and when it does, I will turn toward you inside the paragraph and say so. What I will not do is stop every chapter to run the whole list again. Later we will sit down and talk about seasons on purpose, all of them, in one place. Until then, trust that I know you are here. If the house is loud and the page count looks impossible, start at **A Quiet Tuesday**, keep the closer each week, and come back for the rest when you can. The book will still be here.

## What I'm claiming

The center of this book is one sentence. I did not invent it, and I am not going to pretend I did. Robert Lewis gave it that shape years ago, and men have been handing it to one another ever since, and I have never found a better way to say it. But I have held it up against my own life for a long time, and against the lives of hundreds of men in every season I just named, and I have never found a place where it lies.

A real man rejects passivity, accepts responsibility, leads courageously, and expects the greater reward, God's reward.

Those are the four marks. **The Four Marks** will explain them, and the chapters that follow will take them one at a time. For now I only want you to notice what kind of sentence it is, because it is not a sentence about what a man feels. There is a whole shelf of books for men that start inside the chest, with your heart and your longings and your wild places, and some of those books were good to me when I was young, and I am not writing against them. But this book makes a plainer claim. The four marks are things you do, on ordinary days, and they show. They leave a mark on a man the way a life of work leaves a mark on his hands, and you can see them or you can see their absence, and no amount of feeling makes up for either.

## How the book is built

We start with an ordinary day, because that is where a man's life is decided. **A Quiet Tuesday** names the problem. Then we tell the truth about the room you are in, and we put the four marks in your hands, one by one, until you can say them without looking.

From there we go back to what you inherited and what is still yours: the chair at the end of your childhood table, the anger and the drift, the Father who does not leave, brothers, the ask, the problem inside. Then we go further back still, to the beginning of the human story, so you stop reading your life as if you invented it. Then we walk into the house: the woman, the bed, the daughter, the son. Then we take the long road: work, feeling, season, obscurity, the next move, and the men who stay with you.

Running through all of it is Tuesday. Not the altar. Not the retreat. The day with nothing special in it, when nobody is watching. Every chapter will land, at some point, on a Tuesday-sized picture, and I make no apology for it.

## The four lines at the end

Every chapter ends the same way, with four short lines, and each line comes with a plain sentence about why it matters for that particular chapter. I want to explain them once here so I do not have to keep explaining them.

**Name it** asks you to say out loud, in your own words, what the chapter touched in you. Men are good at nodding along and terrible at saying "that's me," and nothing changes in a man until he says it.

**Do this week** gives you one thing to do before next Tuesday, not a program and not a list, but one thing, and if you do only that one thing from each chapter, you will have done more than most men do with most books.

**Tell a brother** asks you to say the thing out loud to another man. A mark you keep to yourself never hardens into anything. If you do not have a brother yet, **A Man With No Brothers** is for you. Until you get there, telling a brother can mean writing it down where you will see it and telling God, who has heard worse.

**Carry this** hands you one line to take with you into the week.

One more thing about the four lines, because I know how a tired man reads a book like this. You are going to get to a chapter having not done the last chapter's thing. When that happens, do not skip it in shame and do not quit the book. Tell your brother you did not do it, which is itself the "tell a brother" line, and do it this week instead. Every man in these pages has missed a week. The ones who changed are the ones who said so and went again.

## The men you'll meet

You are going to meet a number of men in these pages by name: Roy, who turned in a state trooper's badge; Tomás, who has a baby who does not sleep; Kenny, who is divorced and calls his son every Tuesday; Micah, who sat at the single men's table at weddings for six years; and others. They are composites, which means the conversations happened, at real tables and with real men, but the names and the details have been mixed together so that no one can be picked out of a lineup, and so that you might recognize yourself in one of them without recognizing anyone else.

## One more thing before you start

This book is meant to stand on its own. Read it. Do one thing a week. Find one other man to do it with. That is enough for a man to change.

We also built a twenty four week training around the same spine: videos, printable guides, and a class a church or a small group or a man alone can run. In the back of this book you will find condensed training material keyed to those weeks. If you want the full class, the twenty four week guide is available at walksteadfast.com. The book is the book. The training is the training. Consume either. Consume both. Just do not binge four chapters on a Saturday and call it finished.

So find your cup of coffee. Pull up a chair. Let me tell you about a Tuesday.
