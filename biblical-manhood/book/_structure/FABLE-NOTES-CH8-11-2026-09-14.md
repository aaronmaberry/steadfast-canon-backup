## Chapter 8: The Empty Chair

**File:** `book/08-the-empty-chair.md`
**Body count:** 1,781 inclusive / 1,683 prose

### The new picture

The source hung the chapter on Anthony, the fourth kitchen chair with the box of Christmas lights, and the garage doorway. All of that is gone. The replacement is an ordinary Saturday: Curtis in the passenger seat while his son takes his first laps in a school parking lot, right hand clamped on the door handle, left foot on a brake pedal that isn't there, saying "easy" in a voice he doesn't recognize. The reveal is that nobody ever sat in that seat for him; his mother did it, scared, because his father was long gone. The chair at the end of the table stays the chapter's teaching concept. The passenger seat is where the chair shows up in a grown man's week.

The return scene (the next Saturday) does the work the source's "Anthony's doorway" section did: Curtis tells his son the truth, takes his hand off the handle, and says the first fatherly thing he has ever said on purpose. The closing line of the example ("He'll remember the man who stayed in the seat") sets up the forward point to the Father who does not leave without naming a chapter number.

### Teaching kept, reorganized

- Every man learned what a man is from the chair at the end of the table, before he could read.
- Four kinds of chair (empty, full but gone, full and dangerous, good with edges), now two paragraphs instead of four, with the "good father" reader still told plainly that he inherited something too.
- New organizing sentence for the wound mechanics: **an empty chair never stays empty; something always sits down in it, and whatever sits down in it raises him.** The five lessons from the source (fathers himself, needing is dangerous, reads rooms, performs, knows only what he won't be) are now framed as what sat down in the chair. This is the "hole that doesn't stay empty / how a boy fills it" thread the brief asked to keep.
- Inherited versus yours: the explain/excuse turn, the wife who lives with the fuse rather than the absence, understanding that felt like progress and wasn't.
- Biblical men with empty chairs: Joseph, David, Jesus. Timothy cut for length. The responsibility line was rephrased to "the chair shaped every one of them and excused none of them" so Jesus isn't described as "answerable for the man He became."
- Three moves: admit it, grieve it, take it to the Father who does not leave. Psalm 27:10 and Psalm 68:5-6 (NIV) retained verbatim in `:::margin` blocks.

### Killed or changed per brief

- **Texas:** none in source or rewrite.
- **Chapter numbers:** "it's the whole of chapter 10" is now "gets its own chapter later in this book." "You'll want it in chapter 12" is now "You'll want it later in this book." Editor should confirm the later chapter still calls for the unsent letter.
- **Tuesday:** source had "every Tuesday call at 7:30." Removed. Zero Tuesdays. Saturday appears twice (hook and return scene).
- **Season roll-call:** the source ran six "If you're..." paragraphs plus two add-on men. Now one paragraph under "The chair follows you" covering: young man with a living absent father, brand-new father, divorced seat, man whose father died before the conversation. Kenny is gone; the divorced seat is **James**, addressed directly but with no invented biography (the sentence is about "a divorced father" generally, not James's kids, job, or history).
- **Age numbers softened:** nine, eleven, thirty-eight, eighteen, forties, fourteen, forty all removed. Replacements: "grade school," "before you were old enough to stay home alone," "as a teenager," "gray in his beard," "before he could drive."
- **Retired motifs:** none used. The scene was deliberately placed in a school parking lot, not a driveway, to stay clear of the retired driveway pictures. No stairs, beams, doors, trophies, groceries, recitals, packs, lawn chairs.
- **Pile chant:** the five-lesson section varies its openers (often / for a lot of boys / for others / or / and sometimes) rather than stacking one repeated frame.
- **Four marks sentence:** not used. The verbatim text was not supplied with the Wave 2 brief, and the lock requires it verbatim or not at all. Easy to drop in on request.
- **Em/en dashes:** zero. Swept with a regex on U+2013, U+2014, and double hyphens.

### Your Turn

Locked layout: `## Your Turn`, `---`, four bold heads without periods, "Because ...: ask" as one paragraph under each of the first three, `:::notes` with two underline rows under all four. Carry this is a single line with no Because. All four asks were rewritten rather than lifted; the letter exercise and the "read him the sentence, not the letter" instruction are kept because they chain into later chapters.

### Flags for the editor

- Curtis is a new name for this wave. If it collides with a Wave 1 man, any two-syllable name swaps in cleanly; the name appears eight times.
- The hook implies Curtis's father left when Curtis was a boy and was "gone for years" by the time he learned to drive. Nothing else about the father is specified (alive or dead, why he left), which leaves room for the fatherless chapter later in this book.

## Chapter 9: Anger, the Itch, and the Drift

**File:** `book/09-anger-the-itch-and-the-drift.md`
**Body count:** 1,795 inclusive / 1,666 prose

### The new picture

The source opened on three strangers at a Thursday breakfast, one per exit (the drill thrower, the man on his phone at two in the morning, the man who felt nothing). That stack is gone. The replacement is one man, Owen, taking all three exits in a single week: the slammed dishwasher door, forty minutes locked in the bathroom with his phone, and "whatever you think" from the couch. Under all three sits one unspoken fear, a lost contract and numbers that don't work. The return scene ("Owen's mailbox") has him tell his wife the truth and, the next time the kitchen goes quiet, walk to the mailbox and back before saying the true sentence. "It didn't fix the fall. It fixed the kitchen."

Converting three men into one man does two things the brief asked for. It gives the chapter a single worked example instead of a roster, and it makes the "same wound, three exits" thesis visible in a lived week rather than asserted across a table.

### Teaching kept, reorganized

- Opens by picking up the chapter 8 sentence (the chair never stays empty; something sits down in it) and naming the three things that usually do: out as anger, down as appetite, away as absence.
- "Home exit" kept as the working term, with one refinement the hook makes necessary: block the home exit and the pain finds the next one. The exits are a set with a favorite, not a personality type.
- Anger: fear, shame, or helplessness underneath; Ephesians 4:26-27 (NIV) verbatim in margin; the ninety seconds; "your wife can work with a scared man, she can't work with a storm"; the strongest man in the room. The ninety-second walk is now "to the mailbox and back" instead of "out to the truck," to stay clear of the retired driveway pictures.
- The itch: one itch across every appetite; relief, not pleasure; James 1:14-15 (NIV) verbatim in margin; Paul's "flee" and Joseph's run referenced in prose (1 Corinthians 6:18 and Proverbs 29:11 and 16:32 were cut from margin blocks for length, and their teaching folded into prose); tell one man the whole truth, cut the supply, deal with the loneliness; "the itch shrinks in men who are known."
- The drift: leaving without moving; Ecclesiastes 10:18 (NIV) verbatim in margin; the couch and the ladder; name it to your wife, take one thing back, go find the man who quit caring. New linking line: the drift is how a man becomes the full chair with nobody in it that some readers grew up staring at.
- The same root: three boys, three lessons, every exit leads out of the house. Jesus took none of them (compressed to one paragraph). Forward point to the Father who does not leave, by title phrase, no number.

### Killed or changed per brief

- **Texas:** none.
- **Chapter numbers:** "the thing we talked about in chapter 4" (passivity) dropped; the drift is simply described. "Chapter 13 is about brothers" is now "a whole chapter on brothers later in this book." "That's the next chapter" is now "That's where we go next."
- **Tuesday:** source had "a Tuesday that went badly" and Kenny's "every Tuesday night" calls. Zero Tuesdays. Zero named weekdays at all; the hook runs "the first night," "two nights later," "by the weekend."
- **Season roll-call:** source had four "If you're..." paragraphs plus two add-on men (Eli at the parts counter, Walt and the woman at the office). Now one paragraph: young single (itch loudest, watch the drift), new father (anger at three in the morning, the voice from your father), **James** in the divorced seat (all three exits at once in the first year or two, addressed as a general truth about divorced fathers, no invented bio), older man (drift and anger by way of the television, coffee with her).
- **Named men cut:** Roy (retired officer, coffee), Kenny (divorced, Tuesday calls), Eli, Walt. The coffee prescription survives as an unnamed instruction to older men.
- **Age numbers softened:** twenty-six, fifty-two, forty-five, nineteen, sixty, thirty, "married twenty-six years," "fifteen years," "forty days" all removed. Durations that remain are not ages (forty minutes, three weeks, ninety seconds).
- **Retired motifs:** none. No truck-in-driveway, no recliner (couch instead), no stairs, beams, doors as pictures. The Ecclesiastes rafters line is quoted scripture with a two-sentence gloss, not a load-bearing picture.
- **Pile chant:** the out/down/away triplet is the teaching's spine and appears once. Section openers vary.
- **Four marks sentence:** not used (verbatim text not supplied).
- **Em/en dashes:** zero.

### Your Turn

Locked layout, four `:::notes` blocks, eight underline rows. Asks rewritten. "Name it" keeps the sentence stem "When things get heavy, I." "Do this week" keeps the three-branch first move. Carry this is new: "Out, down, or away, every exit leads out of the house. The pain doesn't need an exit. It needs a Father." The source's "grief with its fists up" line was retired rather than lifted; its idea lives in "anger is the one feeling that feels like power, so he grabs it the way a cold man grabs the nearest coat."

### Flags for the editor

- The "before you turn the page" blessing was kept short (three sentences) to hold the body under the ceiling. If the desk wants the fuller pastoral close from the source, roughly forty words of teaching would need to come out elsewhere.
- Ephesians 4:26-27 is the only anger scripture left in a margin block. If the desk wants Proverbs 16:32 back as the section's closing verse, it costs about twenty-five words.

## Chapter 10: The Father Who Does Not Leave

**File:** `book/10-the-father-who-does-not-leave.md`
**Body count:** 1,785 inclusive / 1,605 prose

### The new picture

The source hung the chapter on the Little League boy scanning the bleachers, with the man "still scanning at thirty-eight in a doorway." The scan survives as an idea (the chapter still says "stop scanning"), but the picture is new: the grade-school pickup line at three. Kids come through the doors looking for one car; the ones who find it drop their shoulders and start talking; one ends up on the curb with a teacher checking her phone while the line empties. Curtis returns from the empty-chair chapter as the man who has been in that line twenty minutes early every school day for years, and who was never once the kid who came through the doors and found the car. He learned to walk home.

The close brings the picture around: Curtis starts saying "Father" out loud in the pickup line, the one place he's alone. It feels like talking to the windshield, then like somebody in the seat beside him (a deliberate echo of the passenger seat two chapters back). Then his youngest comes through the doors, finds the car, and quits looking, and Curtis understands what he's being offered. "He was in the line before the bell. Let your shoulders down. You've been found."

Curtis recurring is the house pattern (the source reused Anthony across these two chapters). It also lets the wound chapter's man complete the arc from sitting in the seat for his son to being a son, which is the "wound toward belonging" movement the brief asked for.

### Teaching kept, reorganized

- The trouble with the word: four childhood chairs produce four false fathers (not coming, silent newspaper, mood to read, good with a gap). Now one paragraph rather than four.
- Read God through your dad versus read your dad through God; original and copy. John 14:9 (NIV) verbatim in margin, glossed in one line.
- What He is like: comes toward you (Luke 15:20 margin, the road-watching father who ran), names you first (Matthew 3:17 margin, blesses first and sends second), carries you (Deuteronomy 1:31 in prose), knows the need (Matthew 6:8 in prose), disciplines because you're His (Hebrews 12:7 in prose, with the rage/discipline distinction for men with dangerous fathers), does not leave (Hebrews 13:5 in prose, John 14:18 margin, "orphans").
- The hardest thing: a man who fathered himself doesn't know how to be fathered; Curtis's honest sentence ("Nobody ever showed me the receiving end") replaces Anthony's. Five practices kept: call Him Father out loud (Romans 8:15 "Abba" in prose), bring Him your exit, let Him tell you who you are, let yourself be carried, stay in the house.
- What this does to your other father: sees him more clearly, honors, names, forgives, may reach toward him "later in this book."
- Close: stop scanning and stop auditioning; standing settled in the Son; 1 John 3:1 quoted in prose ("And that is what we are").

### Killed or changed per brief

- **Texas:** none.
- **Chapter numbers:** "which is what chapter 12 is about" is now "a road we'll walk later in this book."
- **Tuesday:** the source had three ("on a Tuesday, before you've done a thing," "the Father's voice sounds like on a Tuesday," "fail on a Tuesday and get up on Wednesday"). All gone. Replacements: "right now," "on an ordinary afternoon," "fail today and get up tomorrow" (later compressed). Zero weekday names in the chapter.
- **Season roll-call:** source had four "If you're..." paragraphs plus two add-on men (the twenty-year-old in the parked car, the forty-five-year-old praying to a newspaper). Now one paragraph: young, new father (let the baby teach you), **James** in the divorced seat (the Father didn't leave when the marriage did; He was watching the road; no invented bio), older man calling God Father for the first time.
- **Named men cut:** Anthony, Roy (sixty-eight, in his truck). Roy's "coming in out of the wind" line was retired; the older-man beat now reads "heat after a long time in the cold."
- **Age numbers softened:** thirty-eight, nine, twenty-five, thirty years, sixty-eight, twenty, forty-five all removed. "Since you were a boy," "decades of looking," "about a week."
- **Retired motifs:** none. The source's "corner post" (structural metaphor) became "held his own weight so long that leaning feels like falling." The source's "coming in from the truck, careful on the steps" carrying picture was dropped rather than risk the stair and driveway pictures. No bleachers as the central image.
- **Scripture blocks:** source had ten margin blocks. Kept four as margins (John 14:9, Luke 15:20, Matthew 3:17, John 14:18) and moved six to prose reference or short inline quotation (Deuteronomy 1:31, Matthew 6:8, Hebrews 12:7, Hebrews 13:5, Romans 8:15, 1 John 3:1). All retained quotations are NIV verbatim.
- **Pile chant:** none. The "He comes toward you / He tells you who you are / He carries you" heads are the teaching's spine, each followed by a full paragraph.

## Chapter 11: When Home Never Handed You Off

**File:** `book/11-when-home-never-handed-you-off.md`
**Body count:** 1,792 inclusive / 1,608 prose

### The new picture

The source opened on Micah at his grandfather's funeral with a paper plate of casserole, watching the older men by the coffee. That's gone. The replacement is one house with two ways out. Wes, with kids in high school and his name on two vans, still spends Saturday mornings at his father's place handing the man the wrench while his father stands close enough to tell him how; he texts a photo of every finished job and the reply is always a correction. His older brother left the summer after high school in the middle of a fight and has spent the years since proving something to a man he barely speaks to. One boy slammed the door. One boy never really left. Neither was handed off.

That picture carries the two ideas the brief added to the teaching. The slam is now named as the third counterfeit (a boy trying to hand himself off by breaking something), and the orbit is the fourth (the adult son still circling the house, waiting to be told he can stop). "Leaving without slamming" is defined in the section on fathers who won't say it: you honor him and stop orbiting him in the same week, you go because he's your father and not for the sentence, nothing gets broken on the way out, and you take the word from the Father and from men who'll give it.

The stand-in handoff (the source's deacon Hollis at the truck window) is now an unnamed older man from Wes's church, at a utility sink after they swap out a water heater: "I've watched you a long time. You're a good man. I'd trust you with anything I own." Wes dries his hands twice. The close returns to Saturday a year on: he still goes, it doesn't land the way it used to, he wrote a date inside his Bible, and he called his brother.

### Teaching kept, reorganized

- What a handoff is: a man, a word, a weight, a welcome. David to Solomon (1 Kings 2:2-3, NIV, margin), Paul to Timothy with his hands (2 Timothy 1:6, NIV, margin). Weight without word, word without weight.
- Counterfeits: sex, paycheck, the slam, the orbit. The cost: a man who doesn't know he's a man doesn't take a man's weight.
- Jacob's night: stole the blessing, twenty years a fugitive, held on until dawn (Genesis 32:26, NIV, margin), new name and a limp, handed off by God on the ground in the dark.
- How a man receives it now: from the Father first, ask an older man to say it, be welcomed by brothers, take the weight before you feel ready (Solomon acted like a man before he felt like one), mark the date.
- When the man in the chair won't say it: some fathers never will; honor without orbiting; the stand-in man.
- The other side (older men): folded into the seasons pass as its own beat. Saying so is the whole job; a man, a word, a hand on a shoulder, and a "go"; no ceremony needed.
