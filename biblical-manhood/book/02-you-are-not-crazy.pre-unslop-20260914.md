# 2. You Are Not Crazy

A man in midlife sat across from me at a diner a few years back and said something I've heard since from men in every season. "I feel like I've been handed six instruction manuals for being a man, and every one of them contradicts the other five, and I'm supposed to act like that makes sense."

He wasn't angry. He was tired, the way a man gets tired aiming at a target that won't hold still. Be strong. Be vulnerable. Lead. Get out of the way. Your instincts are dangerous. Your failure to act on them is what's wrong with your marriage. One voice said manhood was a costume. Another said it was the most important thing about him. A third said it was whatever he wanted it to be. A fourth said it had been settled long ago and he was simply failing at it. He'd sat in churches that treated him as a problem to manage and in locker rooms that treated him as an appetite to feed. Somewhere in there he'd started to wonder whether the confusion was in the world or in him.

I told him what I'll tell you. The confusion is in the world. The gap you feel is real.

## The picture you were handed

Every man alive today was handed a picture of manhood that doesn't hold together. Most of us have spent years trying to make it hold anyway, and blaming ourselves when it wouldn't. I want to look at that picture plainly, so you can stop blaming yourself for the fact that it doesn't work.

Start with home. **The Empty Chair**, later in this book, goes deep on this. For now, it's enough to say that whoever sat at the end of your childhood table taught you what a man was before you could read, and for most of us the lesson came in fragments. A father who was there and silent. A father who was gone. A father who was a storm. Even a good father left gaps. You started with a piece of a picture and went out to fill in the rest.

The world had plenty to offer, none of it consistent. The culture told you manhood is a problem, that the part of you that wants to build and compete and protect and take charge is residue the species should've outgrown. Then the same culture mocked men who were passive, who couldn't commit, who wouldn't grow up, and never noticed it had asked for exactly that.

The screen gave you a fool, a brute, or a lone hero who needed no one. The fool was the father in every sitcom. The brute was the villain, or sometimes the hero. The lone hero was the one every boy wanted to be, and the trouble with him is that he has no tired wife, no father who calls twice a year, no son who won't talk, no church, no brothers, no ordinary week. He's a man with no house, and you were trained to admire him while you were living in one.

The marketplace said a man is what he earns. The internet said a man is what he watches. Some corners of the church gave you "spiritual leader" so soft you couldn't have told a grown man what to do on a weekday. Other corners gave you warrior and king so loud there was no room for a mortgage, a sick mother, and a shift at the plant. And the shelf of books for men tends to start inside the chest, with what a man feels. Some of that is true. But feeling wild doesn't leave a mark by itself. Only what a man does leaves a mark.

Put the fragment from home next to the manuals from the world, and you have what most men are trying to live by. It was never a picture. It's six maps that don't share a north. You can hold them all in your hands. You can't walk by them. Every bearing contradicts the last, and a man who tries gets turned around and calls the dizziness his own fault.

## What the crossed maps do to a man

I've watched those maps do three things to men. You've probably felt all three.

They make a man wait. When you don't know what a man is supposed to do, the safest move is no move. A lot of male passivity isn't laziness. It's a man standing very still because every direction he could go has been declared wrong by somebody. He isn't on the tailgate because he's lazy. He's on the tailgate because he's confused, and he's decided confused men shouldn't drive.

They make a man perform. If you can't find the real target, you learn to hit whatever target the room wants. You're one man at church and another at work and another with your buddies and another with your wife, and none of them is quite you, and the costume changes wear you out. A performer isn't a liar, exactly. He's a man who never got a stable picture and learned to borrow one from whoever was watching.

And they make a man quietly ashamed. This is the one I see most. Hand a man contradictory instructions he can't live up to, and he doesn't usually conclude the instructions are broken. He concludes he is. He carries a low hum of failure under everything, and he doesn't talk about it, because talking about it would confirm it. The man at the diner had that hum. Most of the men I sit with do. It isn't a sign something's wrong with them. It's the sound of a good man trying to obey six maps at once.

## What's actually true

Here's what I want to say to that hum, slowly.

Your instinct that manhood is a real thing and not a costume is right. It is real, and God made it. On the first page of the Bible He made human beings in His image, male and female, on purpose, and He called it good before anyone had done anything with it.

:::margin
Genesis 1:27 (NIV)
"So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them."
:::

Your manhood isn't a problem to solve or a residue to outgrow. It's a way of carrying the image of God, and it was His idea.

Your sense that most of what you've heard about it doesn't hold together is sound too. You were handed crossed maps, you've been trying to walk by them, and the fact that you keep getting turned around isn't evidence against you. It's evidence against the maps.

And wanting somebody to tell you plainly what a man does is the right want. Men were meant to be handed a picture by other men. Most of us weren't. We've been guessing ever since.

The answer, when it comes, is simpler and harder than anything on the shelf. Simpler, because it fits in one sentence. Harder, because the sentence has to be lived on ordinary days, and no feeling in your chest and no burst on a big day can do that for you.

## One pass through the room

The contradiction lands differently depending on where you're standing. Whatever your season, you aren't alone in it.

A young man hears from his school that his restlessness is a disorder, from his phone that a man is whatever gets attention, from his youth group that a man is a servant leader, and from the older boys that a man is whoever's had the most girls. He's trying to be all of it at once and thinks the fact that he can't is a problem with him. It isn't. The maps are the problem.

A young father is supposed to be present and provide, lead and defer, stay strong for a newborn and stay in touch with his own feelings, all in the same week. He's failing at most of it because it can't all be done. The hum under his exhaustion isn't evidence he's a bad father.

A man in midlife has quietly given up. He's decided manhood is whatever he happens to be doing, mostly working and coming home tired, and he's stopped listening because every voice contradicted the last. His resignation looks like peace. It isn't. It's a man who folded the maps and sat on them.

An older man who lived by a picture handed to him long ago is watching that picture get mocked on every screen his grandchildren hold, and he's begun to wonder whether the thing he built his life on was a mistake. It wasn't. Some of what he was handed was true and some wasn't, and the true parts are still true. He needs somebody to sort it with him, not somebody to tell him it was all wrong.

Same maps. Different seasons. None of them alone.

## What comes next

I'm going to hand you a picture, and the frame of it is one sentence. **The Four Marks** puts that sentence in your hands. It doesn't start in your chest. It starts with what you do. It has room for the man with the mortgage and the shift at the plant, the single man at the back table, the divorced man in the apartment, the old man who just turned in his badge. It doesn't require you to feel wild. It requires you to get up.

## Before you turn the page

The tiredness you feel isn't failure. It's the tiredness of a man who's been carrying a contradiction, strength spent on the wrong load. If the hum has been under your life a long time, let it get quieter tonight. Not because you've solved anything, but because you've seen the maps for what they are. Set them down. Soon you'll have something that holds. May God give you rest from carrying what was never yours to carry, and the courage to pick up what is.
