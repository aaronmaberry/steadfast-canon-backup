# 4. Stop Waiting

There's a stair in a house near you that nobody steps on. Third from the bottom, the one that gives a little and speaks when it takes weight. The kids learned to skip it before they learned to spell their own names. The man who owns the house has stepped over it so many times his legs do it without him, and he knows exactly what it wants: two long screws, a bead of glue, twenty minutes with the drill hanging in the garage.

Ask him and he'll tell you he's going to get to it. He means it, and that's the trouble. Meaning it is the whole of what he's done.

The stair is the least of it. What worries me is what it's teaching everyone who lives with him, which is that a man can know a thing is broken, know how to fix it, and step around it for years. And it isn't the only thing being stepped around in that house. There's a conversation with his wife he's going to get to. A brother he hasn't called since the funeral. A number on a statement he doesn't open. A son who went quiet about the time his voice changed. A church he's going to start showing up at again. He's going to get to all of it. He's been going to for years, and the years are the story.

The first mark of a man is that he rejects passivity. I want to show you what that looks like on an ordinary weekday, because the word makes most men picture somebody on a couch with the blinds down, and that isn't the man I'm worried about. The man I'm worried about is up before the sun. He's tired and useful and working hard at nearly everything except the four or five things that matter most. On those he's waiting, and he's waited so long it doesn't feel like waiting anymore. It feels like the floor.

## What it wears

You'll never catch passivity dressed as itself. If it showed up as plain laziness you'd know it and be ashamed, and shame at least gets a man off the step. So it dresses from a better closet. It borrows the virtues.

It wears patience and says, I'm giving it time. Sometimes that's right. More often time is doing the job the man refuses to do, and doing it badly, because time has no judgment and loves nobody in the house.

It wears peace and says, I don't want to start something. So nothing starts, and the peace he's keeping is the peace of a kitchen where nobody says anything that costs anything.

It wears humility and says, Who am I to bring it up? You're her husband. You're the boy's father. You're the one standing there. That's who.

It wears preparation: another book on marriage, another podcast on fathering, another long talk about the thing instead of the thing. A man can become an expert on the conversation he's never had.

It wears faith and says, I'm waiting on the Lord. There's a real kind of waiting on God, and there's a counterfeit that signs His name to its excuses. I'll come back to the difference soon, because a great deal rides on it.

And it wears busyness, the best disguise of all. A man who's on the road before dawn and home after dark, coaching on Saturdays, setting out chairs on Sunday, doesn't look passive to anyone, least of all himself. His wife could tell you in one breath what he's avoiding. Being busy isn't the opposite of being passive. Often it's the hiding place.

The first man tried hiding too. After the fruit, when God came walking in the cool of the day, Adam was in the trees.

:::margin
Genesis 3:9 (NIV)
"But the LORD God called to the man, 'Where are you?'"
:::

God knew where he was. The question wasn't about location. It meant: where is the man I set here to work this ground and keep it, who stood right there while it all went wrong and said nothing? That question is still walking through gardens. It'll walk through your house tonight, past the stair, with your name on it.

## Why we wait

No man has ever explained his stillness to me with the word passive. He has better words, and every one of them sounds reasonable late at night in a driveway with the engine off.

He says he didn't want to make it worse. That's fear of conflict, the most common reason there is. He figures the quiet he has beats the noise he'd make, and he doesn't figure that quiet has a price too, one that compounds while nobody's making payments.

He says he wasn't sure he was right. That's fear of failure wearing a responsible face. But not acting is a way of acting. It's a decision with results, and he'll answer for them as surely as for a wrong move.

He says nobody asked him. This one runs deep. It usually starts in a boyhood where initiative got you corrected, where the safest way to be in a room was to wait for instructions, and some men carry that into the office and the church and their own marriage, still waiting to be picked. If you're young and this is you, hear it now. Nobody hands a grown man permission to lead his own life. The ground you're standing on is yours to work.

He says it'll sort itself out. It won't, because a field never once weeded itself.

:::margin
Proverbs 24:30-31 (NIV)
"I went past the field of a sluggard, past the vineyard of someone who has no sense; thorns had come up everywhere, the ground was covered with weeds, and the stone wall was in ruins."
:::

Look at what the sluggard didn't do. He didn't torch the field or push the wall over. He did nothing, long enough, and the world handled the rest, because thorns don't ask permission. Neither does distance in a marriage, or interest on a debt, or the silence of a boy who's decided his father isn't coming.

And under every reason a man gives there's one he doesn't say, which is that he's comfortable. Comfort never argues. It keeps the room at the right temperature and waits for you to agree.

## The real kind of waiting

Scripture does tell a man to wait on the Lord, and I won't take that from you. But read how it says it.

:::margin
Psalm 27:14 (NIV)
"Wait for the LORD; be strong and take heart and wait for the LORD."
:::

Be strong and take heart. Those aren't lawn chair words. That's a man standing, doing everything that's his while he waits on the one thing that isn't. Abraham waited most of twenty-five years for the son God promised him, and in those years he moved his whole household to a country he'd never seen, built altars, armed his own servants to go get his nephew back, and stood eye to eye with kings. Waiting on God never once meant sitting down on the parts that were his.

So run this test. When you say you're waiting on God, look at your hands. If they're doing the next thing you already know is right, you're waiting on God. If they're folded until He makes it obvious, you're waiting on comfort and giving it His name.

:::margin
Luke 9:51 (NIV)
"As the time approached for him to be taken up to heaven, Jesus resolutely set out for Jerusalem."
:::

He knew what Jerusalem held. He didn't drift there. He set out.

## The same stair in every house

In a single man's apartment it's called when I'm married, as if a wedding flipped a switch. The man you'll be at the altar is the man you're building this year. In a house with a newborn it's called when things settle down, and they won't. Ten minutes at the counter after the baby's asleep, phones face down, is the whole season. For the man whose marriage ended, it's the call he'll make once the anger cools, and anger cools after a man acts like a man in spite of it, not before. For the man who's handed off the business or turned in his keys, it's sitting by the phone for somebody to need him. Nobody's dialing. The work is wherever he walks in and asks. A young man waits behind the words once I, and the sentence never finishes. A father in the middle of life waits for a quiet son to come around on his own. The step is different in every house. The stepping over is the same.

## The first screw

Name the thing, exactly. Not my marriage. The conversation about money I've owed her since spring. Not my son. Half an hour alone with him in the truck, radio off, and the question I'm scared to ask. A vague thing can't be done. A named one can.

Ask what you're actually waiting for, and be honest, because it's almost never information. It's a feeling. You're waiting to feel ready, or brave, or sure, and none of those come first. They come after.

Find the smallest move that's still a real move. Pulling up his number isn't one. The call is. Sitting with the drill across your knees isn't one. The first screw is.

Do it before noon. Afternoons are where a man's resolve goes to die, and by evening the couch has an argument.

And expect it to feel wrong. Your nerves have been tuned to waiting for years, and the first right move will register as a mistake for about a day. That feeling isn't a verdict. It's a scar stretching.

## What He sees

:::margin
James 4:17 (NIV)
"If anyone, then, knows the good they ought to do and doesn't do it, it is sin for them."
:::

That verse shuts the back door. Doing nothing isn't neutral ground. God writes it in the same book as the things we do. But turn it over. The Father who sees what's done in secret also sees a man come down the hall with a drill in his hand before the house is awake, because he's finished stepping over it. He sees the number dialed and the question asked. There's a reward for that, His reward, and I'll give it to you in **Live for a Reward That Lasts**. For now, fix the step. Then make the call.

## Before you turn the page

Nobody sat you down and taught you to wait. You picked it up the way you picked up your walk, from the men around you, without noticing. You can put it down the same way, one deliberate move at a time, and the first one is the hardest by a mile. May the God who came looking for Adam in the trees find you on your feet.
